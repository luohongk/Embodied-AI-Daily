HOG-WAM: Improving Visual Robustness through Future Gradient Prediction 2026-11-25
📝 Code: https://github.com/iPPLab/HOG-WAM
Show abstract

World-action models jointly predict future observations and actions, yet visual corruption can disrupt the resulting policies. We introduce HOG-WAM, which adds future Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG) as structural supervision derived directly from demonstration images. An auxiliary HOG diffusion transformer (DiT) module reads RGB features through asymmetric attention, allowing its objective to shape the visual representation used for control. The module is used only during training and removed at inference, preserving FastWAM's RGB-to-action architecture. We evaluate four controlled observation corruptions through a visual-corruption extension of RoboTwin, termed RoboTwin-Plus; HOG-WAM achieves the highest success among the compared policies under all four. Mixed-noise success increases from 78.86% to 82.86% relative to FastWAM, with comparable performance on clean scenes. On LIBERO Plus, overall success increases from 74.49% to 75.26%, with the largest gain under sensor noise, from 74.14% to 78.89%. Across four Franka tasks, average success increases from 40% to 59% under clean observations and from 17% to 24% under noisy observations. A representation ablation favors combining RGB observations with auxiliary HOG prediction over replacing RGB with HOG. These findings support future-gradient prediction as complementary supervision for visually robust control, with larger absolute gains on physical tasks than in simulation. Code is available at https://github.com/iPPLab/HOG-WAM.

Nav4Manip: From Navigation to Dexterous Mobile Manipulation through Co-Evolution 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Dexterous mobile manipulation is essential for robots to perform useful tasks in complex, unstructured environments. However, existing systems face two challenges. First, navigation remains too coarse for downstream manipulation, which often requires a precise base position and approach orientation rather than merely reaching a meter-scale goal region. Second, navigation and manipulation are commonly trained in isolation, lacking a feedback loop that enables the two capabilities to co-evolve. These limitations prevent current systems from reliably executing long-horizon dexterous mobile-manipulation tasks in real-world environments. To address them, we introduce Nav4Manip, a bidirectionally coupled co-evolution system for long-horizon dexterous mobile manipulation. It comprises two complementary architectures: Nav4Manip-N learns long-horizon navigation and refines its policy through manipulation feedback, while Nav4Manip-M trains a world-action model (WAM) for downstream dexterous manipulation. We train Nav4Manip-N in three stages: multi-task co-tuning learns navigation behavior and manipulation-aware affordance prediction; rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) rejects low-quality policy-generated paths and fine-tunes the model on retained trajectories; and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) uses counterfactual preferences to further improve navigation decisions. To support this framework, we establish the Nav-N-Manip benchmark and construct Nav4Manip-Pairs from benchmark rollouts, providing large-scale paired navigation--manipulation trajectories and affordance supervision. Extensive experiments on public vision-language navigation (VLN) benchmarks and Nav-N-Manip demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, while real-world evaluations show that Nav4Manip completes diverse dexterous mobile-manipulation tasks. Project website: https://nav4manip.github.io/.

Self-Distilling Future Video Representations for World Action Models 2026-11-25
📝 28 pages; Project page: https://huiwon-jang.github.io/FRED/
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) have emerged as a promising approach to learning generalist robot policies using rich spatiotemporal priors from pre-trained video diffusion models. A recent line of WAMs improves inference efficiency by conditioning a separate action decoder on intermediate future video representations extracted from a single denoising step, avoiding costly iterative denoising required for video generation. This design makes action prediction depend directly on the quality of these future representations. In this paper, we introduce Future REpresentation learning via self-Distillation with temporal context (FRED), a framework that trains WAMs to improve the quality of future video representations from noise in a single denoising step. Specifically, FRED uses self-distillation to predict future representations from noise, using representations extracted from ground-truth future observations as targets. We further condition the video model on historical observations, providing temporal context for predicting future representations better. Across diverse real-world manipulation scenarios, FRED consistently outperforms competing WAMs. It achieves particularly large gains on long-horizon and out-of-domain tasks under zero-shot environment generalization, and substantially improves human-to-robot skill transfer, reaching 50.0% task success on tasks absent from robot training data and demonstrated only in human videos, compared with 10.7% for the best competing WAM.

One Patch, Many Policies: Object-Associated Physical Patch Attacks on Robotic Manipulation 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World Action Models (WAMs) are vulnerable to adversarial visual interference, motivating the study of universal and transferable physical patch attacks. We propose Object-Associated Patch Attack (OAPA), a universal and transferable physical patch framework that enables an attacker to select which task object's manipulation to disrupt through patch placement, without re-optimizing the patch. Our framework combines object-associated placement, differentiable physical compositing, and final action-expert representation divergence. Physical compositing accounts for perspective and occlusion across camera views, while representation divergence guides patch optimization using either relative RMS or cosine distance. Experimental results demonstrate strong attack transferability across task suites and policies, together with the ability to selectively disrupt manipulation of an attacker-chosen task object through patch placement.

Entangle: Scaling Human-Video In-Context World-Action Models across Unseen Tasks and Contexts 2026-11-25
📝 26 pages, 13 figures, including the appendix. Project page at https://entangle-robotics.github.io/
Show abstract

In-context learning (ICL) enables foundation models to perform unseen tasks from context without parameter updates. In robotic manipulation, a human video provides the context that specifies how a task should be performed. However, scaling human-video ICL on world-action models (WAMs) faces two challenges: task-level human-robot pairs are not temporally aligned with robot actions, and standard action losses allow policies to rely on initial-scene or task-identity shortcuts rather than tracking the context. We present Entangle, an in-context world-action model that maps a single human video and current robot observations to future visual states and executable actions. To scale temporally aligned training data, we convert recorded robot videos into matched human contexts via video-to-video generation, supplemented by real paired recordings. For model training, we combine variable-length video windows with an intra-task contrastive objective that distinguishes the paired action trajectory from other executions of the same task, preventing task-identity shortcuts. Without task-specific fine-tuning, Entangle improves steadily as aligned training data scales, reaching an average score of 83.5/100 across six unseen tasks on an Astribot S1 robot, and achieves a 69.5% average success rate on seven unseen RoboTwin tasks, outperforming Zero-WAM by 22.6 percentage points. Under Context-OOD, although trained only on scene-aligned pairs, Entangle transfers context-specified behaviors across rearranged layouts and substituted tools. Project page at https://entangle-robotics.github.io/

Quantization for Action, Execution for Deployment: Joint Precision and Execution Selection for World Action Models 2026-11-25
Show abstract

World action models guide robots by predicting the effects of actions or generating commands from observations. A model may compare hundreds of candidate futures before acting, repeatedly reading weights that must fit in device memory. Post-training quantization reduces the number of bits used to store pretrained weights, but a smaller model is not necessarily a deployable one: temporary tensors can exceed graphics processing unit (GPU) memory limits, while even small prediction errors can change the selected action. This creates two coupled challenges: identifying bit assignments that preserve task performance and determining which of them meet real execution constraints, without exhausting costly task trials. We present PTQ4WAM, a framework for choosing layer-wise bit assignments and execution routes under memory, latency and task-evaluation budgets. Calibration measurements guide candidate proposals, optionally with a language model agent. We profile full-model routes on the GPU before spending task trials on feasible assignments. Fusing group rescaling and reading visual history in place can make an otherwise infeasible assignment deployable. Across 15 workloads from four model families, the median task score is 99.2% of the corresponding 32-bit floating-point (FP32) score. On fixed GPU replays, the median speedup over FP32 is 2.36 times and the median reduction in peak allocated memory is 71.5%. The code will be released soon.

EffectWAM: Learning Action-Grounded Effects for Robot Manipulation 2026-11-25
Show abstract

World-action models connect visual prediction with executable robot control. We present EffectWAM, which develops this connection through three complementary components: Effect, camera positional encoding (CaPE), and Action Grounding. Effect represents visual change as the difference between independently encoded future observations and a shared current-observation anchor. CaPE conditions endpoint pooling on relative camera pose when reliable extrinsics are available, while sharing the same learned parameters with pose-free pooling for uncalibrated trajectories. Action Grounding trains the visual representation to retain measured changes in end-effector pose and gripper state. An exponential moving average of the observation encoder supplies Effect targets. A shared generation expert learns to jointly predict Effect and an executable action chunk through flow matching, and reuses the sampled pair to generate future video on demand. This allows the controller to execute actions without waiting for video synthesis. We train on more than 20,000 hours of heterogeneous embodied data, excluding unused chain-of-thought data, and accommodate dataset-specific joint, end-effector, and hand-pose representations. Our evaluation uses the Small (8B) configuration on all five benchmarks and additionally evaluates the Large (26B) configuration on RoboDojo. Experiments examine closed-loop manipulation across complementary benchmarks, and comparisons across multiple baseline policies show consistent improvements from Action Grounding.

WAtcher: Watching Ahead for Safe Robotic Manipulation with Efficient World-Action Models 2026-11-25
📝 12 pages, 4 figures, 6 tables. Project page: https://zyshan0929.github.io/WAtcher
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) couple future imagination with action generation, but they execute every action chunk while ignoring the potential risk. To fill this gap, we present WAtcher, a safety-aware world-action framework in which the policy imagines the outcome of the candidate chunk before execution. For fast future preview, WAtcher builds on a compact WAM whose video expert imagines the visual outcome in only two denoising steps. In addition, we propose Safe-Guard, a lightweight module kept outside the WAM that scores each candidate chunk from its imagined outcome and triggers a bounded retreat when the risk is high. Safe-Guard is detachable and can be applied to other WAMs. To train Safe-Guard, we build an autonomous pipeline to collect hazard data from RoboTwin, LIBERO, and a real robot. Specifically, the pipeline perturbs expert demonstrations and labels each candidate chunk from the physical events recorded when it is executed, such as contacts and losses of support. Using the ~25,000 labeled windows from this pipeline, Safe-Guard reaches 92.1% balanced accuracy on a blind set, and cuts hazardous closed-loop trials on training tasks from 27.0% to 10.8% without lowering task success. On a held-out 300-question benchmark, it scores 94.2%, against 72.5% and 63.3% for zero-shot GPT-6-Astra and Claude Opus 5.5. WAtcher also reaches 90.4% and 82.4% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 and ManiSkill3, on par with or above leading WAMs, and 74.0% on five real-world tasks with Safe-Guard on, against 59.5% for $π_{0.5}$. Project page: https://zyshan0929.github.io/WAtcher

CoTacWAM: Joint Modeling of Vision and Touch for Robotic Manipulation 2026-11-25
📝 19 pages, including appendix
Show abstract

We present CoTacWAM, a world-action model that learns shared contact semantics between wrist vision and touch, achieving the best observed performance in our nominal manipulation and missing-input evaluations. The two modalities observe the same local interaction through different signals: wrist images reveal nearby geometry, while touch records physical contact. Our approach connects their common contact meaning while preserving the evidence unique to each modality, giving the policy a joint description of interaction across available input combinations. This representation supports predictive action learning alongside head-view scene context and conditions corrections after an independent detector confirms a failed attempt. Experiments across four real-world tasks demonstrate improved manipulation performance over the evaluated baselines, robustness to missing local observations, and further gains from autonomous retries.

G0.7: Scaling Up a Fast World Action Model with Long Context 2026-11-25
Show abstract

World action models (WAMs) adapt pretrained video generators for robot control by jointly predicting future camera frames and actions, transferring physical priors learned from video into manipulation policies. However, generating future frames before every action makes test-time inference too slow for closed-loop control, while most existing policies remain restricted to short observation histories. Building on Fast-WAM, which showed that video prediction shapes policy representations during training so that future frame synthesis can be skipped at deployment, we present G0.7, a generalist world action model scaled along two axes: extending the context window from a single observation to up to five minutes of history, and scaling pretraining to a large-scale corpus of robot teleoperation and human egocentric (ego) video. Across public simulation suites and real-robot deployments, direct action decoding (Fast mode) matches imagine-then-act (IDM) inference from the same checkpoint while running in 60 ms per 16-step action chunk on a single RTX 5090 (7.6x faster than IDM) and supporting pause-free real-time chunking. Performance scales consistently with pretraining data, with human ego video driving the largest gains in out-of-distribution generalization: G0.7 achieves a 70.8 mean score across five simulation benchmark suites, 48% and 54% zero-shot pick-and-place success on Galaxea R1 Pro and R1 Lite, 96.7% in-distribution and 93.3% out-of-distribution success on video-conditioned in-context learning tasks, and 86.9% mean task progress across seven real-world post-training tasks including multi-minute box folding, drink preparation, and product bagging. Sparse-WAM: Accelerating World Action Models via Action-Guided Sparse Imagination 2026-11-25
📝 Xinling Xie and Haodong Wang contributed equally to this work
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) leverage pretrained video models to improve generalization in robot control by jointly predicting future visual states and actions. This capability comes at a substantial inference cost, as dense future-frame tokens are repeatedly processed during denoising. Prior methods address this by token pruning that prioritizes visual fidelity to reduce denoising costs in video diffusion models. However, these methods do not use action relevance to determine which future-frame tokens to retain during joint denoising in WAMs. In this paper, we propose Sparse-WAM, a training-free framework for action-guided sparse imagination that selectively processes future-frame tokens to accelerate WAM inference. We observe substantial overlap in the spatial distribution of attention from action tokens to future-frame tokens (action-to-future attention) between consecutive denoising steps, despite continued updates to the future representations. Motivated by this, we develop Action-Guided Token Selection to retain frame-specific action-relevant regions together with cross-frame context. However, a naive implementation can incur attention-scoring and token-packing overhead that offsets the computational savings from pruning. We therefore introduce Pilot, an efficient engine that reduces sparse inference overhead through lightweight scoring and cross-step reuse of token selections. On LIBERO with FastWAM-Joint and RoboLab-120 with Cosmos 3 Edge, Sparse-WAM achieves inference speedups of approximately $2.0\times$ and $1.8\times$, respectively, over dense eager inference on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, while largely preserving task performance.

Targeted Adaptation of World-Action Models via Interaction and Motion Heads 2026-11-25
📝 Project page: https://headlight-wam.github.io/
Show abstract

World-Action Models (WAMs) are a promising new paradigm of robot manipulation policies that jointly generate future video and actions. Successful manipulation requires both identifying where to interact and modeling how the robot and objects move. Yet standard adaptation methods, such as full fine-tuning and LoRA, do not explicitly select parameters aligned with these manipulation-specific capabilities. Motivated by this challenge, we discover two functionally specialized families of attention heads: Next-Interaction Heads, which localize upcoming grasp-and-release targets, and Motion Heads, which track movement across frames. Furthermore, we introduce HeadLight, a parameter-efficient refinement method that jointly adapts Next-Interaction Heads and Motion Heads. On RoboTwin tasks unseen during fine-tuning, HeadLight achieves 62.28% success, compared with 57.89% for full fine-tuning and 58.66% for LoRA, while updating only 1.1% of parameters. Inference-time ablations further validate the functional importance of these heads: ablating Motion Heads reduces manipulation success from 57.89% to 0.94%, while ablating Next-Interaction Heads lowers it to 40.61%, both substantially more than ablating equally many random heads. Finally, we show that our approach is effective across multiple WAM architectures and real-robot manipulation settings.

Event-WAM: Learning to Act with Event-Derived Futures 2026-11-25
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) leverage future visual modeling for robotic action learning, with recent studies exploring alternative or complementary objectives beyond conventional RGB prediction. We introduce Event-WAM, which incorporates event-derived visual changes as auxiliary future supervision alongside RGB. Event-WAM constructs temporally structured event representations from RGB demonstrations and encodes them using a frozen pretrained RGB video VAE, without training an event-specific VAE. Joint RGB--Event latent flow matching provides appearance- and change-oriented supervision without requiring event observations or explicit future generation at inference. Across simulated benchmarks, Event-WAM maintains comparable performance under standard conditions, while robustness gains under perturbations are benchmark-dependent, including improvements on LIBERO-Plus. Real-world evaluations further demonstrate improved manipulation success under both standard and unseen perturbation conditions. Dynamic Forcing: Learning Action-Grounded Sparse Dynamic Representations with Dense Future Guidance for World Action Models 2026-11-25
Show abstract

World action models (WAMs) support robot control through visual prediction, but dense future modeling repeatedly encodes static context already available in the current observation, while action-relevant changes are often spatially sparse. We study sparse dynamic representation learning, retaining the current observation as a contextual anchor and using a small set of compact dynamic tokens to represent anticipated changes. We develop CompactWAM, a sparse-dense joint predictive framework that enables sparse representation learning within a video-supervised shared backbone, without requiring a reconstruction objective for the sparse pathway. Since a limited token budget alone does not specify which information to retain, we introduce Dynamic Forcing, which pairs direct action supervision with privileged dense future guidance to promote action relevance and dynamic fidelity. While action prediction gradients directly drive compact tokens to capture control-relevant cues, dense future guidance further shapes them via action functional guidance that matches sparse and dense contributions to the action expert, and dynamics-aware distribution matching, which aligns temporal relational changes relative to a static reference. At inference, a single visual forward pass encodes current observations and compact dynamic tokens for action generation, eliminating dense future modeling. CompactWAM achieves 98.4\% success on LIBERO and 77.9\% on LIBERO-Plus, retaining respective success rates of 98.3\% and 76.6\% with only 9.2\% of the dense future token budget. Evaluations on RoboTwin~2.0 and real-world manipulation further demonstrate the effectiveness of the learned sparse predictive representations.

DriftWAM: Faster World-Action Models via Action-Aware Video Drifting 2026-11-25
📝 16 pages, 5 figures
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) unify future video prediction and action generation for robotic control, but their reliance on iterative denoising incurs substantial inference costs. Distilling these models into few-step generators offers a promising route to efficient deployment, yet maintaining task performance under aggressive step reduction remains challenging. Existing methods rely on point-wise trajectory consistency distillation, which pursues high visual fidelity while leaving downstream action information unexploited in optimizing video generation, and also lacks explicit distribution-level alignment. To address this, we propose DriftWAM, a few-step WAM distillation framework that incorporates the actions induced by predicted videos into video learning. Specifically, DriftWAM integrates action-aware video drifting, which performs video distribution matching through a joint visual--action similarity kernel to steer sample evolution under the guidance of these induced actions, with action-guided video regularization, which leverages the compatible teacher--student matches identified by the joint kernel to backpropagate action-level gradients directly into the video generator. Evaluations on the RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO benchmarks, as well as in real-world robotic manipulation tasks, show that DriftWAM consistently outperforms prior consistency distillation methods across few-step settings at the same inference speed.

$\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM: Efficient Hierarchical Memory Across Modalities and Timescales for World-Action Models 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Long-horizon robotic manipulation requires historical context, yet retaining extensive visual histories incurs growing storage and retrieval costs, while aggressive compression risks losing fine-grained information needed for subsequent decisions. Moreover, executed action histories capture intermediate behaviors that sparse visual observations may miss. Preserving this complementary context efficiently calls for modality-specific memory mechanisms that balance recent detail with long-term retention. To this end, we introduce $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM, a world-action model equipped with hierarchical visual and interaction memories. $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM preserves recent visual observations in dense short-term memory while consolidating entries evicted from this window into fixed-size associative long-term memory through gated delta updates. Within the same short- and long-term hierarchy, interaction memory encodes executed action prefixes into compact temporal representations, complementing visual observations with behavioral context. These complementary memories are integrated into world-action modeling through layer-wise retrieval, enabling memory-augmented action generation with a bounded online memory footprint independent of episode length. On the memory-dependent simulation benchmarks MIKASA-Robo and RMBench, $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM achieves +53.2% and +61.2% absolute improvements in average success rates over Fast-WAM, respectively. These gains extend to real-world manipulation, where $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM achieves a +29.3% absolute improvement over LingBot-VA in average success across three tasks. These results suggest that $\mathrm{M}^{2}$-WAM offers a promising approach to efficient memory-augmented world-action modeling.

SplatWAM: Splatting World Action Models for Real-Time 3D Imagination 2026-11-24
📝 21 pages, 15 figures, 13 tables
Show abstract

World models typically predict future observations in pixel or latent space, limiting their ability to directly represent true 3D scene evolution. We present SplatWAM (Splatting World Action Model), a generative world model that learns action-conditioned dynamics directly over compact 3D Gaussian primitives. A tokenizer SplatWAM-T first encodes a variable number of unposed views into a fixed budget of Gaussian tokens. A dynamics model SplatWAM-D then predicts future Gaussian states in a single forward pass per action, using log-Euclidean covariance coordinates and differentiable rendering supervision to facilitate learning in an explicit 3D space. Each predicted state can be rasterized from arbitrary viewpoints, enforcing a shared 3D representation across views by construction. On OGBench Scene, SplatWAM renders novel views 12x faster than video world models (~80 FPS on an RTX 5090) with up to 2.0 dB higher PSNR. We further introduce SplatWAM-P, a lightweight flow-matching policy head jointly trained with the dynamics model. Across 8 task suites, model-free Gaussian-token policies outperform pixel-based counterparts by up to 24.8 percentage points. SplatWAM-P improves over Latent Particle World Model (LPWM) by 12 percentage points and matches HIQL without a learned value function. On LIBERO, our ~1B model outperforms WorldVLA (~7B) and the robot-pretrained OpenVLA (~7B), while matching OpenVLA-OFT when both are trained without large-scale robot pretraining. Code and checkpoints will be released.

iWAM: Efficient Action Prediction from World Models without Explicit Future Prediction 2026-11-24
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) build policies on pretrained video-generation models, on the premise that predicting how a scene evolves helps determine how to act. However, generating future scenes is expensive for real-time control. This motivates efficient proxies for future imagination, but how to train such proxies without sacrificing action-prediction quality remains unclear. An effective proxy need not generate a high-resolution frame; it only needs to provide enough information to distinguish between possible futures. This leads to the design principle behind iWAM: rather than denoising an entire future frame, can we condition the action policy on intermediate features from a single video-model evaluation? We show that the answer is yes. Exploiting the reversible dynamics of flow-matching models, we invert each training future into the corresponding latent noise vector. However, a single velocity evaluation at this latent does not necessarily yield informative features for action prediction. We therefore learn a bidirectional flow map that amortizes future-generation computation, enabling a single model evaluation to provide effective conditioning features. At inference, we sample a latent noise vector from the prior and condition the policy using just one video-backbone forward pass. Across simulated benchmarks and real robots, iWAM recovers the generalization benefits of WAMs while substantially reducing its inference cost.

RoboAda: Adapting Frozen Robot Policies at Test Time to Physics They Cannot See 2026-11-24
📝 This version was posted before the coauthors completed their review of the paper
Show abstract

Pretrained robot policies decide what to do from camera images, yet some of the physics that decides whether their plans succeed is not visible. An object may look light but be 100 times heavier, and a weakened joint or a lagging controller leaves no trace in the image. When the physics at deployment differs from training, the plan is often still right but is executed as if nothing had changed. Training on such variations in advance cannot handle unseen changes, while adapting once deployed either changes the plan, leaving the miscalibrated commands in place, or updates a robot model by gradient steps that do not know how reliable they are and can make the robot worse even when nothing has changed. We propose RoboAda, which keeps the policy and its plan unchanged and corrects the commands that carry it out by inferring at test time how the robot turns commands into motion. The key observation is that the robot's own motion reveals the change: after each action chunk, an inverse-dynamics model of the original robot converts the observed motion into the commands it would have needed, yielding free labels at every step. Competing hypotheses of how the robot responds, including that nothing has changed, are weighted by how well they predict these labels, and the next chunk is sent with the commands that best realize the plan under them. RoboAda needs no reward, no gradient step and no data from the changed robot. We evaluate RoboAda with five frozen policies on LIBERO, two world action models and three vision-language-action models. Under eleven physical shifts, it more than doubles their average success, from 27.8% to 62.9%. It beats the best of seven test-time adaptation methods by 17.3 points on Cosmos Policy, and largely preserves the policies' success when nothing has changed. On bimanual RoboTwin 2.0 in joint space, it raises success under joint-target offsets from 24.9% to 78.8%.

RoboThink: Let the Agent Think, Let the Policy Act 2026-11-24
📝 This version was posted before the coauthors completed their review of the paper
Show abstract

Robot policies act in real time but fail without knowing why, whereas a reasoning agent that watches the robot can often tell what went wrong. Existing systems either hand the agent control, which takes minutes per episode, or let it send instructions. Yet a policy follows a new instruction only from a fresh start, since a half-finished task leaves it in states outside its training data. Nor can an instruction fix how the robot moves. If a drift keeps pulling the hand to the left, "push right, slightly" is not a task the policy has learned. We propose RoboThink, in which the agent thinks and the policy learns to act on the agent's guidance. An instruction channel tells the policy what to do. When the user says "the milk, not the butter" after the robot has started, the agent rewrites the instruction and the robot undoes its own motion, with no training. A feedback channel tells it how to move. It is taught from the agent alone with agent-labelled DAgger, role tokens and counterfactual feedback, and is read only by the policy's action module. On LIBERO, RoboThink with Claude Opus 5.5 raises success from 0.09 to 0.87 under an unknown constant drift and from 0.42 to 0.84 under a random one. It also completes misheard and changed requests in 0.91 to 1.00 of the episodes. Acting alone, GPT-6 Astra needs 2.1 min per success and over a million tokens per episode, whereas RoboThink needs 55 s and 8k tokens. The same recipe works across policies, for both vision-language-action models and world action models, and across robots, from one arm to two arms and a mobile manipulator. On all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks with two arms, success rises from 0.12 to 0.74 under the constant drift and from 0.31 to 0.56 under the random one. Finally, the agent is a plug-in. A policy taught by one agent works with other agents, including open-weight ones, and grows stronger with a stronger agent, without retraining.

Embodied AI Fails the Mirror Test: How a Simple Mirror Breaks Robot Policies 2026-11-24
📝 Code: https://github.com/iPPLab/robodojo-mirror
Show abstract

Can a simple mirror expose the limitations of today's robotic manipulation policies? Inspired by the mirror self-recognition test in animal cognition, we investigate whether robot policies can reliably manipulate physical objects in scenes containing their reflections. We introduce RoboDojo-Mirror, a benchmark built on RoboDojo that preserves task objectives, success criteria, and policy checkpoints while comparing a mirror-free baseline with single-mirror, dual-mirror, and mirrored-tabletop conditions. Across five policies spanning Vision-Language-Action models, World Action Models, and agentic policies, every mirror condition reduces aggregate success rates: averaged over the three conditions, the drop ranges from 10.8 to 56.7 percentage points across policies, and it reaches 62.5 points on the mirrored tabletop.
