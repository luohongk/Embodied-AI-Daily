Making Future-Predictive Representations Actionable 2026-11-17
📝 15 pages, 6 main figures, including supplementary material
Show abstract

Recent world action models (WAMs) drop future prediction at inference, assuming that representations learned through auxiliary future supervision still benefit action generation. However, our layerwise analysis of Fast-WAM reveals a depth-dependent mismatch: while probes decode future state transitions more accurately from deeper visual layers, the gradients and visual focuses of the future and action objectives increasingly diverge with depth, leaving much of this predictive information unused by the action pathway. To address this issue, we introduce RelayWAM, which adds an explicit transition space between the video and action backbones. Conditioned on the task instruction, robot state, and current action estimate, recurrent transition layers extract step-relevant predictive cues across depth and relay them directly into the action attention, without generating future frames at inference. Experiments show that RelayWAM substantially amplifies the benefit of future supervision, translating into stronger generalization under distribution shifts in both simulation and real-world robot tasks. On LIBERO-Plus, RelayWAM achieves 72.03% mean success, compared to 56.42% without future supervision and 51.50% for Fast-WAM.

Future Prediction as Training-Only Video Supervision for Pretrained VLA Policies 2026-11-17
Show abstract

World action models (WAMs) introduce predictive supervision into robot policy learning by jointly modeling future visual observations and actions. Pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policies acquire rich visual and semantic representations as well as action-generation capabilities. Their action objectives typically lack direct supervision for predicting future visual observations. Future videos in robot demonstrations record object motion and task progress. They can provide temporal visual supervision that complements action labels. We propose Training-Only Video Supervision, which introduces future-video prediction as an auxiliary objective for VLA fine-tuning. We condition a randomly initialized video-prediction expert on the VLA's shared vision-language features. The expert learns to predict future video latents from robot demonstrations. The video prediction loss and the original action loss jointly update the shared vision-language backbone, providing additional temporal visual supervision. After training, we remove the video expert and its adapter. The fine-tuned VLA retains its original action-generation pathway at inference. On the 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, our method achieves 71.18% average success, compared with $π_{0.5}$ of 58.33%. In a matched evaluation on four real-world manipulation tasks, success increases from 42.50% to 61.25%. These results show that future visual modeling can serve as auxiliary training supervision for pretrained VLAs. The fine-tuned action policy benefits from this supervision without retaining a video-prediction branch at deployment.

Fly as Instructed: Refining World-Action Policies for UAV Vision-Language Navigation 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Natural language-guided UAV navigation enables intuitive human-robot interaction and flexible autonomous flight in complex environments. However, reliable navigation requires accurate instruction following, continuous task progress tracking, and safe obstacle avoidance, which remain challenging for existing world-action models that primarily focus on action prediction. To address these limitations, we propose a world-action learning framework that integrates three complementary mechanisms: a language-conditioned action interface with targeted post-training for instruction-responsive motion, recurrent instruction-consumption prediction to track task progress and determine termination, and Gaussian-scene post-training to enhance obstacle avoidance through trajectory imitation and differentiable geometric feedback. Closed-loop evaluations and ablation studies demonstrate improvements in sequential task completion, safe goal reaching, and timely termination. Furthermore, direct one-step denoising reduces inference overhead, and real-world indoor and outdoor flights validate the framework's effectiveness.

ThinkWAM: Rethinking Latent Futures for Action Refinement 2026-11-17
📝 21 pages, 12 figures, 15 tables, including references and appendix. Project page: https://thinkwam-project.github.io/
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) couple action generation with predictions of future states. An initial action chunk may be flawed. A future prediction that is not conditioned on the chunk is identical for every proposal and thus carries no proposal-specific signal, limiting its usefulness for refinement before execution. We introduce ThinkWAM, a latent WAM that establishes bidirectional feedback before execution through a proposal-rethink-refine loop: the policy first proposes a complete action chunk, the proposal corrects multi-horizon future latents through a learned residual, and the corrected latents are injected into a second pass of the same action head, which generates the executable chunk. An affordance-guided observation-action memory and a near-fine, far-coarse multi-horizon prediction scheme, both motivated by predictive coding, support this loop, and all prediction stays in latent space. ThinkWAM achieves 98.70% success on LIBERO, 84.76% on LIBERO-Plus without OOD fine-tuning, 85.75%/65.85% on RoboTwin 2.0 Clean/Random, and 65.17% on RoboCasa24. ThinkWAM achieves 64.4% mean success across three real-world OOD conditions. The mean two-pass inference latency per action chunk is 153.48 ms on LIBERO, 164.61 ms on RoboTwin, and 101.2 ms on the Galaxea A1Z. Ablations show complementary gains from rethinking and affordance guidance. Diagnostics indicate that proposal-conditioned corrections change both predicted futures and action trajectories, showing that the corrected future representations improve the final action.

Multimodal foundation models learned to see and to speak by consuming the whole internet. Embodied agents admit no such shortcut, since they require data that couple observations with physical states and actions. These signals can be provided, to varying degrees, by multiple data sources. In this work, we organize the embodied data ecosystem as a "pyramid'' spanning five complementary sources: real-robot data, UMI-style data, egocentric and exocentric data, simulation data, and general vision-language data. We organize the pyramid around the tension between scalability and robot alignment, and further characterize each source in terms of data quality, diversity, reusability, and physical fidelity. We then analyze recent embodied foundation models through the lens of data recipes, examining how different sources are selected, aligned, and mixed during pretraining. For embodied brain models, vision-language-action models, and world-action models alike, we relate data composition to capabilities in perception, reasoning, planning, action generation, and world prediction. To facilitate community use, we additionally consolidate over 10,000 hours of trajectories from widely used robot datasets into a unified LeRobot format, with transparent frame-level quality annotations for filtering. We close by discussing six open challenges: building large-scale tactile datasets, collecting failure and recovery data, developing scalable data-collection pipelines, aligning actions across embodiments, leveraging egocentric data for dexterous manipulation, and designing principled data recipes for robot learning. We hope this work lays the foundation for the design of next-generation embodied systems.

Distilling World Action Models for Efficient Bimanual Manipulation via Anchored Path Matching 2026-11-17
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) jointly generate future video and robot actions through iterative denoising. Few-step distillation reduces the number of model evaluations, but its coarse updates can move the student away from the teacher's trajectory. We propose Anchored Path Matching (APM), which supervises endpoint and short-transition predictions from states reached by the student. From each state, we continue the teacher to zero noise and use its endpoint and first intermediate state as targets. Modality-routed adapters learn to match both targets from the same starting state. When the two gradients conflict, anchor-priority updates preserve the endpoint gradient and remove the opposing component of the local gradient within each modality. One checkpoint supports 1-, 2-, and 4-step joint generation. The sampling budget can be chosen at deployment without retraining or teacher queries. On RoboTwin 2.0, we train only on Clean demonstrations and evaluate directly on the domain-randomized Random setting. At one step, APM improves closed-loop success over continued base distillation at matched inference budgets by 2.0 percentage points on Clean and 1.4 on Random. Video peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) rises from 30.169 to 32.317 dB, with gains in structural similarity (SSIM) and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS). On two real-robot tasks, APM improves success over the one-step teacher and reduces control-cycle latency by $21.3\times$ relative to the eight-step teacher.Project page: https://jialonglong.github.io/Anchored-Path-Matching/.

Plausible Videos Can Lead to Poor Decisions: Diagnosing and CURE-ing World Models for Planning 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Action-conditioned world models are increasingly used as world models for planning, yet a world model that makes plausible, realistic-looking videos that are consistent with the training data can produce poor decisions. We address two key questions: (1) when can we trust a world model for planning, and (2) what should we do when we cannot? We introduce CURE (Certify, Upweight, Rollout, Explore), a certificate-guided technique that assesses whether a world model can be trusted for planning and determines what to do next: whether to upweight the training data to debias the model; roll out more samples at test time; or explore by collecting new data where support (how well the training data cover the planner's decisions) is missing. We deploy CURE across multiple domains, spanning simulated physics based on PhyWorld, MuJoCo arm control, and Language-Table pushing in simulation and on real videos. The decisions our certificate trusts have lower regret than those it does not (e.g., 0.06 vs. 0.38 on Language-Table sim and 0.07 vs. 0.38 on MuJoCo contact), whereas video perceptual quality does not identify good decisions. By focusing on decision quality rather than perceptual quality, CURE lowers regret over the best pessimistic decision rule by 10-25% across support levels, and over world-action models, search and policy learning by 41-64%, given the same data.

How much does hand motion tell us about what a first-person camera will see next? AR-WAM: efficient streaming world-action modeling for autonomous driving 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Autonomous driving requires anticipating how traffic will evolve while responding quickly to new observations. Video foundation models offer the first ability, but repeated history encoding and iterative video generation make them costly to use for driving. We present AR-WAM, an efficient streaming world-action model that combines autoregressive temporal modeling with single-pass future conditioning. Because its video backbone is causal, AR-WAM updates its visual context incrementally, reusing past computation and conditioning future prediction on observed motion. We further hypothesize that future features computed from recent observations can improve generalization in dynamic traffic without iterative video generation. AR-WAM therefore obtains these features in one forward pass and uses them to condition trajectory generation, and reinforcement learning then improves the policy through direct optimization of driving rewards. With a single front camera, AR-WAM achieves 91.5 PDMS after supervised training and 94.0 after reinforcement learning on NAVSIM-v1, outperforming the compared strong end-to-end world-action models. In a dynamic setting of continuous driving, supervised AR-WAM keeps its visual history across decisions and outperforms strong world-action baselines.

UNIVERSE: Unified Video Action Models for Autonomous Driving with Flexible Mask-Modulated Modality Generation 2026-11-16
📝 Accepted by NeurIPS 2026. Code: https://github.com/xiaomi-mlab/UNIVERSE

Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) can improve action generalization in autonomous driving by learning scene dynamics through dense future-video supervision. However, deploying such models often requires costly future-video generation, while architectures that bypass video rollout typically separate video and action parameters or alter their learned dependencies at inference. We introduce UNIVERSE, a unified video-action model built on a single mask-modulated Diffusion Transformer (DiT) that combines shared-parameter learning with modality-independent inference. Its Modality-Decoupling Visibility Mask allows future video and trajectory tokens to attend to a common historical context while blocking mutual attention between the two future modalities. As a result, future-video supervision directly updates the parameters used for trajectory denoising, yet video tokens can be omitted at test time without changing the trajectory branch's training-time attention dependencies. The same model supports trajectory-only, video-only, and joint video-action generation. Trajectory-only inference achieves a 4.3x speedup over coupled video-action rollout with comparable planning accuracy. UNIVERSE achieves 91.0 PDMS on NAVSIM, compared with 89.6 for a Two-DiT variant, and demonstrates strong zero-shot transfer to nuScenes and Bench2Drive without target-domain fine-tuning. Controlled ablations further support the complementary benefits of shared parameters, dense video co-training, and modality-decoupled visibility.

LEON: Operator-Structured Latent Evolution for World Modeling 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Modeling action-conditioned physical evolution is fundamental to latent world models. World Action Models (WAMs) couple such models with policy learning through predictive supervision or future-conditioned action generation. Yet their latent transitions typically rely on transformer-based predictors, leaving dynamical structure to be learned implicitly from data. We derive a latent-transition formulation from controlled mechanical evolution. A common finite-dimensional projection of observable dynamics yields a shared operator family affine in physical control and an explicit projection residual. Guided by this principle, we introduce the Latent Evolution Operator Network (LEON), which adapts pretrained visual features into learned observables and evolves them through an affine family of shared operator matrices and complementary additive updates. Coefficients inferred from evolving observables and action-related context modulate the operators. We instantiate LEON in two WAM architectures while preserving their distinct original prediction targets and policy interfaces. Across single-arm and bimanual manipulation, LEON improves average policy success over corresponding WAM baselines, including under diverse perturbations. Ablations support its observable adaptation and operator-structured evolution, while controlled-system evaluations demonstrate improved extrapolation beyond training conditions.

OA-WAM: Object-Addressable World Action Model for Robust Robot Manipulation 2026-11-15
Show abstract

World action models (WAMs) learn manipulation by predicting actions and future observations, yet their success drops sharply when the camera viewpoint, object layout or robot initial state changes. Such changes keep the instructed object the same but alter the appearance and pose features the policy observes, which in standard attention also form the keys that locate the object. We introduce OA-WAM, an object-addressable WAM that finds each object by an identity address fixed for the episode and reads its changing state. In every layer of a pretrained multimodal trunk, object-slot keys depend only on the address and frame position, and values carry the current state; the same addresses key the action pointer and index per-object forecasts, which can trigger re-grounding. Fine-tuned on standard LIBERO demonstrations, OA-WAM transfers zero-shot to LIBERO-Plus with 90.5% success, outperforming all ten published baselines. It also leads on LIBERO (99.0%) and, fine-tuned on BridgeData V2, on SimplerEnv WidowX (88.9%). On five real-robot tasks, it succeeds in 89 of 100 nominal trials, versus 73 for the strongest baseline. Ablations trace its robustness to addressing rather than to object slots: with standard attention, slots do no better than a slot-free policy, and addressing them by identity raises geometric success by 10.7 points. Forecasting the addressed objects is likewise a stronger training signal than forecasting image codes.

SALA: Semantically Aligned Latent Actions Enable Dual-Axis Scaling of Embodied Foundation Models 2026-11-15
Show abstract

Embodied foundation models hinge on diverse robot interaction data, which are costly to collect. Egocentric human videos offer a complementary source of experience, and latent action models (LAMs) provide a popular means of leveraging these videos for policy supervision. Compared with world-action models (WAMs), LAMs can offer greater training efficiency and potentially lower inference costs by avoiding explicit pixel prediction during policy learning and execution. However, most existing LAMs learn latent actions solely through video reconstruction, which can cause their representations to cluster by visual appearance rather than by the underlying actions. As a result, they struggle to align human actions with their robot counterparts. To bridge the gap, we introduce SALA, which emphasizes establishing the semantic correspondence for latent actions. Without requiring paired data, we use the language descriptions of unpaired human and robot clips to estimate semantic similarities and supervise cross-embodiment correspondence. Specifically, we translate these similarities into soft neighborhood structures that guide human latent actions toward semantically related robot latents, while robot action reconstruction grounds the latent space in control. The resulting shared latent action space provides a unified supervision signal for training an embodied foundation model on both human and robot videos. Notably, latent action supervision enables scaling along two axes: (1) pre-training on increasing amounts of general human video consistently reduces robot action prediction error on downstream tasks, and (2) post-training on increasing amounts of task-specific human video substantially improves real-world robot task success rates.

ReactBench-AD: Do Driving VLAs and World-Action Models React to Imminent Hazards in Their Path? 2026-11-15
Show abstract

Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) and World-Action Models (WAMs) have emerged in autonomous driving with the promise of strong generalization to long-tail scenarios. Although these models achieve state-of-the-art results on autonomous driving benchmarks, most existing benchmarks are dominated by nominal scenarios and therefore cannot test this promise. To test one such long-tail capability that is critical for safety, we introduce ReactBench-AD, a benchmark that measures how driving models react to objects that appear in the ego vehicle's path. Built on real NAVSIM and PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles scenes, ReactBench-AD composites a crossing pedestrian, a standing pedestrian, or a stationary car consistently across all camera views and history frames. Each edited scene thus forms an exact, pixel-level counterfactual pair with its original, which allows us to isolate the causal effect of the inserted object on the predicted trajectory. Evaluating four VLAs and two WAMs, we find that they react weakly, often barely at all. Finally, we test how easily one of the evaluated VLAs, ReCogDrive, can be taught to brake hard for imminent hazards: a short fine-tuning stage on edited scenes substantially improves its reactions, with the learned behavior generalizing to unseen objects and fully real scenes, at minimal cost to nominal driving. These results call into question the out-of-the-box long-tail capabilities of these models, but suggest that they can be a strong foundation for acquiring them with little additional training, highlighting the value of real or synthetic data that demonstrates the long-tail behaviors nominal datasets rarely contain. Code and data will be made publicly available.

DiffWAM: Learning World Action Model from Consecutive Visual Difference 2026-11-15
Show abstract

World Action Models (WAMs) have emerged as an important paradigm in robot learning, jointly predicting future scene states and robot actions from current observations and instructions. However, future states entangle task-related changes with large amounts of task-irrelevant content, such as static backgrounds, which weakens the model's ability to capture changes relevant to task execution. To address this, we propose Difference World Action Model (DiffWAM), a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework that enhances world representations by jointly learning future-state prediction and consecutive visual difference prediction. We define consecutive visual differences as differences between the latent representations of consecutive observations, providing an explicit learning signal for temporal variation. The framework comprises a World Expert for future frame prediction, an Action Expert for action generation, and a Diff Expert for predicting these latent differences along the trajectory. A structured unidirectional attention mask conditions the Diff, World, and Action Experts solely on the current-frame representation while blocking attention between experts. This design allows both the World and Diff Experts to be removed during inference, introducing no additional inference overhead. DiffWAM achieves state-of-the-art performance on the LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 benchmarks and substantially improves over FastWAM on real-world manipulation tasks. Specifically, it attains average success rates of 98.8%, 92.65%, and 88.3%, outperforming the state-of-the-art baseline (i.e., FastWAM) by 1.75, 1.55, and 15.0 percentage points, respectively.

Cache or Denoise Less? Residual Reuse in a Flow-Matching World Action Model 2026-11-15
📝 Honorable Mention at the CoRL 2026 Workshop on Efficient Foundation Models for Real-Time Embodied AI (EEAI). Code and data available at https://github.com/gabhinav001/cache-or-denoise-less
Show abstract

Flow-matching robot policies generate each action chunk through repeated network evaluations, one per denoising step. Cross-cycle caches such as C$^3$ache reuse computation from earlier replanning calls, but whether they beat simply taking fewer steps is unclear. We compare the two in Fast-WAM, a 6B-parameter world action model, on LIBERO-Goal. Two uncached steps keep the success of ten: on all 500 initial states they are non-inferior within two percentage points, for one checkpoint and noise seed. Under PyTorch's default compile mode, they also cut a replanning call on an RTX A6000 from 202 to 92 ms. This leaves little room for caching. A cache that keeps two fresh steps cannot be faster than two uncached steps: every call still encodes the images, runs the video expert and computes those two steps. Caching the first of two steps, refreshed every fourth call, saves at most 11--14% of a call by our cost model. Cached policies are also fragile. Their losses tracked the share of the flow path the cached steps cover: under Fast-WAM's current time shift, C$^3$ache's eight cached steps cover 80% of the path instead of 44%, and the cached policy succeeds on 63% of states against 95% uncached. Resampling the noise on every call cost caches covering 60% or more of the path 14--40 points. These results argue for fewer-step baselines and for testing reuse under the deployed sampler and noise settings. We release our evaluation harness, logs and a checklist.

ResWAM: Learning What Changes, Not What Stays, for World-Action Models 2026-11-14
Show abstract

World-action models (WAMs) augment vision-language-action policies (VLAs) with future prediction so that the policy can anticipate how its actions will affect the physical world. Existing WAMs supervise complete future observations. However, observations typically change little over an action chunk, so the future loss is dominated by copying static background content rather than learning task-relevant robot and object motion, limiting policy learning. Moreover, existing WAMs rely on large video generation models or separate world models, incurring heavy training and inference compute that hinders closed-loop deployment. To improve policy performance, we propose visual residual learning, supervising only the feature change from the current frame to the future frame in a frozen DINOv3 space. Compared with complete future features, this residual learning target yields a higher feature rank with richer and more diverse representations of scene changes, and spatially concentrates on the robot arm and manipulated objects rather than the background. To further improve computational efficiency, we present ResWAM, a residual world-action model with 2.3B parameters in total, including a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT that jointly trains an action branch and a residual branch via conditional flow matching. Without robot pretraining (i.e., pretraining on external robot action trajectories beyond the benchmark demonstrations), ResWAM achieves top average success rates on LIBERO-Plus (81.1%), RoboTwin 2.0 (93.8%), and RoboDojo (9.40% success, 13.41 score) at the lowest training (2.7-4.0 TFLOPs) and inference compute (1.0-1.4 TFLOPs), and leads real-robot Open, Put, and Stack (80.0% average, +10.0% over $π_{0.5}$) at 92.7 ms latency.

WA-JEPA: Goal-Aware Predictive Feedback Action Refinement for a World-Action Model 2026-11-14
📝 16 pages, 5 figures, including supplementary material. Code available at https://github.com/Dovisya/WA-JEPA
Show abstract

JEPA-based world models predict action consequences in latent space, but representative planning approaches rely on candidate-action search, requiring repeated evaluation of action sequences and incurring substantial online computational cost. Direct action generation avoids this search, yet how to leverage latent predictions to guide action generation given task goals remains a key challenge in translating learned physical dynamics into effective decisions. We introduce WA-JEPA, a world-action model that couples action generation, action-conditioned latent prediction, and goal-aware feedback. The model first generates an action proposal and predicts the resulting future scenes and proprioceptive states, then refines the proposal through two complementary feedback paths, using the task goal as a reference. Goal-discrepancy residual feedback (GDRF) uses differences between the latent representations of predicted scenes and the task goal to guide chunk-wise action refinement. Future-scene context feedback (FSCF) preserves fine-grained predicted scene representations to guide subsequent action refinement. Jointly learned from offline demonstrations, this process iteratively updates the same plan before execution, making future predictions directly participate in action generation without candidate-action search. We evaluate short-horizon goal reaching, long-horizon tasks, and full-task execution. The results show that goal-aware predictive feedback supports competitive task performance while maintaining a compact model and low online computational cost. Vision-Language-Action Models: A Life-Cycle Survey of Design, Training and Deployment 2026-11-13
📝 43 pages, 9 figures, 11 tables
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models adapt web-pretrained vision-language models to output robot actions, so that web knowledge carries over to physical control. In under 4 years the field has moved from RT-2, the first VLA, to open models that fine-tune on a single consumer GPU and to proprietary generalists that clean unseen homes. This survey reviews 88 works from December 2022 to October 2026, mainly on manipulation and navigation, plus humanoid control, world-action models and evaluation. We follow a VLA through its life cycle, from model design to training and deployment, along seven axes. Model design covers (a) inputs and memory, what the model observes; (b) architecture, how its pipeline is built; (c) action output, how it produces motor commands; and (d) intermediate predictions, what else it predicts, from reasoning steps to future observations. Training covers (e) pretraining data, the mix of robot, web, human and simulated data, and (f) post-training, adaptation to the target robot. Deployment covers (g) real-time execution, keeping pace with the robot. We analyse 22 models on these axes and report their training data, model size, inference speed and hardware. Three trends emerge: (i) training data has broadened, from about 10,000 hours of teleoperated robot data ($π_0$) to 23,000 community-collected episodes (SmolVLA) and 20,000 hours of egocentric human video (GR00T N1.7); (ii) model sizes have split: most recent models have 3-7 billion parameters, while a new group under one billion runs on consumer GPUs; and (iii) generalist VLAs are emerging that handle both manipulation and navigation, such as UniVLA and Qwen-VLA. We also compare VLAs with world-action models, which predict future observations as well as actions. We review evidence that some VLAs with near-perfect benchmark scores fail when objects or the camera move slightly, and open up further research questions.

Joint World Action Models through Differentiable Trajectory Optimization 2026-11-13
📝 29 pages, 6 figures, 14 tables
Show abstract

Joint world action models (WAMs) generate future actions and states together, modeling world-action trajectories rather than actions alone. However, trajectory imitation does not ensure that these trajectories are task-effective or consistent with dynamics. We present \textbf{world action trajectory optimization (WATO)}, which optimizes generated actions and future states jointly for control. WATO uses both as references in a dynamics-constrained trajectory optimization problem and differentiates the task loss through the optimized trajectory to train the generator. This makes generated trajectories task-aware and dynamics-consistent while using trajectory optimization only during training. At deployment, WATO directly generates candidate trajectories and ranks them with a world model, without iterative trajectory optimization. Across $7$ visual control environments, WATO improves average success from $79.9\%$ for the imitation joint WAM to $91.7\%$, outperforming strong model-based, goal-conditioned RL, and generative control baselines. Compared with cross-entropy method (CEM), WATO is on average $11.5\times$ faster and uses $600.5\times$ fewer world model evaluations at deployment.

FlashRT-PAI: Fast Kernels Are Not Fast Pipelines for Physical AI 2026-11-12
📝 source code https://github.com/flashrt-project/FlashRT
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA), video-action (VA), and world action models (WAMs) form the backbone of modern physical AI systems, yet their real-time single-batch inference remains a defining bottleneck. Latency is dominated not by individual kernel computation, but by system-level overhead: thousands of short kernel launches, intermediate data movement across operator boundaries, and redundant recomputation across consecutive decisions. General-purpose graph compilers cannot address these costs, as they optimize individual calls in isolation and lack visibility into cross-call state semantics and global pipeline structure.
