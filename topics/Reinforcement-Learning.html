How Does RL Post-training Induce Skill Composition? A Case Study on Countdown 2026-10-22
Show abstract

While reinforcement learning (RL) successfully enhances reasoning in large language models, its role in fostering compositional generalization (the ability to synthesize novel skills from known components) is often conflated with mere length generalization. To this end, we study what RL post-training teaches about skill composition and how the composition structure affects the skill transfer. We focus on the Countdown task (given n numbers and a target, form an expression that evaluates to the target) and analyze model solutions as expression trees, where each subtree corresponds to a reusable subtask and thus can be viewed as a "skill." Tracking tree shapes and their success rates over training, we find: (i) out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization to larger n and to unseen tree shapes, indicating compositional reuse of subtasks; (ii) the order of skill acquisition depends upon the composition structure---models master shallow balanced trees (workload is balanced between subtasks) before deep unbalanced ones, with persistent fragility on right-heavy structures for a fixed composition depth. Our diagnostic reveals what is learned, in what order, and where generalization fails, clarifying how RL-only post-training induces OOD generalization beyond what standard metrics such as pass@k reveal. To study the implications of our findings for real-world tasks, we present a preliminary study on formal theorem proving.

METTA: Memory-Enhanced Test-Time Adaptation for Meta-Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-22
📝 9 pages
Show abstract

Meta-reinforcement learning aims to train agents that adapt quickly to unseen tasks from limited interaction, but this requires representing task evidence over long interaction histories. Classical recurrent agents compress experience into hidden states, while transformer-based agents retrieve from past trajectories at quadratic memory and computation cost. In this work, we propose Memory-Enhanced Test-Time Adaptation (METTA) for meta-reinforcement learning. It decomposes the fast adaptation phase into two temporal scales: a causal neural memory performs fine-grained within-episode adaptation with linear sequence complexity, while compact episode-level summaries support long-range cross-episode task inference without attending over the full interaction history. Moreover, equipping agents with neural memory provides sufficient expressive capacity and test-time training capability, enabling effective adaptation to unseen held-out tasks and task families at meta-test time. On unseen held-out Meta-World ML10/45 test environments, METTA achieves a success rate over $50\%$ while maintaining a low training budget and short wall-clock time. On long-context sparse-reward PointMaze tasks, it outperforms transformer-based baselines by exhibiting more structured exploration behavior. These results show that METTA provides an efficient path toward more adaptive and scalable long-context meta-RL.

Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Instrumental convergence is often invoked as a general reason to expect capable agents to acquire or express power-seeking subgoals: self-preservation, resource acquisition, deception, or resistance to oversight which poses an important threat to the society. For LLMs, reinforcement learning is the first stage of post-training that directly optimizes behavior against an objective; therefore, it is the stage where convergent instrumental behavior, on IC grounds, likely to appear. Does reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) make a model more likely to express instrumental-convergence behavior, or does this depend on the training regime, evaluation frame, and stage of post-training? We study this question as a checkpoint-conditioned behavioral trajectory rather than as a single before/after safety score. Using InstrumentalEval, we evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think across SFT, DPO, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final RLVR model. The trajectory is nonmonotonic: instrumental rate rises from 25.0% at SFT to 46.1% near RLVR step 200, then falls to 11.8% at the final checkpoint. Paired item-level tests and step-wise regression support the final SFT-to-RLVR reduction. Prompt framing does not explain this pattern: a harmful-prompt indicator combined with other framing effects explains only 15% of within-checkpoint score variance on average, and Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) removes a detectable framing direction without changing the SFT-to-final-RLVR gap. Together with preliminary 32B endpoint results (35.53% SFT to 22.37% RLVR final), these findings indicate that RLVR does not monotonically increase instrumental behavior in this setting: the rate rises early, then falls below the SFT baseline. The result is specific to this model family and benchmark, and judge agreement is moderate, so absolute rates should be read cautiously.

DiffDreamer: Coupling World Models with Differentiable Simulation for Agile Quadrotor Control 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Tasks such as quadrotor dynamic-gate racing require agents to anticipate external motion while maintaining precise high-frequency control. World-model-based reinforcement learning provides a natural solution by learning latent dynamics for future imagination, yet it often entangles environmental evolution and the robot's own dynamics in a single learned rollout, where model bias can affect policy optimization efficiency. We propose DiffDreamer, a differentiable reinforcement learning framework that tightly couples a learned world model with differentiable simulation for actor optimization. In one optimization step, the learned world model propagates latent states that capture external dynamics and history-dependent features, while the differentiable simulator propagates the robot's explicit physical state. We further decompose the actor objective into a critic-driven pathway for non-differentiable task rewards and a simulator-driven pathway for differentiable motion rewards. Experiments on aggressive tracking and dynamic-gate racing show that DiffDreamer improves training convergence over the model-free, model-based, and differentiable-RL baselines, and final performance over these baselines and classical planning and control methods. Additional experiments assess reward decomposition and the odometry-conditioned relative-state representation through ablations. We also provide proof-of-concept real-world flight experiments for validation.

Humanoid Mobility via Attention-based Contacts 2026-10-22
📝 under review
Show abstract

Humanoid mobility over complex terrain requires accurate foot placement and coordinated whole-body motion capable of robust adaptation. Reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained without motion references generalize well across terrains, yet eliciting effective whole-body coordination through reward design and exploration alone remains challenging. In contrast, reference-based methods yield natural motions but struggle to adapt beyond the predefined trajectories in their training data. We introduce a learning framework that bridges these two approaches by using motion data strictly as a training prior. An explicit, self-supervised, attention-based contact planner predicts future body-environment contacts, while a contact-conditioned tracker translates these plans into coordinated whole-body motion. Together, they establish contact as an interface between sensory observations and control actions. This abstraction allows the policy to internalize the contact-aware structure of locomotion rather than directly replicating kinematics. Consequently, a single policy can exploit data-driven motion priors for natural whole-body coordination while retaining the flexibility to discover environment-dependent behaviors through reinforcement learning. Building on the learned contact planner and tracker, a subsequent fine-tuning stage bootstraps their acquired capabilities to explore environment variations beyond those covered by the original motion data. Operating entirely from onboard observations without any reference inputs at deployment, this framework enables versatile humanoid mobility in challenging environments.

Projecting Latent RL Actions: Towards Generalizable and Scalable Graph Combinatorial Optimization 2026-10-22
📝 Preprint
Show abstract

Graph combinatorial optimization (GCO) has attracted growing interest, as many NP-hard problems naturally admit graph formulations, yet their combinatorial explosion renders exact methods computationally intractable. Recent advances in Reinforcement Learning (RL) combined with Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have significantly improved learning-based GCO solvers. However, existing approaches face limitations in both generalization across diverse graph instances and computational scalability as action spaces grow. To address both challenges, we introduce \textit{projection agents}, a GCO methodology built on the Wolpertinger framework and adapted to a shared GNN-based latent space for observations and actions. These agents are trained to solve GCO problems by predicting a desired latent action in a single forward pass, which is subsequently decoded into a valid discrete action in the graph using the latent space. Across diverse benchmarks, our approach achieves up to 6.41$\times$ faster inference and up to 75\% better generalization than existing solutions using a simple nearest-neighbor decoding, while opening the door to strong RL performance in super-linear decision spaces with multiple interdependent variables. Finally, we release \texttt{LaGCO-RL}, a Python library that automates latent action-space construction and supports existing RL-GCO solutions, promoting reproducibility and adaptation to new GCO benchmarks.

Same Luck, Different Choice: Twin-World Credit Assignment for Agentic Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Agentic reinforcement learning rewards a complete episode, yet the policy needs credit for every decision inside it. Counterfactual branching estimates this credit by re-running the episode after an alternative decision, but the branch draws its sampling noise independently of the factual episode, so the outcome difference mixes the effect of the decision with the luck of everything that follows. We observe that in agentic search the retriever, the corpus, and the grader are deterministic, so an episode is a deterministic function of the policy's decisions and its sampling noise. Twin worlds are therefore realizable: the same noise, a different decision. We introduce TWICE (TWIn-world Credit Estimation), which keys the Gumbel noise of every token by turn and line, rolls out counterfactual twins that inherit the factual episode's downstream noise, and turns their paired outcome differences into turn-level advantages for policy-gradient updates on actor-sampled trajectories; only the actor is deployed. Twin credit is unbiased for any coupling quality, its variance is that of an independent counterfactual scaled by $1-ρ$, where $ρ$ is the twins' outcome correlation, and under coupling the number of rollouts needed to resolve an advantage of size $Δ$ falls from $Θ(Δ^{-2})$ to $Θ(Δ^{-1})$. A square-root rule spends the twin budget where outcomes are predicted to disagree per unit cost. On seven question-answering benchmarks, TWICE improves the average exact match of GRPO by 5.3 and 4.5 points on Qwen2.5-3B and 7B, outperforms tree-search and counterfactual-branching baselines, and uses 68% fewer auxiliary tokens than VinePPO.

Towards User Simulators That Stay On Task And Still Sound Natural 2026-10-22
📝 30 pages, 16 figures, 6 tables. In submission
Show abstract

Users increasingly rely on AI systems for extended, multi-turn tasks, yet evidence indicates that LLM-based system performance degrades over prolonged interaction. To evaluate and improve AI assistants in such settings, we need user simulators that faithfully follow complex user intents while exhibiting realistic conversational behavior. Existing approaches manifest a trade-off: prompted assistants can follow complex user intents but lack behavioral fidelity, while specialized user simulators better approximate conversational behavior but struggle to remain faithful to complex intents. In this paper, we propose a reinforcement learning (RL) recipe that jointly rewards task intent adherence and behavioral naturalness. Ask About the Right Moment: Training-Free Object Identity Reasoning for Video-Language Models 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Many questions about a video concern one physical object. Where does the ball end up after the cups are shuffled? Which card is the queen? How often was the mug covered? Video-language models (Video-LMs) recognise such objects in single frames but do not keep them apart over time. On real shell-game videos, open models from 8B to 235B parameters, their thinking variants, a reinforcement-learned tool agent and three training-free tool agents all stay within ten points of chance. We separate semantic identity binding from temporal identity maintenance. An object's identity should be fixed at a moment when the evidence for it is visible, and carried afterwards by a tracker. \emph{Track, Then Bind} implements this principle without training. A promptable video tracker follows every candidate object and draws it with a track number. A frozen vision-language model answers a static question about the identifying evidence, which binds the target to a track. The answer is then derived from the tracked state in text, without asking the model to reinterpret the original video during read-out. A single configuration covers all benchmarks. With Qwen3-VL-32B it solves both VET-Bench tasks, where a fine-tuned trajectory model reaches 92\% and 90\% and the same VLM voting over 16 samples stays at 30--32\%. It answers 55.4\% of the hardest Perception Test cup games, where the best published model answers 41.5\%, and, using track-derived timelines, 48.6\% of TOC-Bench questions, against 37.1\% for the same model answering directly. Drawing the same tracks on the video and asking the question directly falls 12--19 points short of the agent on real cup games.

TRCA: Transition-wise Rubric Credit Assignment for Long-horizon LLM Agents 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Long-horizon large language model (LLM) agents are typically optimized with sparse terminal outcomes, making fine-grained credit assignment across multi-step interactions difficult. Existing approaches either rely on process evaluators, which incur annotation and inference costs, or derive step-level credit from successful trajectories. However, successful trajectories are extremely scarce during early-stage reinforcement learning, substantially weakening anchor-based methods. We propose Transition-wise Rubric Credit Assignment (TRCA), which derives step-level supervision directly from action-induced transitions without learned evaluators or successful anchors. TRCA evaluates each transition using Evidence, Execution, and Invalidity rubrics to capture task-relevant information acquisition, valid task execution, and invalid or regressive behavior. From these judgments, Foundational Rubric Reward measures local transition quality, while Breakthrough Rubric Reward tracks newly covered Evidence and Execution conditions to reward incremental task progress. Combined with terminal outcomes, these signals produce fine-grained step-level advantages for policy optimization. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, and seven search-augmented question-answering benchmarks show consistent improvements over the evaluated baselines. With Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, TRCA improves the WebShop score by 6.0%-12.6%; with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, it improves the average SearchQA score by 1.9%-18.3%. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of transition-wise rubric credit assignment for long-horizon tasks with sparse successful anchors.

Replan for Error Recovery: Training Embodied VLMs through Verifiable Interaction with Action-Grouped Turn-GSPO 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Embodied VLMs can execute increasingly complex manipulation tasks, yet execution errors can leave the environment in states that require replanning for recovery. Existing recovery methods either rely on inference-time mechanisms with fixed policies or learn from failure examples and reference corrections rather than verified action outcomes. To enable recovery learning through verifiable interaction, we introduce the Embodied Replanning for Recovery Environment (ERR-Env), which provides feedback on task progress and execution errors. We further construct ERR-Bench, a benchmark with held-out tasks spanning nominal, intermediate, and error states for evaluating recovery capability and execution reliability. We then propose Action-Grouped Turn-GSPO (AGT-GSPO), an online reinforcement learning method combining turn-level rewards for task progress and error states with action-grouped advantage estimation, and use it to train ERR-VLM, a compact 2B embodied VLM. To Distill or Decide? Understanding the Algorithmic Trade-off in Partially Observable Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-22
📝 45 pages, 9 figures, published at NeurIPS 2025
Show abstract

Partial observability is a notorious challenge in reinforcement learning (RL), due to the need to learn complex, history-dependent policies. Recent empirical successes have used privileged expert distillation--which leverages availability of latent state information during training (e.g., from a simulator) to learn and imitate the optimal latent, Markovian policy--to disentangle the task of "learning to see" from "learning to act". While expert distillation is more computationally efficient than RL without latent state information, it also has well-documented failure modes. In this paper--through a simple but instructive theoretical model called the perturbed Block MDP, and controlled experiments on challenging simulated locomotion tasks--we investigate the algorithmic trade-off between privileged expert distillation and standard RL without privileged information. Our main findings are: (1) The trade-off empirically hinges on the stochasticity of the latent dynamics, as theoretically predicted by contrasting approximate decodability with belief contraction in the perturbed Block MDP; and (2) The optimal latent policy is not always the best latent policy to distill. Our results suggest new guidelines for effectively exploiting privileged information, potentially advancing the efficiency of policy learning across many practical partially observable domains.

RIPPLE: Recovery Internalization through Process-Level Policy Learning and Effect-Aware Credit for Long-Horizon Agentic Coding 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Long-horizon coding agents can observe a failure and generate a plausible critique yet continue along the same incorrect trajectory. We call this the Critique-to-Action Handoff Gap. We introduce RIPPLE, a recovery-oriented training framework that internalizes process-level guidance into a single self-recovering policy. A prefix-only teacher provides evidence-grounded recovery supervision without access to future actions or terminal outcomes. After lightweight distillation, reinforcement learning assigns stage-specific credit to reflection triggering, diagnosis, and corrective action. Local validity signals gate trajectory advantages so that intermediate recovery decisions do not indiscriminately inherit terminal outcomes. Across four repository-level software-engineering benchmarks, RIPPLE improves the base policy by an arithmetic mean of 4.8 score points, with no auxiliary critic at deployment. Controlled ablations isolate the benefits of local, stage-aligned credit, and matched-prefix interventions show that critique semantics affect subsequent correction. Recovery gains concentrate where the prefix contains sufficient diagnostic evidence and the repository state remains reversible within the remaining budget.

R$^2$ Flow: Recursive Self-Improvement via Recursive Skill Evolution 2026-10-22
📝 27 pages
Show abstract

LLM-based agents can improve themselves across tasks by reusing and revising the skills they orchestrate into executable procedures. Flow-based training fits this loop: it samples procedures in proportion to reward, and the flow through each skill credits it for the next library revision. Three obstacles stand in the way of making this self-improvement reliable: flow training suffers strategy collapse over tree-structured histories; nonnegative flow-based credit rewards frequent use as if it were benefit; and library edits rest on the task reward the policy optimizes. We introduce R$^2$ Flow, a recursive self-improvement framework that alternates policy learning, independent verification, and versioned skill-library updates on a shared-state orchestration graph. The graph merges histories that differ only in the order of independent steps, allowing flow training to pool evidence across equivalent executions. A flow-share readout of the trained flow, invariant to the backward policy, and a separate signed utility rank which skills to change, verifier evidence decides whether an edit is warranted, and a residual-variance plateau sets when to update. Committed edits reshape the graph the next policy learns on, realizing recursive skill evolution. Across question answering, mathematical reasoning, interactive decision making, and code generation, R$^2$ Flow improves task accuracy and library-edit precision over heuristic orchestration, reinforcement learning, and skill-evolution baselines, and transfers across executors. Code is available at https://github.com/beita6969/r2flow.

Reinforcing intra-operative tumour localisation with weak supervision from pre-operative appearances 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Limited tumour visibility on intra-operative images complicates surgical navigation, including liver hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) targeting on computed tomography (CT) imaging. This makes labelled datasets difficult to curate for deep learning, and transferring pre-operative delineations through registration remains challenging due to intra-operative deformation and lack of paired pre- and intra-operative data. We propose to transfer tumour appearances learned from pre-operative magnetic resonance (MR) images to guide localisation on intra-operative CT through weak supervision. A classifier is trained to classify tumour presence on pre-operative images. This is applied to windows on intra-operative images, and despite the domain gap, it assigns higher tumour-presence probabilities on average to tumour-containing windows. Reward-Weighted Preference Optimization for Selective Combinatorial Optimization Problems 2026-10-22
📝 37 pages, 12 figures
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning has emerged as an effective approach for combinatorial optimization, but most existing methods focus on problems such as the Travelling Salesperson Problem (TSP) in which all elements must be served. Selective combinatorial optimization problems (SCOPs), including the Orienteering Problem (OP), Prize-Collecting TSP (PCTSP), Stochastic PCTSP and Knapsack Problem (KP), additionally require selecting which elements to include. This selection creates sampled solutions with substantially different objective values, yet standard preference optimization reduces such comparisons to binary rankings and discards the magnitude of these differences. We introduce Reward-Weighted Preference Optimization (RWPO), a pairwise training objective that incorporates reward-gap information into preference learning. For candidate trajectories sampled from the same instance, RWPO weights each preference loss according to the normalized difference in their rewards, assigning greater training emphasis to comparisons that represent larger improvements in solution quality. We further combine RWPO with scale-specific fine-tuning and reward- and likelihood-weighted tilted sampling to adapt trained policies to larger and shifted instance distributions without relying on local-search post-processing. Experiments on the OP, PCTSP, Stochastic PCTSP, and KP show that RWPO consistently improves over unweighted preference optimization and policy-gradient baselines across a wide range of instance sizes. On the OP, the resulting solver exceeds the mean reward of the Compass metaheuristic at scales from 100 to 2,000 nodes. The results demonstrate that preserving reward-gap information can substantially improve preference-based learning for selective combinatorial optimization and support generalization to large-scale instances.

From Comparison to Selection: Enhancing Cross-Scale Generalization of Pointer Networks Trained with Reinforcement Learning for for Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem 2026-10-22
📝 12 pages
Show abstract

Vehicle routing in delivery and logistics remains challenging as the number of clients varies. Classical approaches based on mixed-integer programming and heuristics can scale poorly or require problem-specific design, motivating learning-based alternatives. Reinforcement learning is particularly promising, provided a single policy can generalise across problem sizes without retraining. Although attention-based Pointer Networks are inherently size-agnostic, their solution quality degrades under both upscaling and downscaling. Using the Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP) as a controlled proxy, we investigate this degradation and identify three contributing factors: shifts in node density, attention dispersion, and dilution of the mean-pooled graph context. Exploiting this complementarity, we introduce an approach selector that chooses the best model for each training-target size pair. The selector reduces cost relative to the reference model across all nine seen train-test pairs and eight of nine pairs involving unseen sizes (CVRP35, CVRP75, and CVRP200), achieving improvements of up to 3.2%.

HiSTEVE 1.0: Harnessing Human-Interface VLA Agents for Open-World Games 2026-10-22

Where Do Long Captions Fail? Position-Aware Diagnosis and Reinforcement Learning for Detailed Image Captioning 2026-10-22
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 38 pages, 3 figures; includes references, appendices, and the paper checklist
Show abstract

Detailed image captioning is usually framed as an amount problem: a model should mention more visual facts while avoiding hallucination. However, autoregressive VLMs generate captions as ordered sequences, and later generations may be produced under weaker or noisier visual grounding. We therefore argue that long-form captioning should be studied not only by what facts are mentioned, but also by where they appear. We introduce a position-aware rubric representation that decomposes dense human references into atomic visual claims, verifies generated captions against those claims, and induces a quartile-wise composition of supported, hallucinated, and generic content. This lens reveals two qualitatively different failures in long captions: models tend to produce unsupported specificity in the middle, while the final portion often escapes into vague, non-evidential language rather than simply accumulating more hallucination. A same-reference human control does not exhibit these patterns; additional human audits examine the visual validity of the induced labels. Motivated by this diagnosis, we propose PAGER (Position-Aware Grounded Evidence Reinforcement), a GRPO training objective with separate signals for supported fine-grained middle evidence, evidence-bearing tail continuation, claim coverage, and content-aware length control. The result is a closed loop: the same ordered claim representation identifies where long captions fail and supplies the position-specific reward needed to repair those failures. Experiments on detailed-caption benchmarks show that PAGER improves position-wise grounding and pairwise caption preference while preserving competitive global caption quality.

AtManRL: Towards Faithful Reasoning via Differentiable Attention Saliency 2026-10-22
📝 19 pages, 23 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly rely on chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning to solve complex tasks, yet a reasoning trace may merely accompany the final answer rather than causally contribute to it or faithfully reflect how it was reached. We introduce differentiable attention manipulation to train salient reasoning with reinforcement learning -- method which we call AtManRL. By optimizing an additive attention mask that identifies the CoT tokens needed to recover the model's answer, we derive a saliency reward and combine it with an outcome reward in GRPO with decoupled advantages. Because both saliency and faithfulness depend strongly on trace length, we evaluate against a sweep of length-penalty baselines that traces the trade-off a pure length penalty can reach, rather than against a single baseline. Across three models (Qwen2.5-3B, Granite-3.3-2B, and Phi-4-mini) and four benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, ProofWriter, and QuestBench), AtManRL sits above this trade-off curve by 3.9-4.0pp in saliency, measured as answer changes under mistake injection, and by 5.7-7.7pp in faithfulness, measured as hint verbalization under hint injection, while matching the accuracy of baselines of similar length. These results suggest that training explicitly for saliency is a promising route towards chain-of-thought reasoning that is more faithful to the model's actual decision process.

FlowCritic: Bridging Value Estimation with Flow Matching in Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-22
Show abstract

Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a central paradigm for aligning Large Language Models with human values. However, the reliance on sensitive preference data necessitates rigorous differential privacy (DP) guarantees to prevent user information leakage. While multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has been proven effective across both collaborative and competitive tasks, existing algorithms often struggle to scale to large populations of agents.
