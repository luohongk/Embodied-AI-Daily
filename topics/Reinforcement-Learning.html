EgoActionBench: Benchmarking Dense Action Captioning for Egocentric Human Videos 2026-11-02
📝 32 pages, 16 figures. Project page: https://egoaction-bench.github.io
Show abstract

Learning from human data offers a scalable path toward embodied intelligence, yet it requires models to accurately understand fine-grained human actions from egocentric observations. However, existing benchmarks remain limited in action atomicity, temporal precision, and hand-action association. We introduce EgoActionBench, a benchmark that represents continuous human manipulation as temporally localized atomic actions associated with the left hand, right hand, or both hands. EgoActionBench contains 1,407 episodes with 41,132 human-refined action annotations. To evaluate such dense predictions, we propose an evaluation protocol that retrieves temporally eligible candidates, selects a prediction by content agreement, and scores semantic fidelity and temporal overlap alongside action precision and recall, jointly capturing what happens, when it happens, and which hand performs it. We benchmark 20 baseline models and systems on EgoActionBench and further adapt two backbones, Qwen3-VL-4B and LLaVA-OneVision-2-8B, through supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning on automatically annotated training episodes. EgoAction improves EgoActionScore by 15.9 and 23.2 points on the two backbones, and both adapted models outperform all open-source baselines. Our experiments reveal substantial limitations of current models in action atomicity, temporal precision, and hand-action association, highlighting the gap between general video understanding and the action understanding required for embodied intelligence.

GUI web agents require realistic and interactive environments that remain stable during both evaluation and training. Live website evaluation suffers from changing content, access restrictions, and limited coverage of authenticated workflows, while existing synthetic environments for training often lack functional depth and visual fidelity. We propose WebHarbor, a suite of 153 executable and dockerized website environments that faithfully reconstruct the visual interfaces, content, and functional workflows of real-world web services under deterministic control. We build WebHarbor through task-driven human-agent collaboration, where environments evolve as new tasks expand their functional scope, coding agents implement the required changes, and human contributors audit visual fidelity, functional correctness, and task validity. Not All Reasoning Generalizes: A Case Study on Learning Generalizable Reasoning Strategies in RLVR 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) can enable generalizable improvement for large language model (LLM) reasoning. This paper studies how to incentivize such generalization via reasoning strategies. We study this question with a controllable equation-solving framework: finding multiple inputs x for equations f(x)=y, where f is a non-bijective string function. This setting makes reasoning strategies observable while supporting a clear definition of easy-to-hard out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, e.g., more solutions, longer y, etc. We uncover a subtle RLVR failure: it teaches the model to solve in-distribution tasks with high accuracy and structured step-by-step derivation, yet under OOD shifts, the same model abandons derivation and collapses to ineffective brute-force enumeration, causing sharp accuracy drops. This observation reveals the deeper challenges of RLVR generalization as it is neither incapable base model nor reward hacking, since the model succeeds in-distribution with the correct reasoning strategy. The key to mitigating this failure is an indirect intervention that surprisingly helps: co-training with bijective inversion tasks. Although these bijective tasks are actually not uniformly harder and even further from the non-bijective OOD evaluation, they make derivation more certain and substantially restore the correct strategy and accuracy under OOD shifts. This effect further motivates our theoretical analysis: outcome-only RLVR can shape reasoning processes through implicitly crediting the effective reasoning strategies more, even without process supervision. To conclude, our analysis framework and findings systematically reveal the crucial role and incentivization of reasoning strategies in RLVR generalization under OOD shifts. Code is available at https://github.com/ziqipang/rl_meta_thinking.

FADE: Factored Ability--Difficulty Filtering for Online RLVR Scheduling 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Group-based reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) learns only from prompts whose sampled completions disagree: all-pass and all-fail groups produce zero relative advantage and burn generation budget without moving the policy. Adaptive schedulers try to steer rollouts toward informative prompts, yet they rely on per-prompt pass-rate trackers that entangle two quantities evolving at very different speeds---the policy's shifting competence across reasoning skills and each prompt's intrinsic difficulty. When a single exponential average or Beta counter tracks both at once, short memory scrambles the relative ordering of prompts whereas long memory reports stale pass rates. We introduce FADE (Factored Ability--Difficulty Estimation), a bilinear state-space filter that separates the two timescales. FADE represents each prompt's success logit as the inner product of a whitened $K$-dimensional skill loading with a fast-drifting policy ability vector, minus a slow-shrinking scalar item intercept. Using Pólya--Gamma augmentation, each minibatch of binary rollout outcomes updates the $K$-dimensional ability posterior in closed form via a rank-$K$ Woodbury step, instantly transferring competence shifts to all unobserved prompts that share those skills while leaving item intercepts stable. From the resulting posterior mean and variance we derive a closed-form utility for the probability of drawing a non-degenerate rollout group. Across five reasoning benchmarks, two base models, and four scheduling paradigms under a matched rollout budget, FADE consistently improves downstream accuracy and calibration while adding less than $0.6$\,ms per step.

D&A: Decoupling Description and Assessment Enables VLMs as Zero-Shot Robotic Reward Models 2026-11-02
📝 Website at https://weirdlabuw.github.io/dna
Show abstract

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) hold promise for zero-shot video task-progress estimation in robot learning, yet existing frameworks frequently suffer from confirmation bias, hallucinating progress in failed executions due to implicit success assumptions or relative temporal ranking designs. Naive prompting of frontier models collapses into a binary success detector that fails to capture nuanced, non-linear progress and failures. While post-training approaches can mitigate this through explicit training, they demand expensive real-world robotics data and require computationally intensive post-training for every individual base model. To resolve these limitations, we introduce D&A (Describe & Assess): our approach decouples progress estimation into an objective, task-grounded textual description of scene dynamics followed by an assessment phase using explicit rubrics. By compelling the VLM to synthesize an explicit, text-based definition of absolute success before scoring, the framework anchors evaluations to an external, global criterion rather than the video's internal timeline. Empirical results demonstrate that our method calibrates zero-shot estimates and mitigates confirmation bias, providing fine-grained, partial progress metrics that accurately penalize failed executions while correctly interpolating successful runs. Being purely prompt-based, D&A generalizes across tasks, embodiments, and benchmarks---matching or surpassing post-trained reward models---and its reward estimates enable efficient downstream reinforcement learning.

When Robots Outweigh Objects: How Inertia Mismatch Shapes Robot Policy Learning 2026-11-02
📝 9 pages, 17 figures. Project website: https://younghyopark.me/inertia-mismatch/
Show abstract

Conventional robot arms can be extremely heavy compared with the everyday objects they manipulate, creating a significant inertia mismatch at contact. Even a small accidental bump from a heavy arm can knock a light object out of place. Modern learning methods have nevertheless demonstrated a wide range of successful manipulation behaviors on these platforms, suggesting that even extreme inertia mismatch can be overcome with sufficient data. However, it remains unclear whether high inertia imposes a hidden cost on learning, potentially worsening scaling with data or lowering the performance ceiling of modern robot learning methods. We investigate this question through controlled simulation experiments that vary robot and object masses. We find that reinforcement learning succeeds across a broad range of mass combinations, while behavior cloning learns more reliable policies when a lighter robot manipulates a heavier object, and more data narrows the resulting gap without closing it. Behavior cloning likewise degrades on a simulated robot arm as its motors reflect more inertia through their gearing, even though its weight stays the same. Finally, because new hardware is rarely an option, we explore ways to lower contact inertia on the arm one already has, either by choosing a lower-inertia workspace before collecting data or by steering the demonstrator toward lower-inertia solutions of the task. Bridging Neural Tangent Kernel Analysis and Reinforcement Learning for Deep Function Approximation 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Despite their widespread success across machine learning, the learning dynamics and generalization properties of neural networks remain poorly understood. The neural tangent kernel (NTK) provides an analytical characterization of the learning dynamics of infinitely wide neural networks and serves as a useful proxy for finite-width networks in supervised learning. In this work, we extend the NTK framework to reinforcement learning (RL) by introducing a temporal-difference (TD) flow formulation that accommodates time-varying mini-batches and TD targets, yielding a closed-form solution for value function evolution in an overparametrized setting. Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) potentially offer a pathway to practical quantum advantage, but their optimization is heavily hindered by barren plateaus and numerous local minima. While classically simulable Clifford circuits can warm-start VQAs to accelerate convergence, existing heuristic-based initialization methods struggle to scale within vast combinatorial search spaces. We address this bottleneck with CRiSP (a Clifford Reinforcement Learning agent for State Preparation), which casts Clifford prefix selection as a sequential decision-making problem. CRiSP utilizes Neural-Guided Monte Carlo Tree Search, driven by a Transformer-based policy trained via self-play, to insert learned Clifford gates before fixed parameterized rotations. An Axiomatic Framework for Reinforcement Learning in Open Multi-Agent Systems 2026-11-02
📝 43 pages, Published at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Open multi-agent systems, where only a subset of agents are controllable, are central to real-world domains such as smart grids, yet lack a principled foundation for reinforcement learning algorithm design. We study this setting through n-agent ad hoc teamwork (NAHT), a recent framework for learning in open multi-agent environments. We propose an axiomatic framework that derives value function learning from the Shapley axioms (Efficiency, Symmetry and Linearity), treating them as structural constraints rather than heuristic design choices. This formulation builds on a novel NAHT decomposition, which characterises the fundamental difference between open and fully controllable multi-agent systems, and establishes a cooperative game-theoretic foundation for learning. Building on this framework, we introduce Shapley Machines and Banzhaf Machines, two generic algorithmic templates that can be instantiated with standard reinforcement learning methods. When applied to strong base algorithms such as IPPO and POAM, our methods consistently improve learning efficiency and generalisation to unseen agent types and conventions across NAHT benchmark tasks. SQLoom: Set-Contrastive Learning of Syntactic, Semantic, and Logical Dependencies for Text-to-SQL 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Text-to-SQL translates natural language questions into SQL queries, enabling database access without writing SQL. Fine-tuning open-weight large language models (LLMs) is cost-effective, but existing methods treat SQL as ordinary text and optimize token-level likelihood without explicitly modeling its syntactic, semantic, and logical requirements. Thus, a single incorrect alias or comparison operator can invalidate a query while contributing little to training loss. We propose SQLoom, a constraint-aware fine-tuning framework with supervision at three levels: syntactic validity under SQL grammar and database schema, semantic alignment between query decisions and the question, and logical consistency across dependent clause decisions. SQLoom extends supervised fine-tuning with a unified set-contrastive objective favoring sets of correct outputs over minimally different incorrect ones at each level. LLM agents propose this supervision, which a SQL parser and execution on counterexample databases verify. We also release Spider-Refined, a training dataset correcting 378 flawed Spider annotations and enriching database schemas. Experiments on Spider, BIRD, and three perturbed Spider variants show that SQLoom improves execution accuracy for Qwen3-1.7B from 62.9% zero-shot to 88.7% on Spider development, reaching 89.2% on Spider test and 73.4% on BIRD development. It outperforms fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and agent-based baselines with up to 70 billion parameters. On question perturbations, SQLoom loses at most 0.6 accuracy points versus up to 12.1 for agent-based methods. It generates SQL in a single pass at 0.42s per query, 23$\times$ faster than a GPT-4-based agent method. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of set-contrastive supervision over token-level training.

Inferring Transferable Rewards via Active Inverse Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) aims to recover expert rewards that transfer across environments with differing dynamics. Prior work has shown that such transfer is possible when gathering expert demonstrations across sufficiently different dynamics. In practice, however, expert demonstrations are costly, making it critical to select the right dynamics to reduce the quantity of expert data required for learning transferable rewards. We propose a two-phase approach: we first cycle through the available dynamics to obtain a reliable reward estimate, then actively select dynamics to reduce our uncertainty about the expert reward. Crucially, this yields novel sample complexity guarantees on transfer that hold for any dynamics, with a rate independent of the number of available dynamics. Empirically, our method significantly reduces the number of expert demonstrations needed to learn a transferable reward.

To address pressing scientific challenges such as climate change, increasingly sophisticated generative models are being developed to efficiently sample the large chemical space of potential functional materials. The proliferation of these models has necessitated the establishment of rigorous evaluation metrics. While uniqueness (U), novelty (N), and stability (S) of samples serve as standard metrics, their current formulations show several limitations. U and N rely on binary comparisons of crystals, rendering them dependent on heuristic thresholds, incapable of quantifying the degree of similarity, sensitive to atomic coordinate perturbations, and not invariant to sample permutation. Similarly, the binary assessment of S risks a premature exclusion of marginally unstable yet potentially novel candidates. Finally, the use of cSUN as a reward signal in reinforcement learning is explored, showing that its adjustable weighting scheme effectively enhances sample diversity and convergence to a better optima in a Chemeleon2 case study.

DeepWaveRL: Self-Supervised Full Waveform Inversion via Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Full Waveform Inversion (FWI) is a fundamental inverse problem in geophysics to estimate subsurface properties, such as velocity, from seismic measurements. Recently, self-supervised learning methods remove the need for ground-truth velocity maps during training by leveraging a forward operator to simulate seismic data from predictions and backpropagate the gradients of reconstruction loss through it. In this work, we consider a more challenging setting without accessing such gradients. To this end, we incorporate reinforcement learning into self-supervised FWI and propose a framework named DeepWaveRL. Specifically, we reformulate FWI as a policy learning problem, where the policy network predicts velocity maps as actions, and a forward operator is used solely for simulation. A global scalar reward is computed based on data misfit for each prediction and used to weight policy gradients. Thus, learning proceeds without accessing the gradients from the forward operator. Given the indirect and coarse supervision signals, we further identify key strategies to stabilize learning and mitigate the spatial credit assignment bottleneck. Experimental results show that, despite relying entirely on a single scalar reward, DeepWaveRL achieves competitive performance compared to supervised and self-supervised baselines. These findings suggest that our framework offers a flexible and complementary learning paradigm for the FWI research community.

The Cost of Mismatch: Noise Amplification in Zeroth-Order Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-02
📝 Accepted to NeurIPS 2026 (Poster). 31 pages, 2 figures, 6 tables. Code is available at https://github.com/lianmin-chen/the_cost_of_mismatch
Show abstract

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization has emerged as a promising approach for memory-efficient large language model (LLM) post-training, yet its reliable application to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) remains largely unexplored. Motivated by the distinct data regimes of on-policy RL and fixed-data optimization, we develop a generic variance-theoretic framework to investigate what limits the reliability of ZO gradient estimation. Our analysis reveals that estimation reliability is structurally determined by whether the two finite-difference evaluations are performed on matched data. On-policy reinforcement learning (RL) under the mismatched-data regime produces a $1/μ^2$ oracle-noise amplification that sets an irreducible noise floor as the perturbation scale $μ$ decreases, whereas Boltzmann Targeted SFT (BOLT) under the matched-data regime eliminates this amplification via noise cancellation. We further derive convergence guarantees with explicit constants that cleanly separate the two regimes. These results naturally lead to ZO-BOLT, which applies ZO optimization to BOLT's RLVR-aligned weighted-SFT objective. Controlled perturbation-scale sweeps and matched-versus-mismatched end-to-end comparisons validate the predicted effect of data matching. Experiments across three model variants and two mathematical reasoning benchmarks further show that ZO-BOLT consistently improves over the pretrained baseline while retaining a near-inference memory footprint. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/lianmin-chen/the_cost_of_mismatch.

Contextual Integrity (CI) defines privacy not merely as keeping information hidden, but as governing information flows according to the norms of a given context. As large language models are increasingly deployed as personal agents handling sensitive workflows, adhering to CI becomes critical.
