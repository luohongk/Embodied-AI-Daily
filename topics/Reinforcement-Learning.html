ASTOR: Multi-Task Code Reinforcement Learning via Utility-Driven Coordination 2026-10-29
📝 Accepted to NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards has proven effective at post-training LLMs for coding, yet deploying separate task-specific specialists incurs costs that scale with the number of tasks, motivating a unified multi-task RL (MTRL) approach. However, existing MTRL methods treat all coding tasks uniformly, relying on fixed data curricula under a shared optimization strategy, ultimately limiting the effectiveness of multi-task training. To address these limitations, we propose ASTOR, a multi-tASk code reinforcement learning framework via uTility-driven coORdination. Centered on task utility, a signal capturing each task learning potential and cross-task synergy, ASTOR comprises two coupled modules: 1) Hierarchical Utility-Routed Data Scheduling module hierarchically allocates training budget and prioritizes informative prompts, steering training toward the most valuable data and 2) Adaptive Utility-Calibrated Policy Optimization module dynamically scales per-task KL regularization, matching update constraints to each tasks current training state. Experiments on two widely-used LLMs across four representative coding tasks demonstrate that ASTOR consistently improves a single model across all tasks, outperforming the best task-specific specialist by 9.0%-9.5% and surpassing the strongest MTRL baseline by 7.5%-12.8%.

Timbre: Reliability-aware harmonic consensus for label-free multimodal test-time rexinforcement learning 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Label-free test-time reinforcement learning adapts vision--language models using rewards derived from their own predictions. However, misinterpreted visual evidence and sensitivity to textual formulation can produce a consistent but incorrect consensus that majority voting reinforces. Cross-view aggregation can expose such errors, but image and text transformations may themselves degrade task-relevant evidence to different extents. We introduce \method, a reliability-aware harmonic consensus framework for multimodal test-time reinforcement learning. \method constructs a $2\times2$ grid combining original and perturbed images with direct-solving and reframing modes, selects pseudo-labels through weighted harmonic aggregation, and adapts modality-level weights using batch-averaged, temporally smoothed shifts in pseudo-label confidence as a heuristic reliability signal. We formulate consensus as a weighted $χ^2$-barycenter problem whose unique minimizer is the normalized weighted harmonic aggregate, without requiring correct-answer invariance. An exact support--dispersion decomposition characterizes when harmonic aggregation preserves or corrects arithmetic rankings, while a sensitivity analysis establishes how modality reweighting changes pairwise ranking margins, informing interpretation of the heuristic updates. Across six benchmarks and three Qwen3-VL model scales, \method outperforms TTRV in 16 of 18 benchmark--model settings on adaptation instances and 17 of 18 settings on held-out instances under matched estimation and optimization rollout counts. These results demonstrate improved target-task adaptation and transfer to unseen instances without further updates.

Latent Policy Steering through One-Step Flow Policies 2026-10-29
📝 RSS 2026, Project Webpage : https://jellyho.github.io/LPS/
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) allows robots to learn from offline datasets without risky exploration. Yet, offline RL's performance often hinges on a brittle trade-off between (1) return maximization, which can push policies outside the dataset support, and (2) behavioral constraints, which typically require sensitive hyperparameter tuning. Latent steering offers a structural way to stay within the dataset support during RL, but existing offline adaptations commonly approximate action values using latent-space critics learned via indirect distillation, which can lose information and hinder convergence. We propose Latent Policy Steering (LPS), which enables high-fidelity latent policy improvement by backpropagating original-action-space Q-gradients through a differentiable one-step MeanFlow policy to update a latent-action-space actor. By eliminating proxy latent critics, LPS allows an original-action-space critic to guide end-to-end latent-space optimization, while the one-step MeanFlow policy serves as a behavior-constrained generative prior. This decoupling yields a robust method that works out-of-the-box with minimal tuning. Across OGBench and real-world robotic tasks, LPS achieves state-of-the-art performance and consistently outperforms behavioral cloning and strong latent steering baselines.

Safe Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning: Zero Constraint Violation and Primal-Averaging Convergence 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Many applications of multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) require policies that balance conflicting objectives while satisfying safety constraints. We study safe MORL with zero constraint violation throughout learning, covering both cumulative and stepwise constraints. A key challenge is to explore sufficiently under unknown dynamics and constraint costs without violating the constraints. For a given preference over objectives, we develop an algorithm to learn a safe Pareto optimal policy by combining optimistic reward estimates with pessimistic cost bounds. With high probability, every policy executed over $T$ episodes incurs zero constraint violation. The algorithm returns a safe Markov policy induced by the averaged occupancy measure and achieves an $\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ suboptimality gap under smooth Tchebycheff scalarization. We further develop a preference-free algorithm that first explores safely without a prespecified preference and then computes a safe policy for any given preference without further interaction. Over $T$ exploration episodes and $K$ planning iterations, it retains the same safety guarantee and achieves an $\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{T}+1/\sqrt{K})$ suboptimality gap. Both algorithms require knowledge of a policy that strictly satisfies the constraints and its costs. We then relax these requirements, incurring at most constant cumulative violation. To our knowledge, we provide the first sample-efficient MORL algorithms that guarantee safety for every executed policy.

Optimal Value Inference for Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-29
Show abstract

We study offline inference for the optimal value in reinforcement learning under finite state and action spaces. Two new nuisances are derived as fixed points of a self-induced Bellman equation, in which we approximate the maximum Bellman operator by its softmax correspondence. We propose a debiased estimator through the Neyman orthogonality and establish its asymptotic normality under diverging horizons even when the behavior policy changes with time, as long as the nuisances have the statistical rates that can be achieved by many machine learning methods. We provide a concrete estimating procedure for these nuisances and show they can lead to valid inference. Synthetic experiments validate the numerical performance of our inference method, and we implement it in real-life decision-making problems, including bike repositioning and AI agentic tool use.

Train the Agent, Not the Expert: Learning to Harness Heterogeneous Experts for Multi-Turn Visual Reasoning 2026-10-29

Show abstract

Recent advances in computer vision have produced powerful specialized models for detection, segmentation, counting, and other visual tasks. However, these models are typically optimized for isolated tasks, limiting their ability to support general-purpose visual intelligence, particularly when complex language understanding and dense small-object perception are required. We propose VisHarness, a trainable visual agent that decouples high-level perception, reasoning, and decision-making from low-level task execution. Rather than training a task-specific model, we train VisHarness to harness a set of carefully designed heterogeneous visual experts. This paradigm preserves the agent's general intelligence while leveraging the precision of specialized visual experts for specific tasks. Our code, model weights, and training data are publicly available at https://github.com/fyw1999/VisHarness.

MRC-MMAgent: Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Role Collaboration in Multimodal Tool-Using Agents 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Multimodal tool-using agents augment vision-language models with access to external information and capabilities, enabling them to solve knowledge-intensive and visually complex tasks that cannot be addressed using visual context alone. However, existing reinforcement learning pipelines largely train single-role agents to search and answer using terminal rewards, overlooking how humans inspect retrieved evidence, reformulate queries, and revise conclusions during search. Consequently, they fail to optimize this self-corrective process over complete interaction trajectories. We propose MRC-MMAgent, a multi-role collaborative reinforcement learning framework that incorporates trajectory assessment and continuation directly into the executable agent loop. First, the framework establishes a multi-role collaborative loop that integrates solving, inspection, and continuation, enabling the agent to acquire evidence through tools, evaluate the preceding trajectory, and resume problem solving when the current answer is insufficiently supported. Second, we introduce a role-coupled learning objective that combines task success, interaction validity, and successful correction with full-trajectory policy optimization across the decisions generated by all roles. Extensive experiments show that, using only the original baseline training set without constructing additional datasets, our method consistently outperforms the corresponding base models and achieves leading or highly competitive performance across benchmarks.

The Hitchhiker's Guide to System 1 Models: Fast Decision-Making Beyond LLMS 2026-10-29
📝 version 1.1 - fixes to references/some content fixes
Show abstract

Large Language Models excel at reasoning and generation but their autoregressive, token-by-token inference makes them unnecessarily slow, expensive, and poorly calibrated for the many routine decisions (routing, gating, scoring, classification) that modern AI systems require at sub-second latency. A growing class of models addresses this gap: System 1 decision models, architectures that map an input directly to a typed, structured decision in a single forward pass. This guide provides a comprehensive, practitioner-oriented treatment of the field. We propose a five-family taxonomy covering encoder-based classifiers, reward models, non-autoregressive decision engines, contrastive decision models, and extraction models, and we provide in-depth coverage of each family's architecture, training methodology, and deployment trade-offs. A central theme is calibration: we develop the theory of proper scoring rules, analyze Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), and examine recently discovered phenomena including IIA violations, ordinal-scale bias, and few-shot recalibration under distribution shift. On the integration side, we describe how System 1 models serve as decision infrastructure within agentic workflows and System 1 + System 2 cascades that route between fast and slow models based on calibrated confidence. The guide covers 18 application domains, maps the rapidly growing open-source ecosystem (over 70 model reproductions in weeks), provides runnable code throughout, and includes a practitioner's checklist, an open-source model zoo, and 22 self-assessment questions with detailed answers. Spanning 190 references, it is intended as a unified entry point for researchers and engineers working at the intersection of fast decision-making, calibration, and production AI systems.

There Is No Free Lunch in RLVR: Every pass@1 Gain Has a pass@k Price 2026-10-29
📝 19 pages, 4 figures. Code and verification package (34/34 automated checks): https://github.com/yliusci/no-free-lunch-rlvr
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) makes a language model solve more problems on the first try and fewer when given many tries: it raises pass@1 while the base model overtakes it at large sampling budget k. Is this a failure of RL to add reasoning, or a transient that more training removes? We settle the question with a theorem. The reversal is a price, and we compute its exact rate. Modeling RLVR as an exponential tilt of a base policy over strategies shared across prompts, we prove that under exclusive specialization every update that raises pass@1 strictly lowers the success of some prompt; a strictly positive price on prompts (Stiemke duality) is the exact condition, and being merely the best strategy on a prompt is provably not enough. When the loss reaches the hardest prompts, it forces a pass@k crossover, and the number of crossings is at most the sign variation of the shift in per-prompt success, a totally positive bound that is tight and that recovers the known single-crossover result as its order-preserving case. The crossover is not a bug to patch: a constrained update traces the exact pass@1 versus pass@k frontier, and removing the crossover entirely costs the entire pass@1 gain. The same tilt identity yields entropy collapse and the entropy-performance law with an identifiable exponent, unifying two literatures under one mechanism. Reinforcement cannot widen the reasoning frontier; it can only move along it, at a price we make measurable by a diagnostic that reads the bound, the order-preserving test, and the price from any evaluation log. There is no free lunch, only a price.

Towards Better Training Signal: Advantage Clipped Policy Optimization 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a cornerstone for improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), but the need for on-policy data substantially limits training efficiency. Reusing off-policy data through importance sampling (IS) can improve efficiency but introduce considerable instability. Hence, algorithms such as PPO and GRPO widely adopt IS-ratio clipping to stabilize training. However, training stability and gradient estimate are mainly determined by the product of IS ratio and advantage. To further stabilize training, we propose ACPO, which clips the product of the IS ratio and the advantage, leading to more stable gradient estimates. We also establish a connection between ACPO and gradient clipping in policy mirror descent (PMD), which is a standard technique to stabilize optimization process, and prove the convergence of clipped-PMD under the standard RL setting. Experiments on widely used mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that ACPO consistently outperforms PPO and GRPO in both accuracy and training efficiency, delivering 4-6 percentage points gains on standard math benchmarks, with Qwen3-8B+PPO. Hence, ACPO is a practical and effective alternative to conventional IS-ratio clipping for RL post-training of LLMs.

Multi-image composition (MICo) is a critical yet challenging capability for large-scale image editing models, where consistency degrades rapidly as the number of reference images increases. Grounding Multimodal Reasoning with Evidence-Ablated Negatives 2026-10-29
📝 NeurIPS2026
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a powerful tool for improving multimodal reasoning. However, standard outcome supervision suffers from credit assignment ambiguity, as a model may receive positive rewards by exploiting language priors or dataset shortcuts rather than grounding its reasoning in the requisite visual evidence. This makes high-quality negative responses critical, since effective negatives should remain plausible under the current policy while exposing the shortcut that should be suppressed. In this paper, we propose EVAN, a multimodal reinforcement learning framework utilizing EVidence-Ablated Negatives to train LMMs against their own language-prior failures. A Sequential Decision Framework for Evaluating LLM-Guided Search 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Recent systems applying retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and "deep research" iteratively generate queries, retrieve documents, and examine their contents, using the resulting information to guide subsequent search steps. When this process is carried out autonomously rather than by a human searcher, the system must make explicit decisions about which search trajectories to explore and what objective the search is intended to optimize. We introduce a simple framework for reasoning about such search policies, providing a principled approach to evaluation. The framework models the search environment as a set of ranked lists and represents behavior using two primitive actions: select, which advances judgment through an existing ranking, and spawn, which generates a new ranking through query generation or reformulation. Each action produces a document to inspect, yields information that updates the search state, and contributes a reward for a task-specific objective. Within this abstraction, a variety of existing retrieval strategies can be expressed as policies operating over a common environment, while common retrieval objectives can be represented through reward and weighting functions over the resulting search trajectory, and the resulting formulation admits a natural reinforcement learning interpretation. We illustrate the approach using high-recall information retrieval (HRIR), where the goal is to identify as many relevant documents as possible with limited judging effort, showing how the framework provides a unified way to describe and reason about strategies for both classical retrieval tasks and emerging LLM-guided search systems. Through experiments, we demonstrate that both the underlying retrieval method and the "deep research" policy employed can materially affect performance.

Event2Reward: Learning Robotic Rewards from Progress Relations 2026-10-29
📝 44 pages, including references and appendices
Show abstract

Learning task progress from robot demonstrations provides a practical way to construct dense shaping rewards for robotic reinforcement learning. A common approach uses normalized demonstration time as progress labels, implicitly assuming monotonically increasing task progress. During policy exploration, however, actual task progress can stall or even regress. Such models may mistake regressions for progress, assigning positive shaping rewards to actions that cause progress loss. Real suboptimal and failed executions offer corrective experience, but assigning absolute progress values to their states is difficult. Our key observation is that stalls, regressions, and recoveries establish local relative progress relations even when absolute progress is unknown. We introduce Event2Reward, which uses normalized temporal labels to anchor the progress scale and these within-trajectory relations to constrain local changes, combining absolute and relative progress supervision. Its progress predictions serve as potentials for reward shaping. We curate an event-annotated dataset from approximately 100K raw real-robot execution records, covering successful, suboptimal, and failed executions, without requiring framewise absolute progress labels. Under the same training budgets, Event2Reward improves average policy success by 18.16 percentage points over a baseline using only sparse task rewards across 40 tasks in four LIBERO suites. On real robots, average success after 30 minutes of wall-clock training reaches 60.0% on three precise insertion tasks and 90.0% on three object manipulation tasks, exceeding the sparse-reward baseline under the same budget by 36.7 and 23.3 percentage points, respectively. Ablation studies further show that absolute progress supervision and relative progress relations are complementary, with joint supervision yielding better downstream policy learning.

Reasoning or Pattern Exploitation? Mechanistic Insights from RL-Trained Language Models 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly described as acquiring "reasoning" skills after reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. However, whether these gains reflect genuine reasoning or more superficial adaptations remains underexplored. In this work, we probe LLMs trained in a finite and exhaustively analyzable logical domain, namely Tic-Tac-Toe, and trace how internal representations evolve across reinforcement learning (RL) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Quantitatively, RL yields high accuracy and robustness across prompt variations, suggesting the emergence of game reasoning. Mechanistic interpretability, however, paints a different picture: features extracted with sparse autoencoders (SAEs) reveal that models primarily adapt to rely more heavily on information already explicit in the prompt, such as whose turn it is, game progression and board occupancy. In comparison, high-level concepts like board symmetries and strategic forks remain weakly represented, raising concerns that reasoning benchmarks overestimate model abilities. This suggests that RL-driven "reasoning" may conflate task-specific adaptations to input information with reasoning ability. We provide, for the first time: (i) a systematic interpretability pipeline tracing representation dynamics across RL training in small-scale LLMs; (ii) an extension of SAE-based feature discovery to hypothesis-driven testing in a finite logical domain; and (iii) an interpretability-based demonstration that RL post-training neither adds nor removes concept information but reorganizes which features dominate the representation, with causal interventions locating the decision downstream of it, in the late-layer state.

Appreciation Teaches Unauthorized Execution: Self-Evolution Degrades the Robustness of Personal Agents to Indirect Prompt Injection 2026-10-29
Show abstract

Self-evolving personal agents act for users and learn from user feedback. However, if an agent obeys an instruction that an untrusted third party hides in content, the third party gains control of the agent. Such indirect prompt injection (IPI) poses severe risks. We investigate whether self-evolution degrades the robustness of personal agents to IPI. To study this question, we design a controlled two-phase methodology. In the adaptation phase, the user asks the agent to read external content and complete the task described in it, a common request in daily use. The agent then learns from the user's feedback and improves itself as designed. In the test phase, a third party writes a similar task into new external content, but the user's test request does not authorize the injected task. Robotic Data Inoculation 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Modern robot learning systems feed on high-quality demonstration data, and their performance hinges on coverage. Without good coverage, small errors compound and the policy can quickly go into out-of-distribution states from which it cannot recover. Yet there is no principled way of collecting high-coverage data, and data collection often relies on heuristic instructions to human operators. Purely online reinforcement learning (RL) explores at the cost of safety and sample efficiency, while DAgger-style interventions require the policy to fail by chance before a human can correct it. In this paper, we ask instead whether we can modify how the robot behaves during data collection to collect more useful data. We propose Data Inoculation (DI), a shared-control framework in which a human provide closed-loop teleoperation commands while a residual policy perturbs these commands. The residual policy is trained online, on the growing dataset, with the same reward-maximizing objective later used for policy extraction, so its perturbations steer data collection toward the deviations a policy trained on the data would make. These perturbations expose the robot to a wider range of states, while the human's continuous corrections provide recovery demonstrations, "inoculating" the dataset against the learner's future mistakes while the human remains in control. On three challenging real-world robotic tasks, DI consistently improves or matches the success rate of policies trained with both behavioral cloning and offline RL, showing DI as an effective data collection strategy for improving downstream policy performance.

Reinforcement Learning for the Game of Balatro: A Fast Simulator and Two-Policy Training Approach 2026-10-28
📝 Accepted at EXAG 2026 (AIIDE Workshop on Experimental AI in Games)
Show abstract

Balatro, a 2024 roguelike deckbuilder, presents three difficulties that have historically derailed reinforcement learning methods: a 3,000-action state-dependent action space, horizons of several hundred decisions with sparse rewards, and pervasive stochasticity in both card draws and shop generation. Published RL results on the game remain scarce. We introduce an end-to-end pipeline comprising (i) a high-throughput Rust simulator that is automatically state-diffed against the live game client via BalatroBot, (ii) a condensed observation-action interface with dynamic per-step masking of legal moves, and (iii) a multi-agent PPO architecture that decomposes play into a tactical blind policy and a strategic shop policy that share the full game state but act in disjoint phases. Long-horizon credit assignment is partially addressed by per-agent reward routing with explicit cross-agent credit. On the main joint run, training metrics show a sharp phase transition in which the first-blind clear rate converges above 99% and mean episode return increases by roughly an order of magnitude, plateauing at a mean maximum ante of 3 out of 8; single-agent baselines under identical rewards oscillated between greedy and hoarding failure modes and did not stably clear the first blind. These results establish the two-policy decomposition as a strong reactive baseline for Balatro and localise the remaining gap to long-horizon strategic planning rather than tactical blind play.

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) enables teams of agents, such as robots and autonomous vehicles, to learn coordinated behavior, but what such teams learn is largely determined by their reward function. Because task success alone provides too little guidance for learning, intermediate rewards are usually designed by hand, and this effort must often be repeated whenever the environment or the team changes.
