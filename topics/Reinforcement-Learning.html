Learning Grounded Reasoning from Human Gaze 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Human visual reasoning is driven by systematic exploration and extraction of task-relevant visual evidence. Human gaze provides a window into this process, making gaze a useful source of guidance for visually-grounded reasoning in vision-language models (VLMs). However, human gaze also includes noise and redundancies that may transfer poorly to VLMs. In this work, we investigate how human gaze data can be effectively leveraged to improve visual reasoning in VLMs, while maintaining alignment with human behavior. We collect 7,109 human gaze trials from a diverse and challenging set of visual reasoning tasks, and train grounded reasoning models with different forms of gaze guidance. Across evaluations in chart understanding, visual search, and counting, we find that using human gaze as a prior for grounded reasoning consistently outperforms supervision using the full gaze sequence. Based on these findings, we introduce GGR (Gaze-Guided Reasoner), which achieves greater human alignment and more reliable visual grounding while matching or outperforming leading grounded-reasoning baselines in accuracy. Reinforcement learning further improves task accuracy, gaze alignment, and grounding quality despite using no gaze supervision. Behavioral analyses show that gaze supervised models exhibit more visual exploration before arriving at an answer. Our results suggest that the value of human gaze lies in guiding spatial exploration and shaping reasoning behavior without requiring complete imitation of human behavior.

Get Hint-Augmented GRPO Done Right Through Activation Steering 2026-11-17
Show abstract

GRPO has become the dominant reinforcement learning method for eliciting reasoning capability in LLMs using verifiable rewards. However, it suffers from a fundamental failure mode: when a training question exceeds the base model's current capability, all generated responses fail and receive zero reward, yielding no gradient signal and stalling further capability expansion. An emerging class of GRPO extensions, termed Hint-Augmented GRPO (HA-GRPO), adds a "hint" to the context to reduce the difficulty of the question, thereby producing a usable gradient signal. Nevertheless, existing HA-GRPO methods share a key issue: no hint is available at inference time to append to the question, creating a training-inference context mismatch that leads to inconsistent performance gains. In this work, we reinterpret the function of hinting -- not as reducing question difficulty, but as steering model behavior -- and propose ASH (Activation Steering for Hinting), a novel HA-GRPO method built on this view. During training, the model is presented with both hinted and plain questions, while a trainable steering vector is optimized to widen the margins between the model's generation probabilities for responses to the hinted version relative to the plain version, thereby capturing the question-agnostic component of the behavioral shift induced by the hint. During inference, the trained steering vector is directly injected into the model's activation, reproducing the hint-conditioned behavior without requiring any hint in the context. Experiments with four LLMs (Qwen3-1.7B/4B-Base, Llama-3.2-1B/3B-Instruct) show that ASH consistently improves pass@$k$ on competition-level mathematics benchmarks (AMC, OlympiadBench, AIME24, AIME25) and out-of-distribution benchmarks (Minerva, GPQA-Diamond). Notably, it improves average pass@8 of Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-4B-Base by \textbf{2.60}\% and \textbf{2.21}\%.

CADENCE: Learning Acyclic Gaits for Model-Based Quadruped Locomotion 2026-11-17
📝 18 pages, 8 figures, 2 tables
Show abstract

Model-based quadruped controllers usually step on a cyclic gait, which lifts the legs in a fixed order on a fixed period, wherever the safe footholds are. We present CADENCE, a learned step planner with a small action space. Every 40 ms, it picks one of at most 65 discrete choices: no step, or one leg's step to a safe foothold. It also predicts a swing time. We train CADENCE by reinforcement learning on top of a convex model predictive controller (MPC). Nothing prescribes a gait: there is no gait schedule, and the reward has no gait term, only a cost per step. The one structural constraint is that at most two legs may swing at once. The policy we analyzed developed an acyclic gait, with no fixed period or stepping order, yet it still paired diagonal legs. An inverse footstep model (IFM), fitted on a controller's logged steps, turns each foothold into a command. With only the IFM refitted, on fewer than 20,000 steps, CADENCE ran frozen on OCS2's nonlinear MPC. In simulated trials on a range of terrains (26% to 49% voids) and speeds (0.1 m/s and 0.2 m/s), it succeeded in 85.1% (mean of five seeds), against 67.8% for OCS2-Trot, 65.5% for OCS2-Single, and 39.6% in a static walk ablation of CADENCE. At 49% voids, it succeeded in 72.9%, against 43.0% for OCS2-Trot.

Learning to Undo: Transfer Reinforcement Learning under State Space Transformations 2026-11-17
📝 NeurIPS'26 paper
Show abstract

Transfer learning in reinforcement learning (RL) has shown strong empirical success. In this work, we take a more principled perspective by studying when and how transferring knowledge between MDPs (a source and a target) can be provably beneficial. Specifically, we consider the case where there exists an undo map such that applying this map to the target's state space recovers the source exactly. We propose an algorithm that learns this map by matching state feature statistics gathered from both MDPs, and then uses it to transfer the source policy to the target. We theoretically justify the algorithm by analyzing the setting when the undo map is linear and the source is linearly-$Q^\star$ realizable, where our approach has strictly better sample complexity than tabula rasa RL in the target MDP. Empirically, we demonstrate that these benefits extend beyond this regime: on challenging continuous control tasks and Atari games, our method achieves significantly better sample efficiency. Overall, our results highlight how shared structure between tasks can be leveraged for efficient transfer of policies across environments.

Deep Reinforcement Learning for Heat Pump and Underfloor Heating Control: An End-to-End Case Study 2026-11-17
📝 Preprint submited to journal
Show abstract

Efficient control of underfloor heating (UFH) systems coupled with heat pumps (HPs) is challenging due to the high thermal inertia of buildings and the slow dynamic response of UFH systems. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) offers a data-driven control approach capable of simultaneously optimizing energy consumption and thermal comfort. However, the application of DRL-based controllers in occupied buildings under real operating conditions remains limited. This work presents and end-to-end case study (development, evaluation, and real-world deployment) of a DRL-based controller for an integrated UFH-HP system. First, a simulation-based pilot study was conducted to assess different DRL algorithms, control strategies, and configurations. Subsequently, a digital twin of a real residential building was developed and used to train DRL agents under simulated operating conditions. The best-performing agent was then deployed in the physical building without additional online fine-tuning. The results show that the controller successfully maintained indoor temperatures within the prescribed comfort limits while dynamically adjusting the HP supply temperature and UFH valve positions. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of deploying DRL controllers in occupied buildings and highlight their robustness and the practical challenges associated with transferring control policies from digital environments to real heating systems.

When Anchors Shift the Target: Advantage-Gated Adaptation for Diffusion Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-17
📝 12 pages, 4 figures, including supplementary material
Show abstract

Forward-process reinforcement learning adapts diffusion and flow-matching models by re-noising rewarded generations into regression targets. Because negative advantages induce concave updates with unbounded repulsive gradients, existing methods stabilize training using cached predictions from a moving policy copy, yet these anchors also alter what positive feedback teaches the model to fit. We formalize this conflict as anchor coupling: after separating frozen-reference regularizers, affine moving-anchor rules cannot eliminate negative curvature while preserving every positive regression target. We propose Advantage-Gated Adaptation (AGA) to decouple these roles. Positive samples retain standard target fitting, whose self-limiting update vanishes at the observed target independently of the moving anchor. Negative samples instead use the first-order tangent of the concave objective at the moving-policy prediction, preserving the local repulsion direction with a prediction-independent gradient. Within the quadratic-anchor family, these requirements uniquely determine the parameter-free gate $ω(a)=\max(-a,0)$. Because the moving copy provides local linearization rather than retention, AGA assigns long-horizon behavior preservation to a frozen pretrained reference regularized at its operating classifier-free guidance scale, interpreted through an implicit-reward lens. Across compositional generation, text rendering, and preference alignment, our experiments show that displacing positive targets degrades visual quality and heightens sensitivity to policy-copy schedules, whereas AGA consistently improves the reward--quality trade-off.

M3OPD-Reasoner: An Open Recipe for Post-Training Multimodal Reasoning with Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (MOPD) enables a single student to absorb the skills of several domain teachers by learning from its own generated responses. We present Multimodal Model Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation Reasoner (M3OPD-Reasoner), a fully open recipe for post-training vision-language models (VLMs) over three reasoning domains: image STEM, image perception, and video. We curate two datasets, M3OPD-SFT-1.35M for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and M3OPD-RL-193K for reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards. On these data, we compare post-training recipes that start from Qwen3.5-9B: training one model on the mixed data of all domains (MixSFT and MixRL), and training one teacher per domain with Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), directly or after an SFT warm-up, and distilling the teachers into one student with MOPD. We evaluate on 21 image STEM, image perception, and video reasoning benchmarks after we decontaminate the training data against them. The comparison establishes MOPD of teachers trained with GSPO directly from Qwen3.5-9B as the best recipe: the student closely matches each teacher on its own domain and is on average above the mixed-training baselines on the same prompts. Since MOPD already merges the domain capabilities that MixRL would otherwise have to learn, its student is a stronger starting point for RL, and we ask whether the same data can carry the model further from there: we add MixRL on top of the MOPD student, on the same prompts and with the same settings, which, to our knowledge, no prior VLM recipe has studied. The same prompts still improve the MOPD student, with no collapse, and give our main model, M3OPD-Reasoner-9B. MOPD and MixRL can therefore work as consecutive stages that get more out of the same data. We release all data, the training code, and the checkpoints of every stage.

Residual-Guided Lightweight Reinforcement Learning for Risk-Aware Trajectory Selection with a Frozen World Model 2026-11-17
📝 6 pages, 2 figures
Show abstract

In automated driving, world models provide future scene predictions for evaluating candidate trajectories, but fixed selection rules may not capture scene-dependent trade-offs. We propose a residual-guided reinforcement learning (RL) framework for lightweight trajectory selection with a frozen world model and a fixed candidate bank. The framework uses proximal policy optimization (PPO) to fine-tune a joint policy over candidate selection and bounded trajectory residuals. A context-conditioned ranking network learns candidate preferences from feedback obtained by evaluating local perturbations of the selected trajectories. At inference, the selector combines learned preference and predicted risk ranks using fixed weights and returns the selected candidate trajectory without modification. Fine-tuning updates 16,106 parameters across the actor and critic, while the deployed selector retains only the 11,725-parameter ranking network, with the residual branch and critic used exclusively during training. In open-loop evaluation on the nuScenes validation set, the method reduces average Euclidean trajectory error (L2) by 3.99% relative to the RiskWorld model baseline without RL-based selection. Under matched budgets for feedback accesses and actor updates, residual exploration reduces L2 by 0.83% relative to the categorical PPO variant trained without residuals. The method also maintains lower L2 than RiskWorld when risk features are removed or shuffled.

DexFormer 2.0: Towards a Cross-Embodiment Foundation Policy for Dexterous Grasping 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Dexterous grasping policies are usually trained for one arm paired with one hand. Every new pairing then needs its own policy, and the number of policies grows with every arm and hand added. We ask whether a single policy can control many arm-hand combinations and transfer to pairings and hands it has never seen. This is hard because robots differ in the number, layout, and dynamics of their joints, so their observations and actions share no common interface. We present DexFormer 2.0, a single reinforcement learning policy for cross-embodiment dexterous grasping. It maps every robot to a canonical joint interface with validity masks, encodes each joint's morphology with attention biased by the kinematic tree, and uses a Transformer-XL memory to infer embodiment-dependent dynamics from recent interaction. When Do Typed Decision Models Work in Transportation? Evidence from Travel Choice and Traffic Signal Control 2026-11-17
📝 22 pages, 4 figures
Show abstract

Typed decision models combine natural-language understanding with a structured interface that returns a selected action and an option-level probability distribution without generating lengthy textual responses. Their low inference cost and response latency make them attractive for transportation problems with predefined alternatives. This paper investigates their effectiveness, robustness, and efficiency in two representative transportation tasks: individual travel-mode choice prediction and sequential traffic signal control. We systematically evaluate TypeSafe Jev and GPT-6 Luna Decisions against conventional transportation methods, reinforcement learning algorithms, and four chat-based large language models. Experiments on the Swissmetro dataset show that typed decision models achieve lower prediction accuracy than traditional discrete choice models and the strongest chat-based baseline, while their performance varies substantially with input representation and question framing. In closed-loop traffic signal control experiments, typed decision models demonstrate competitive performance against established controllers in several scenarios, but exhibit performance degradation under more challenging network conditions. Furthermore, the typed models achieve lower inference latency and computational cost than the evaluated chat-based large language models, highlighting their potential for efficient, zero-shot transportation decision-making. Overall, these findings demonstrate both the potential and limitations of typed decision models, emphasizing the importance of input design, task characteristics, and robustness evaluation for their effective application in transportation systems.

Action-outcome coupling: a pre-investment feasibility audit for offline reinforcement learning in supply chain operations 2026-11-17
📝 40 pages
Show abstract

Prescriptive analytics initiatives in logistics, such as offline reinforcement learning and next-best-action systems, are often funded before anyone verifies that the available historical logs can support an improved decision policy. We formalise this pre-investment feasibility question via action-outcome coupling: a total-effect variance index measuring how much expected outcomes change with the logged action. Coupling provides an algorithm-independent upper bound on any policy's achievable value, expressed in monetary units per decision for both single-step and discounted sequential objectives. We also analyse the asymptotic false-licensing risk of an invalid workflow: fitting a value model and evaluating the model's greedy policy on the same data. The false-licence rate is driven mainly by the number of state cells over which selection is performed, rising from the nominal test level with one cell to near certainty with a few dozen, while overlap has a smaller effect. We then propose an audit that assigns logs to four tiers of decision support, separately for fixed and contextual policies, and returns an explicit stopping rationale. On a randomised e-commerce log, the audit endorses a fixed recommendation policy, certifying gains equal to 30 per cent of the theoretical ceiling; the remaining gap reflects bound looseness under an 80-action design. Two public logistics logs fail at different checkpoints: one because the target is an accounting identity of the action, and another because, with 27 clusters, estimated coupling stays below the design's detectability threshold even though a conservative offline learner certifies an improvement in 29 of 30 seeds.

Learning Agent-Centric Vision-Language-Action World Models for Autonomous Driving 2026-11-17
📝 Accepted by NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Recent progress in integrating vision-language-action (VLA) models with world modeling has significantly advanced end-to-end autonomous driving by allowing systems to reason about imagined futures instead of merely reacting to current observations. However, most world VLA models generate future frames at the pixel level with plenty of dense visual tokens. Although such generated futures can be visually plausible, they preserve many driving-irrelevant appearance details, i.e., only a small portion of the tokens directly encode critical planning-relevant factors, such as agent geometry, motion dynamics, and interactions. This introduces a significant performance bottleneck. An important question thus arises: what world state should a world VLA model reason over to support planning beyond pixel-level prediction? We argue that planning-oriented world modeling should focus on a compact and structured agent-centric latent state that captures the interactions between objects in the driving scene over time. As such, we introduce AgentWorld, an agent-centric VLA world model that uses discrete agent tokens jointly for reasoning and trajectory planning. Each token is dynamically grounded to a surrounding agent associated with its object's state, such as semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion, and interaction context. AgentWorld learns these tokens through three key processes: explicit geometric grounding, reasoning-oriented supervised fine-tuning, and agent-oriented reinforcement learning. Extensive open-loop and closed-loop experiments under diverse driving scenarios show that AgentWorld enhances planning capabilities, fully exploits visual evidence and agent dynamics, and yields interpretable agent-centric world states.

Outcome-Balanced PPO and Policy-Induced Flow Rectification for Flow-Based VLA Policies 2026-11-17
📝 15 pages
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) enables post-training of flow-based vision-language-action (VLA) policies by optimizing expected task return through environment interaction. However, this optimization raises two challenges: policy updates can alter the velocity-field geometry underlying the score approximation used for stochastic transitions, and sparse multi-task rollouts can yield uneven learning signals across tasks and advantage signs. We introduce an RL framework that combines Outcome-Balanced Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with policy-induced flow rectification. Outcome-Balanced PPO reweights positive advantages using task success-rate estimates and rescales positive and negative advantages while preserving their signs. Policy-induced rectification periodically fits a linear-bridge flow to ODE endpoint pairs generated by the current policy, refreshing the representation used in subsequent stochastic optimization. We evaluate the framework on simulated manipulation benchmarks and additionally study a component combination in a Real2Sim2Real task, examining task performance, optimization behavior, and flow consistency. The proposed framework improves success rates over the evaluated baselines across the simulated benchmarks.

WATERFALL: Workflow for Adaptive Training with Evolutionary Reward Formulation and Automated Learning Loops 2026-11-17
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (poster). 78 pages, 13 figures, 19 tables. Code: https://github.com/etriantafyllidis/waterfall OpenReview: https://openreview.net/forum?id=TI1lG64bTY
Show abstract

Reward design remains a central bottleneck in reinforcement learning (RL), particularly in sparse-reward, long-horizon and partially observable settings requiring dependency chaining, memory, and deceptive affordance disambiguation. While recent foundation-model approaches reduce manual reward engineering, most assume goal-conditioning, task descriptions, expert demonstrations, or ground-truth metrics. These assumptions are problematic in partially observable environments, where task-relevant objectives, affordances, and dynamics must be discovered through interaction rather than disclosed a priori. We formalise this problem setting by introducing WATERFALL, an iterative population-based workflow for automated reward discovery under the strict observability constraints inherent in POMDP environments. WATERFALL achieves this by (i) synthesising diverse programmatic reward candidates via persona-conditioned generation, (ii) evaluating candidate behaviour from raw visual rollouts utilising a Swiss-system evolutionary tournament judged by vision-language models to identify elites, and (iii) leveraging longitudinal assessment histories to iteratively mutate, escalate, or prune candidates. We evaluate WATERFALL across fully observable and reveal-gated partially observable environments ranging from discrete to continuous, comparing against sparse-reward RL, intrinsic-motivation and foundation-model reward-synthesis baselines. Our results indicate that WATERFALL discovers and refines reward programs without access to privileged information, outperforming baselines that retain their native information advantages, most clearly in the POMDP regime. Extensive ablations substantiate that visual evidence, tournament-based selection, and iterative refinement are jointly important. Enforcing this contract is what separates autonomous reward discovery from benchmark-privileged reward shaping.

Video-HopChain: Multi-Hop Questions and Confidence-Gated Exploration for Video Reasoning Models 2026-11-17
Show abstract

HopChain has shown on still images that multi-hop data synthesis improves vision-language reasoning, because long chain-of-thought reasoning exposes errors that compound across steps, while most data used for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) rarely demands a chain of visual evidence, so these weaknesses are likely to stay unexposed. We observe the same problem in video, where this framework has not yet been explored. We therefore build Video-HopChain, a dataset of 22,550 multi-hop video questions over 13,378 videos, together with a held-out benchmark of 1,000 questions. Each question chains three to six yes/no questions about moments in one video, and each yields one of two integers depending on its answer. The final answer is the sum of these integers, so an exact match on that sum gives the verifiable reward that RLVR needs. We first train Qwen3-VL-8B with GRPO on a standard video dataset, and a second stage on Video-HopChain then raises the mean over eight video understanding and reasoning benchmarks from 55.4 to 57.9 and improves every one of them. Training on such a dataset, however, exposes a known limitation of GRPO: its learning signal comes from the reward variance within a group, so hard questions whose rollouts are all incorrect and easy questions whose rollouts are all correct both leave the group with no gradient. To recover these groups at the same compute budget, we introduce Confidence-Gated Exploration (CGE). With 8 rollouts per question, CGE samples the first 4 as usual. If these 4 are either all correct or all incorrect, it samples the last 4 with the policy's most confident token masked inside the reasoning span, and removes the masked positions from the loss while all 8 rollouts enter the advantage. With CGE, the mean rises further to 59.3. We release the dataset, the checkpoint, and the data generation and training code.

Better Rankers, Worse Retrievers: The Hidden Cost of RL Post-Training in Generative Recommendation 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Generative recommenders decode the semantic ID of a user's next item with a language model, and post-training them with reinforcement learning (RL) after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has become standard practice. The gains are mostly reported on the first ten results, yet in deployment such models hand hundreds of candidates to a downstream ranker or reward model. We show that RL post-training has a hidden cost: it improves accuracy at the top of the list (hit@K for small K) but, on all five datasets we study, lowers the recall of the candidate list (hit@K for K in the hundreds). The model becomes a better ranker and a worse retriever. Controlled experiments trace the loss to the negative advantages that RL assigns to the candidates it samples, the rollout. The root cause is that only one next item is logged per user, so RL treats every other candidate as wrong. Yet these candidates are the model's most plausible alternatives, and often the next items of other users with similar histories. Penalising them pushes down exactly what similar users choose next, and these items drop out of those users' candidate lists. This diagnosis points directly to a remedy: out-of-rollout anchoring, a single additional loss term that preserves the SFT model's ordering of the items outside the rollout. It keeps the gain at the top and restores recall to the SFT level on all five datasets, and it is effective under other RL configurations as well. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of this recall loss: we measure it, identify its cause, and show that, once the cause is known, a simple remedy suffices.

Pixel is All You Need 2026-11-17
📝 20 pages, 6 figures, 7 tables, including references and appendices
Show abstract

Text, images, audio, touch, and even robot actions can, in principle, all be rendered onto a single canvas, suggesting an ambitious goal of unifying all modalities within vision. Language provides the natural first test. To subsume it, a visual model must reason directly over text in pixels. Across a wide range of image, video, and unified multimodal generators, however, we find that their visual generation modules cannot perform symbolic reasoning over text on the canvas. They succeed only when an underlying text module or prompt rewriter already supplies the answer, effectively reducing the generator to a mere renderer, a limitation that post-training does little to resolve. To test whether this failure stems from pixels themselves or from current training paradigms, we train a family of purely pixel-space generative models from scratch without any text pathway. With images as their sole input and output, these models read questions, write intermediate steps, and produce answers directly on the canvas. Remarkably, they exhibit hallmark behaviors of large language models. Performance improves predictably with scale following a power law. Writing intermediate steps frame by frame substantially outperforms single-frame answering, echoing chain-of-thought, while reinforcement-learning post-training further strengthens reasoning. Moreover, sharing one canvas allows the same model to reason across modalities, a capability that text-only language models lack. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of these phenomena in purely pixel-space generative models free of text tokens.

Seeing with You: Perception-Reasoning Coevolution for Multimodal Reasoning 2026-11-17
📝 21 pages, 15 figures, 6 tables
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has substantially enhanced the reasoning capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). However, existing RLVR approaches typically rely on outcome-driven optimization that updates both perception and reasoning using a shared reward based solely on the final answer. This shared reward blurs credit assignment, frequently improving reasoning patterns while failing to reliably enhance the accuracy of upstream visual evidence extraction. To address this perception bottleneck, we introduce PRCO (Perception-Reasoning Coevolution), a dual-role RLVR framework with a shared policy. PRCO consists of two cooperative roles: an Observer that generates an evidence caption tailored to the question and a Solver that predicts the final answer based on this caption. Crucially, PRCO employs role-specific reward signals: the Solver is optimized using verifiable outcome rewards on the final answer, while the Observer receives a utility reward derived from the Solver's downstream success. Extensive experiments across eight challenging multimodal reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that PRCO yields consistent improvements across model scales by over 7 points on average accuracy compared to the base model, outperforming prior open-source RL-tuned baselines.

TRAIL-IRL: Crossed Reward-Temperature Mixtures for Unlabeled Trajectory Clustering 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Different paths need not imply different preferences, trajectories also differ in how randomly actions are chosen. Many multi-reward inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) methods assume common action randomness and can confound it with preference heterogeneity. We introduce TRAIL-IRL, which clusters unlabeled trajectories using rewards for preferences and temperatures for action randomness. Its crossed mixture pairs each reward with a shared set of temperature levels, allowing action regularity to vary within preference groups. With centered and normalized rewards in a known finite-state environment, we establish the identifiability conditions under which the distribution of sufficiently long trajectories determines rewards, temperatures, mixing weights, and class counts up to relabeling. Under the same conditions and reward constraints, common-temperature reward mixtures with the same component count cannot reproduce a crossed mixture with multiple temperatures. We develop a generalized expectation-maximization algorithm and select class counts using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Simulations show improved predictive fit, reward recovery, and trajectory classification over common-temperature mixtures with the same component count. For 140 bird-year trajectories from 79 white storks, BIC favors four reward classes and three temperature levels over common-temperature candidates. Younger birds have higher posterior membership probabilities for a southwestward migration class, whereas older birds have higher probabilities for a northern retention class. Within reward classes, higher temperatures are associated with more variable movement directions and positions farther ahead along the migration route, consistent with social information use and collective sensing.

Multivariate Distributional Reinforcement Learning Using Sliced Divergences 2026-11-17
📝 Published at ICML 2026. 71 pages, 13 figures
Show abstract

Distributional reinforcement learning (DRL) models the full return distribution rather than expectations, but extending it to multivariate settings remains challenging. Many common metrics do not naturally generalize beyond one dimension or lose computational tractability, and the multivariate case introduces additional difficulties such as general matrix discounting, for which no contraction results are available. We introduce Sliced Distributional Reinforcement Learning (SDRL), which lifts tractable one-dimensional divergences to multivariate return distributions via projections. We prove Bellman contraction for uniform slicing under shared scalar discounting, and introduce a maximum-slicing variant with contraction under general dense discount matrices. SDRL supports a broad class of base divergences; we analyze Wasserstein, Cramér, and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), and characterize which SDRL variants suit the standard single-sample Bellman update used in distributional RL. We evaluate SDRL on a toy chain problem and a gridworld image-based environment as well as a subset of Atari games.

Learning Accurate End-Effector Trajectory Tracking with Inertial Feedback for a Quadruped Manipulator 2026-11-17
Show abstract

Accurate end-effector trajectory tracking on quadruped manipulators is challenging because uncertainties in actuators, foot-ground contact, and geometric errors are propagated through the leg-arm kinematic chain. Detecting such errors requires sensing beyond joint encoders that can capture the effects of these uncertainties on the robot's motion, since joint encoders only provide measurements at the actuated joints and cannot fully observe mechanical errors such as backlash, dimensional inaccuracies, or structural deformation. This work addresses these challenges using a reinforcement learning framework with end-effector (EE) inertial feedback, targeting local high-accuracy manipulation while maintaining a nominal body pose and support configuration without intentional body motion or foot repositioning. The learned policy uses EE inertial measurements to compensate for tracking errors induced by uncertainties throughout the leg-arm system that cannot be fully inferred from proprioception alone.
