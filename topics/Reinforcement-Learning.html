Internalizing Investment Mandates: A Neurosymbolic RL Approach to Portfolio Allocation 2026-11-12
📝 Accepted at ICAIF '26
Show abstract

Institutional portfolios operate under investment policy statements that specify asset allocation constraints, often conditioned on market regime information. Deep reinforcement learning has been shown to optimize financial performance but typically does not enforce rule compliance, making it unsuitable for real-world deployment. Existing neurosymbolic methods such as Probabilistic Logic Policy Gradient (PLPG) offer constraint-satisfying reinforcement learning (RL) via differentiable symbolic supervision but assume a finite, discrete action space. We present a PLPG-inspired continuous allocation method that estimates the probability that the policy's own executed action satisfies the investment mandate, using reparameterized Monte Carlo sampling, and adds it to the training objective. No post-hoc enforcement mechanism intercepts actions at inference time and compliance is learned by the agent. Audited directly on deterministic mean actions with no correction, the policy reduces violations of a six-rule regime-conditional mandate from 96-98% for unconstrained RL and 33.4% for a reward-penalty baseline to 4.8%, showing that a continuous-action neurosymbolic policy can substantially internalize a symbolic mandate.

Learning When Diffusion Language Models Should Regenerate 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Remasking allows diffusion language models to revisit generated text, but effective correction requires knowing when and what to revise. Our diagnosis on Sudoku reveals two gaps. Hidden states contain error information that token confidence does not reliably expose, and remasking an isolated error usually reproduces it when the surrounding context remains fixed. These findings motivate DiVR , a verifier that predicts whether a partially generated sequence will reach a correct answer. Trained on the final correctness of the model's own trajectories, DiVR decides when to regenerate a block and which alternative to retain, while the generator itself stays unchanged. On five reasoning benchmarks with LLaDA-8B-Base, DiVR raises the average accuracy of the fine-tuned generator from 35.3\% to 43.4\% at $1.9$-$3.5\times$ the cost of greedy decoding, above search baselines that use $3.8$-$7.4\times$. It also complements post-training such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, and generalizes to other generators, including Dream.

Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards 2026-11-12
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 (Spotlight)
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) typically relies on a verifier that can reliably judge whether sampled solutions are correct. However, in many realistic settings, complete verification is generally unavailable, while cheap checks can only falsify failures. We formalize this regime as one-sided verifiability (OSV): verified negatives are reliable counterexamples, but verified positives are merely non-falsified candidates and may contain many false positives. This asymmetry makes standard RLVR unreliable, because reinforcing verified positives can directly reward shortcuts that pass partial checks. To address this issue, we propose Negative-Only Policy Optimization (NOPO), which treats verified positives as unlabeled and updates the policy only by suppressing trusted verified negatives. Specifically, NOPO combines a reference-relative negative suppression loss with a group-level adapter that scales each update by the proportion of non-falsified responses. Across unit-test, constraint-check, and LLM self-verifiers, NOPO improves pass@k over baselines on code, logic, and math benchmarks when oracle rewards are unavailable. These gains persist under inference-time scaling and cross-benchmark transfer.

Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent's actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions. As a teacher, JEV raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy over GRPO from 72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. These results suggest that a poor actor can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents.

Learning Dynamics of the Influencer's Game in Resource Landscapes 2026-11-12
📝 23 pages 6 figures
Show abstract

Across domains as varied as ecology, economics, and online platforms, individuals are incentivized to position themselves within resource-rich strategy spaces. In this work we introduce a new spatial game framework called the influencer's game. Within this new framework players' strategies are represented as positions within a trait space. Players then receive a share of a portion of a resource based on their relative influence at that resource's points traits. For the influencer's game, we show that there is a unique symmetric Nash equilibrium that exists under strict log-concavity of the influence kernel. Using the unique equilibrium we show that the stability of the system depends on the influence kernel's parameters, number of players, and the resource distribution shape. For any dimension we demonstrate a stability threshold that depends on both the scatter and curvature of the players. When this threshold is crossed, the symmetric equilibrium destabilizes and gives way to a variety of bifurcation types; including, specialist clusters, limit cycles, and sub-games that reproduce the larger game's structure. We ground these results using real-world data for resources as a demonstration of the game's application. Finally we extend the game dynamics to use multi-agent reinforcement learning and further show that the predicted bifurcation structure persists under partial observability and discretized strategy spaces. In all, the influencer's game provides a novel framework for predicting when competitive pressure drives convergence versus fragmentation with possible applications across domains.

Support-Constrained Offline Policy Improvement for Pediatric ECMO from Limited Clinical Data 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Managing children on ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation) requires balancing short-term physiological stability with the long-term goal of preventing neurological injury. Standard reinforcement learning can propose risky, out-of-distribution actions, while imitation learning is limited to reproducing clinician behavior. We introduce Support-Constrained Fitted Q-Iteration (FQI-SC), an offline reinforcement learning framework that cautiously improves upon expert behavior by restricting optimization to empirically supported state-action pairs and defaulting to the clinician baseline in low-data regions. On 78 pediatric trajectories totaling over 6,000 hours of clinical data, we evaluate our framework using Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) and a behavioral disagreement. Additionally, we formalize the performance-safety trade-off by introducing a unified Balance Score ($S(π)$). Our empirical evaluation shows that support-constrained policy improves the Balance Score by 38\% relative to FQI, improves estimated value by 1.28 FQE units over imitation learning, and reduces disagreement with clinician actions by 71\% relative to FQI.

Convergence and stability of Q-learning in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning promises, among other benefits, to efficiently capture and utilize the temporal structure of a decision-making problem and to enhance transfer learning capabilities, but theoretical guarantees lag behind practice. In this paper, we propose a Feudal Q-learning scheme and investigate under which conditions its coupled updates converge and are stable. By leveraging the theory of Stochastic Approximation and the ODE method, we present a theorem stating the convergence and stability properties of Feudal Q-learning. This provides a principled convergence and stability analysis tailored to Feudal RL. Moreover, we show that the updates converge to a point that can be interpreted as an equilibrium of a suitably defined game, opening the door to game-theoretic approaches to Hierarchical RL. Lastly, experiments based on the Feudal Q-learning algorithm support the outcomes anticipated by theory.

From Delegation to Coordination: Learning Efficient Multi-Agent Deep Research 2026-11-12
📝 16 pages, 3 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

Multi-agent deep-research systems use delegation to parallelize search and keep long tool traces out of the main agent's context. Yet delegation helps only when task allocation and subtask execution are coordinated: the main agent must issue useful subtasks, while subagents must return reliable evidence. Existing training either optimizes only the main agent or broadcasts the same team advantage to all agents, which can reward misleading subagent reports and penalize useful ones. We introduce LEAD-RL (Learning Effective Agent Delegation with Reinforcement Learning), a joint main-agent-subagent training framework with contribution-aware credit assignment. A training-time rubric evaluates the research utility of each subagent report and gates its inherited team advantage, while final-answer correctness remains the only team reward; no token, step, or speedup reward is used. Across five benchmarks and two model families, LEAD-RL improves answer quality. On BrowseComp, it improves accuracy by 2.49 percentage points and reduces critical-path cost by 24.5% over joint training with shared team credit. During validation, accuracy increases by 6.78 points at approximately constant critical-path cost. These results show that contribution-aware credit assignment improves the quality-efficiency trade-off of multi-agent deep research.

Skill Adapter: Personalized Modification and Online Routing of General-Purpose Skills 2026-11-12
📝 9 pages, 3 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents use external skills to reuse task procedures and experience with tools. General-purpose skills describe shared execution procedures, while specific tool choices, operating practices, and output requirements vary across users. In long-horizon tasks, subgoals, available inputs, and environmental states change as execution progresses. Agents must select currently applicable skills from an expanding skill pool and prepare candidates for subsequent steps. We propose Skill Adapter, which enables personalized modification and online routing of general-purpose skills. The system distills local adjustments from user requirements and execution feedback and stores them as Adapters linked to general-purpose skills. The routing agent selects general-purpose skills and applicable Adapters based on the current subtask, execution progress, and environmental feedback, incrementally maintaining a task-level online skill library that the main LLM reads on demand. The system preloads candidates for subsequent nodes and rechecks their applicability once actual inputs are determined. Reinforcement learning optimizes the two types of decisions using modification gains and routing outcomes, respectively. We evaluate Skill Adapter on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SkillsBench. Compared with SkillRL, it improves task success rates by an average of 11.43 percentage points and reduces mean execution time on all three benchmarks. Skill Adapter retains its performance advantage when the skill pool grows to 1,000 skills.

The Value of Thinking: Learning When to Reason in Hybrid Decision Models 2026-11-12
📝 20 pages, 3 figures
Show abstract

Existing adaptive-reasoning methods shorten the explicit reasoning a model writes, or skip it by switching modes; either way the answer is still written out token by token. \emph{Rather than deciding how much to think}, we explore a different paradigm, \emph{zero-or-$T$ thinking}: a single model either answers from the implicit thinking inside one forward pass, generating no token, or performs full autoregressive reasoning at a cost of $T$ tokens when that is worth paying. We realise it in a \emph{hybrid decision model} (HDM), where one frozen backbone serves both modes and a lightweight \emph{self-routing block} chooses between them. The block estimates the \emph{value of thinking}, the utility the slow mode is expected to add over the fast one, from what that implicit thinking has already produced, and the slow mode runs only where that value exceeds a price $λ$ set per request. Where no per-decision label exists, as in episodic agentic tasks, we introduce \emph{Calibrated Decision Policy Optimisation} (CDPO), a reinforcement-learning recipe that trains the block from episode-level returns. We evaluate HDM on MMLU-Pro, AGIEval, GPQA-Diamond, JevBench, BANKING77 and MiniWoB. Specifically, on JevBench HDM-4B scores $0.857$ against Jev's $0.851$ ($+0.7\%$) at a median $0.179$\,s against $0.652$ ($-72.5\%$). On the agentic venue MiniWoB HDM-27B cuts token cost by $80.1\%$.

CADET: Construction of PCB Routing Instances with Executable Demonstrations 2026-11-12
📝 28 pages, 10 figures, 15 tables. Code and data: https://github.com/LGAI-Research/CADET
Show abstract

Printed circuit board (PCB) routing is the stage of physical layout that finds paths connecting the placed pads of each net. Public datasets in this domain are few, and most contain only the final states of routed boards, not demonstrations of the electronic design automation (EDA) engine actions that produce them. To close this gap, we propose CADET (Constructing executAble Demonstrations for Engine-grounded Training), which jointly generates synthetic boards and demonstrations built from EDA engine actions. The resulting RouteDemos provides 100,000 synthetic boards routed with zero design rule violations (DRVs), together with their demonstrations. Their distributions over 17 statistics resemble those of 142 real reference boards. On real test boards, the policy raised the rate of complete DRV-free routing from 38.0% to 67.2% over an RL-only baseline. This rate was comparable to that of the open-source autorouter FreeRouting and exceeded those of the other baselines, including GPT-6 Astra. We release the dataset and its generation code as open source at https://github.com/LGAI-Research/CADET.

Achieving strong optimization generalization across diverse optimization problems while requiring limited training resources remains a challenging problem for optimization-oriented large language models (LLMs). Existing learning-based methods, however, typically require large-scale supervised data, expensive intermediate step verification, and extensive computational resources, while exhibiting limited optimization generalization across diverse optimization structures. Outcome-Mass Alignment: Controlling Length-Dependent Trajectory Influence in Multi-Turn LLM Agent Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-12
📝 18 pages, 6 figures, including appendices
Show abstract

To keep long interaction contexts tractable, stepwise group-relative training for multi-turn LLM agents materializes each source trajectory into multiple response rows and valid tokens. This expansion creates an outer-accounting problem distinct from within-trajectory credit assignment: row-based outcome statistics can count one episode outcome multiple times, while token-level reduction can assign unequal aggregate objective mass across trajectories. We term these effects outcome-statistics multiplicity and objective-mass imbalance. We introduce Outcome-Mass Alignment (OMA), a two-component method that makes both accounting choices explicit. Outcome Alignment computes outcome statistics over unique source trajectories, while Mass Alignment controls aggregate objective mass after within-trajectory aggregation of optimizer-native token objectives. Neither redesigns the base optimizer's credit assignment, importance ratios, or clipping. Across matched ALFWorld GRPO comparisons at two model scales, where failed trajectories typically expand much more than successful ones, OMA improves matched-horizon endpoint success by 7.0-9.4 percentage points. Ordinary WebShop exhibits weak or reversed expansion-outcome coupling and correspondingly mixed effects, whereas in a success-oriented WebShop setting, WebShop-SOL, OMA improves success nAUC from 0.399 to 0.511. Component ablations and GiGPO and GSPO comparisons further support component separability and cross-optimizer compatibility. These results show that trajectory expansion always defines an implicit weighting rule, but its practical effect depends on how expansion couples to outcomes and optimizer-native updates.

Reward Inflation: A Healthy Stimulus for Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-12
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Reward serves as the primary learning signal in reinforcement learning (RL). However, while reward magnitudes are typically held fixed throughout training, their temporal modulation remains underexplored. In this paper, we propose reward inflation, a gradual scaling of rewards over the course of training, and show that it can act as a healthy stimulus for RL. Theoretically, reward inflation induces an implicit recency weighting that upweights recent transitions during policy updates, enabling faster adaptation. We further show that reward inflation suppresses the emergence of dormant neurons by sustaining gradient signals as the policy saturates, suggesting a possible contribution to plasticity preservation. Empirical results on ALE games and MuJoCo tasks corroborate these findings, showing that an appropriate level of reward inflation benefits a broad range of tasks. Finally, we introduce Fed, an adaptive variant that adjusts the inflation level on the fly, and find that it often improves upon fixed inflation. Source code is available at https://github.com/northadventure/RewardInflation.

TEMPO: Semantic-Action Decoupled RL Post-Training for Vision-Language-Action Models 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are commonly adapted to downstream manipulation tasks via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or online reinforcement learning(RL) post-training. SFT is prone to distribution mismatch, and existing RL approaches typically optimize all trainable components within a single RL loop at a uniform update rate, although these components play distinct functional roles. We propose TEMPO, a semantic-action decoupled, two-timescale RL post-training framework for VLA models. TEMPO freezes the pretrained vision-language backbone and adapts two components with dedicated RL loops: the semantic projection layer, which produces the latent action conditioning the action expert, and the low-level action expert itself. The semantic projection layer is updated infrequently and the action expert frequently, which aims to limit latent-action drift while allowing the action expert to learn from online feedback. Our analyses show that, compared with equal update frequencies, less frequent semantic updates reduce latent-action drift and improve policy performance. Experiments on the CALVIN benchmark and real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate that TEMPO outperforms both pretrained state-of-the-art VLA models and the RL post-training baseline, while reaching and maintaining higher evaluation rewards on three real-world tasks.

Optimising Neural Speech Codecs for 300bps Communication using Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-12
📝 Accepted at the 2026 IEEE Spoken Language Technology Workshop (SLT 2026). Code and pretrained checkpoints: https://github.com/demo941/ClariCodec
Show abstract

In bandwidth-constrained communication such as satellite and underwater channels, speech must often be transmitted at ultra-low bitrates where intelligibility is the primary objective. At such extreme compression levels, codecs trained with acoustic reconstruction losses tend to allocate bits to perceptual detail, leading to substantial degradation in word error rate (WER). This paper proposes ClariCodec, a neural speech codec operating at 300 bits per second (bps) that reformulates quantisation as a stochastic policy, enabling reinforcement learning (RL)-based optimisation of intelligibility. Specifically, the encoder is fine-tuned using WER-driven rewards while the acoustic reconstruction pipeline remains frozen. Even without RL, ClariCodec achieves 4.64% WER on the LibriSpeech test-clean set at 300 bps, already competitive with codecs operating at higher bitrates. Further RL fine-tuning reduces WER to 3.55% on test-clean, corresponding to a 23.5% relative reduction while preserving perceptual quality. In addition, we adapt ClariCodec to a streaming configuration and show that the proposed RL-based optimisation remains effective under streaming constraints, achieving 4.53% WER on test-clean with a theoretical latency of 374 ms.

Safety as a Budget: Calibrating the Control Channel of Constrained GRPO for Vision-Language Models 2026-11-12
📝 10 pages, 5 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning can teach vision-language models (VLMs) to decline harmful image-text requests, but how safe the result becomes is set indirectly, through reward weights found by trial runs. Stating safety as budgets on the rate of harmful assistance and the rate of over-refusal, enforced by Lagrange multipliers inside GRPO with an off-the-shelf scorer as the cost, is the natural fix, and it fails twice. The multiplier compares the scorer's mean with the budget, and scorers built for ranking get the mean wrong: a fixed-point analysis shows that the mean bias, not the budget, then decides whether the multiplier settles, saturates or switches off. And standard GRPO standardizes the penalized reward within each group, which cancels the multiplier before it reaches the gradient. Two-channel constrained GRPO repairs both: the base reward keeps the raw score for the within-group comparison, the penalty and the constraint estimate pass through a monotone map fitted on judge-labeled training responses, and the advantage keeps the multiplier. On a Qwen3-VL-2B policy with a training-free readout of an open 4B model as the scorer, held-out harm on 1,037 adversarial prompts falls from 8.9% under standard GRPO to 4.8%, lower in every seed, where the uncalibrated budget gives 7.6%; over-refusal stays at most 2% on 400 benign queries and MMBench is unchanged. The gain is largest where the intent sits in the image (10.1% -> 5.1%) and grows on the unseen HADES benchmark (28.9% -> 12.8%). Order-preserving distortions of the scores reproduce each predicted controller state in every seed, and the results hold under a map fitted on another judge's labels, an image-reading judge and a human audit.

We present Jais 3, a fleet of six open, Arabic-centric agentic language models ranging from 0.9B to 375B parameters. Multi-agent reinforcement learning has shown great promise for traffic signal control. However, most existing methods rely on two key assumptions: (i) agents are assumed to be homogeneous with unified observation/action spaces; (ii) full and reliable lane-level traffic observations are available to all agents. ART for Diffusion Sampling: A Reinforcement Learning Approach to Timestep Scheduling 2026-11-12
📝 12 pages. Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

We consider time discretization for score-based diffusion models to generate samples from a learned reverse-time dynamic on a finite grid. Uniform and hand-crafted grids can be suboptimal given a budget on the number of time steps. We introduce Adaptive Reparameterized Time (ART), which controls the clock speed of a reparameterized time variable to redistribute computation along the sampling trajectory while preserving the terminal time, with the objective of minimizing the aggregate Euler discretization error. We derive a randomized companion ART-RL that recasts ART as a continuous-time reinforcement learning problem with Gaussian policies, and prove a two-directional bridge between the two: the deterministic ART optimum lifts to an optimal Gaussian policy, and conversely any optimal Gaussian policy must recover the ART control through its mean. This bridge turns continuous-time actor--critic learning into a principled, rather than heuristic, route to the deterministic timestep optimum. Within the official EDM pipeline, ART-RL improves FID on CIFAR--10 across a wide range of budgets; after one-time offline training, the distilled deterministic schedule transfers without retraining to AFHQv2, FFHQ, and ImageNet at no extra inference cost.

When Can Learned Uncertainty Be Trusted? An Audit of Distributional Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-12
📝 25 pages, 8 figures, 3 tables (main text); includes supplementary material
Show abstract

Many learned predictive models represent their uncertainty as a probability distribution, and planners and policies use this distribution to judge risk. However, the fact that a model outputs a distribution does not tell us whether that distribution captures the uncertainty in the environment which is relevant for a decision. In this work we study this question in distributional reinforcement learning, where the learned return distribution P_θ(Z | s,a) can be compared directly with an environment-grounded distribution P_env(Z | s,a) estimated by snapshot-restart Monte Carlo. Our audit first screens for the claims that a risk-sensitive user would act upon, using the excess Wasserstein gap between the top two actions (which is equal to the mass by which first-order stochastic dominance is violated). Each claim is then tested against ground truth using permutation nulls, bootstrap refutation and FDR control. Across QR-DQN, C51 and IQN on MinAtar (33 runs), 40-95% of the strongest claimed risk trade-offs are refuted at 95% confidence, their placement is statistically indistinguishable from a truth-blind placement, and almost none of them can be confirmed. A similar pattern is seen for a pretrained full-Atari QR-DQN. On positive controls of known magnitude the same audit confirms 96-100% of real claims (correlation 0.89-0.92), so it is able to detect real risk when it is present. Acting on the learned CVaR advice at the most-flagged states gives results ranging from beneficial to significantly worse than chance. Ensembling, risk-sensitive training and recalibration do not fix the problem, and the recalibration experiment indicates that the learned spread has no usable information about the true risk. Based on these results, we suggest that uncertainty estimates, including those of learned world models, should be checked against realized outcomes at the decisions where they are used..

Show abstract

We study constrained bilevel reinforcement learning in which the lower-level problem is a constrained Markov decision process with a generally parameterized policy. The lower-level solution map can be set-valued and need not be Lipschitz continuous in the upper-level variable, so standard hypergradient analyses do not apply. We therefore define the upper-level objective as the expected upper-level loss of the policy returned by a fixed stochastic lower-level algorithm, which we call the approximate response. This objective is well defined without uniqueness of the lower-level solution. To compute its gradient, we randomize the reward and constraint values used during lower-level training without changing their means, so that the upper-level variable affects training only through known probabilities. Disk-resident graph indexes such as DiskANN store each vector with its graph edges as one record on SSD pages. A search needs the page of every record it expands, so its page reads depend on the page layout, that is, which records share a page. Search agents trained with reinforcement learning query one such index run after run, and every graph traversal can be logged with the records it expands. We therefore use the logs of a finished run to lay out the index for a declared memory budget, so later runs read fewer pages. In contrast, existing layouts such as Starling and MARGO are computed from the graph and vectors and use no logs. Learning a layout from logs is challenging, because the logs must stay valid under every layout, the saving depends on what the budget caches, and the best layout is NP-hard to find. It then selects one layout, together with the budget split between hot records and a page cache, by replaying validation steps. Reinforcement learning in real-world systems often involves delayed feedback, which violates the Markov assumption and impedes both learning and control. Canonical augmentation-based approaches address this issue by augmenting the state with action histories, but suffer from state-space explosion and the resulting sample-complexity burden. Despite recent progress, state-of-the-art augmentation-based methods either primarily alleviate this burden for the critic or employ separate mechanisms for the actor and critic. To address this limitation, we propose delayed homomorphic reinforcement learning (DHRL), a framework grounded in MDP homomorphisms that introduces a belief-equivalence relation over the augmented state space to collapse control-redundant augmented states into a compact abstract state space. This framework yields exact abstraction under deterministic dynamics and approximate abstraction under stochastic dynamics, providing a unified abstraction mechanism that benefits both the actor and critic. In finite domains, exact abstraction preserves optimality and recovers the delay-free sample-complexity order, while approximate abstraction admits a bound on the value loss of the resulting policy. For continuous domains, we introduce deep delayed homomorphic policy gradient (D$^2$HPG), a deep actor-critic instantiation of the DHRL framework. Experiments on MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks demonstrate that D$^2$HPG outperforms strong augmentation-based baselines, especially in long-delay regimes. Is the Importance Ratio Necessary for Stable Reinforcement Learning in LLMs? 2026-11-11

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become central to post-training large language models (LLMs). However, popular RL methods like GRPO incur non-negligible overhead by computing both old-policy and current-policy likelihoods to form importance sampling ratios. In this work, we propose Likelihood-Gated Policy Optimization (LGPO), which enforces a soft trust region constraint via likelihood-based gating, eliminating the need to compute old-policy likelihoods. Empirically, we show that removing the importance sampling correction term does not harm training stability, whereas removing the trust region mechanism can lead to collapse. Moreover, ratio-based clipping can fail in fully on-policy training: the importance ratio stays at 1, so the ratio-based trust region constraint never activates. Under standard training settings where GRPO is stable, LGPO achieves comparable training stability and peak performance while reducing training time by ~18% on average relative to a standard implementation that recomputes old-policy likelihoods. In fully on-policy training, where GRPO fails, LGPO remains stable, enabling more efficient and robust LLM RL post-training across training regimes.
