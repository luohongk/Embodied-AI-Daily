RobustRLlib: A Unified Library and Benchmark for Robust Reinforcement Learning Algorithms 2026-10-23
📝 Project page, code, and tutorial: https://robustrl-lib.github.io/
Show abstract

Robust reinforcement learning (RL) is critical for reliable real-world deployment, where policies may fail under diverse shifts and disruptions. Existing robust RL algorithms offer a rich set of mechanisms and principles, yet two major barriers limit their broader potential: limited algorithm reusability and an unclear empirical capability boundary. We introduce RobustRLlib, an algorithm-centric library and benchmark that addresses both challenges. It integrates 17 representative robust online, offline, and safe RL algorithms under a unified interface for substantially improved reusability, together with an enhanced evaluation platform spanning diverse real-world shifts and advanced humanoid and vision-language-action tasks. Systematic evaluation over 273 perturbation configurations provides an up-to-date capability map of current robust RL algorithms, revealing where they remain effective and where substantial robustness gaps persist. As an open-source, user-friendly platform, RobustRLlib offers a reproducible and extensible codebase for applying existing methods to new problems and developing new robust RL algorithms.

RynnUnify: Towards Unified Embodied Brain 2026-10-23
Show abstract

We introduce RynnUnify, a 16B unified embodied model that combines strong embodied understanding with long-horizon visual planning through interleaved video--language reasoning. With capabilities spanning spatial reasoning, physical prediction, and grounding, RynnUnify connects an understanding of the physical world with visual anticipation of how it evolves across successive subtasks. Building on these capabilities, we further develop an agentic extension through reinforcement learning to selectively use video predictions for reasoning about unobserved states and interaction outcomes. We also develop a world-action extension of the full RynnUnify model, translating its understanding and prediction capabilities into robot control. To evaluate these capabilities, we introduce Rynn-VP and Rynn-GEU for long-horizon visual planning and anticipatory reasoning. Experiments show that RynnUnify achieves the highest average embodied understanding score among the evaluated unified multimodal models and improves the Rynn-VP overall score by 9.75 points over Cosmos 3 Nano. Its agentic extension improves the Rynn-GEU average by 6.0 points over direct reasoning, while the world-action extension achieves 51.5\% success on 20 selected composite RoboCasa365 tasks and 46.7\% average success across three real-robot tasks. Together, these results establish RynnUnify as a shared foundation for embodied understanding, visual planning, and robot control, taking a step toward a unified embodied brain.

Where Rounding Happens Matters: Rounding-Location Alignment Stabilizes LLM Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning for large language models often uses separate systems for inference and training. Even with identical weights and BF16 tensors, implementation differences can make an intended on-policy update off-policy and destabilize training. Existing remedies align the entire inference and training stacks, change global precision, or modify the learning algorithm. We ask whether aligning selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while other implementation differences remain. We align training-side rounding locations with inference in three regions---RoPE, SwiGLU, and residual RMSNorm---by retaining selected intermediates in FP32 until their BF16 outputs. Across four Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in the studied runs, despite remaining numerical discrepancies. The interventions keep the inference engine, matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective unchanged.

Recommender regret for improving regularised RL estimation with bandits and MCTS 2026-10-23
📝 10 pages, plus appendices for proofs and additional information
Show abstract

Regularised reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms rely on estimators, like Monte Carlo tree search or one-step look-ahead, to provide policy, value, or Q-value estimates in training and inference. However, existing bandit-based estimation frameworks evaluate sampling efficiency, not the quality of estimates. We address this by formalising estimate generation as a recommendation process, treating bandit-based sampling as an improvement operator. To evaluate the quality of a recommendation (estimate), we define regulariser-aware recommender regrets, analysing their lower bounds, and proving that simple algorithms like uniform sampling can achieve these bounds. Finally, we unify bandit- and search-based target improvement under recommender MCTS. Across synthetic trees, arcade games, and scientific discovery tasks, recommenders often yield faster target convergence and superior downstream performance. Our experiments characterise for the first time the potential of regularised MCTS in scientific applications.

HoloBoxing: A Simple Recipe for Humanoid Boxing with 7 Minutes of Single-GPU Training 2026-10-23
Show abstract

HoloBoxing presents a simple recipe for learning humanoid boxing by building on a pretrained whole-body controller. The recipe separates reusable motor skills from task decisions: a compact planner requests future body-point targets, while the frozen controller handles joint-level execution. A learned decoder maps 32-dimensional actions to structured target windows, and measured-state imitation initializes the planner before reinforcement learning. Task learning then refines interaction behavior while reusing the decoder and controller, avoiding the need to relearn whole-body control. This combination keeps task optimization focused on a compact upper policy and supplies an initial behavior before reward-driven exploration. Starting from that available controller, the recipe accounts for about seven minutes of additional training, including reused representation fitting and a single-GPU task run. This excludes controller pretraining, data preparation, evaluation, and search. Simulated boxing experiments and component controls evaluate the resulting behavior and the roles of imitation initialization and the target representation.

Reinforcement Learning with Multi-Step Transition Predictions: A Bellman Theory 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Multi-step predictions of future transition realizations can improve reinforcement learning, but their value remains difficult to quantify. Directly including these predictions in the state can lead to exponential state-space growth, making direct dynamic programming computationally expensive. We study discounted finite MDPs under a block-feedback protocol: at the start of each K-period block, the decision maker observes $K$-step probabilistic transition predictions for a subset of actions and adapts decisions to observed states within the block. We show that the optimal value satisfies a Bayesian Bellman equation on the original state space. This representation reveals that predictive information creates value by recursively changing the local order of maximization and expectation in the Bellman expansion. We formalize this effect through a Bellman--Jensen analysis and quantify the effects of prediction horizon, error, action coverage, and within-block feedback. We propose block-feedback Bayesian offline learning with online adaptation (BF--BOLA), a model-based algorithm for learning near-optimal policies, and establish upper and lower sample-complexity bounds that match up to logarithmic factors in the stated regimes. The results show that predictions can reduce transition learning to uncovered rows and that learning a near-optimal policy can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the Bayesian value function.

NgramRL: Accelerating RL Post-Training for Structured Generation via Rollout-Aware N-gram Speculative Decoding 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models, but its cost is dominated by autoregressive rollout generation, which can exceed 70% of training time. Existing speculative-decoding methods are poorly suited to this setting: model-based drafters consume scarce GPU memory and may become misaligned as the policy evolves, while existing model-free methods do not explicitly exploit repetition across training epochs. We observe that recent rollouts of the same prompt provide a high-quality, self-updating draft source. Based on this insight, we propose NgramRL, a per-prompt n-gram drafter constructed from bounded recent rollout history. NgramRL requires no auxiliary model, drafter training, or persistent GPU-resident drafter state, while target-model verification preserves the rollout distribution. To hide CPU cache lookup and scheduling, we further introduce an adaptive fan-out prefetcher that predicts likely verification outcomes (j, b), consisting of the accepted draft length j and target-emitted continuation token b. A causal budget allocator concentrates prefetches on high-probability outcomes, while uncovered outcomes fall back to sequential lookup without changing committed tokens. Across two model families and three reasoning and structured-generation workloads, NgramRL commits 3.60-7.77 tokens per target-model forward in the warm-cache regime, up to 1.99x more than SuffixDecoding and 7.0x more than prompt-lookup decoding. On Spider, the prefetcher covers 96.6% of Qwen3-8B outcomes at a candidate budget of 16 and improves measured rollout throughput by 1.38x over synchronous NgramRL, approaching its 1.40x overlap ceiling.

No Eyes, No Problem: What Does Touch Contribute to Blind Baoding Ball Manipulation? 2026-10-23
📝 9 Pages, 8 Figures
Show abstract

Humans manipulate objects within their hands through touch alone with remarkable ease, but for robots, achieving sustained real-world manipulation without vision remains an open challenge. Although reinforcement learning (RL) can generate sophisticated blind manipulation policies in simulation, transferring them to a real dexterous hand remains hindered by continuously changing contacts, motion-induced tactile information drift, and the gap between simulated and physical dynamics. We study this problem through Baoding ball rotation on the Shadow Dexterous Hand Lite, using only proprioception and binary tactile feedback as observations. We achieve successful zero-shot transfer through a combination of a sensor-aligned, coupling-aware embodiment in simulation, tactile calibration, and two novel episodic domain randomisations: slew-rate action limits and STuck-AT Taxels. On the physical hand, the policy achieves sustained blind Baoding rotation, completing $\textbf{225 consecutive $\mathbf{180^\circ}$ rotations (112.5 full rotations) at $\sim0.36 \;\text{rot/s}$}$ in a single continuous trial without any vision or object-state information. Furthermore, we evaluate the contribution of tactile sensing relative to proprioception alone and identify the hand regions where tactile information is most important. This is the strongest reported hardware result for Baoding ball manipulation in both rotation count and speed, irrespective of the sensing modality used. By effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap, our framework sets a new benchmark for sustained tactile-driven dexterous manipulation on physical hardware.

Regularised RL improves sim-to-real transfer 2026-10-23

Show abstract

Regularised reinforcement learning (RL) has provable robustness guarantees that should ease the transfer of policies from simulation to robots, known as sim-to-real. Yet, weakly regularised algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) remain the de facto choice in sim-to-real pipelines, while the practical benefits of regularisation for transfer remain poorly characterised. In this work, we study the sim-to-real performance of commonly used RL algorithms, with the aim of characterising the effects of regularisation on robotic performance. To that end, we derive Soft MellowMax Actor-Critic (SMM-AC), a novel regularised RL algorithm based on the soft mellowmax value function, which carries strong robustness guarantees. On MuJoCo continuous control benchmarks, SMM-AC is competitive with baselines like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). On real robots, SMM-AC achieves higher return, while exhibiting greater robustness to perturbations in observations, the environment, and dynamics than baselines. Together, these results demonstrate that regularised RL improves real-world policy deployment through better robustness to the sim-to-real gap, and changing real-world conditions.

The rapid progress of reasoning and agentic large language models (LLMs) has increased the demand for long-context inference, but self-attention (SA) scales quadratically with context length. To address this, we study SWARR (Sliding-Window Attention with Reinforced Adaptation for Math Reasoning), a practical recipe for adapting SWA models to mathematical reasoning. SWARR has two stages: (1) efficient conversion from a pretrained SA model to SWA with supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which avoids pretraining a new base model, and (2) policy adaptation with reinforcement learning (RL). We find that SWA still underperforms SA after SFT, and we hypothesize that this gap is caused in part by a data-architecture mismatch: most SFT data are prepared for SA models and may contain long-range dependencies that are difficult for SWA to model. Because on-policy RL optimizes self-generated trajectories under the SWA constraint, it can adapt trajectories to better match SWA. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that this recipe substantially narrows the gap between SWA and SA, recovering much of the accuracy lost during SWA conversion while preserving the efficiency benefits of linear-complexity attention. Our central contribution is the empirical finding that RL changes the conclusion one would draw from conversion and SFT alone about SWA's viability for math reasoning.

DT-JEPA: Learning to Predict Future Representations for Decision-Making 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Decision Transformer (DT) has demonstrated strong performance in various offline reinforcement learning (RL) tasks by conditioning action generation on expected future returns and historical trajectories. However, treating expected future returns as the condition provides an incomplete description of the future, as trajectories with different state transitions and behaviors may share similar returns. Moreover, expected future returns inherently depend on task-specific reward functions, which makes policy learning reliant on reward-labeled datasets and restricts its scalability to tasks with similar but unseen rewards. Recently, Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) have emerged as a promising paradigm of self-supervised learning-based world models, which learn predictive representations of future dynamics in latent space rather than reconstructing future states in the original space. Inspired by JEPA, we propose DT-JEPA, an offline pretraining and online fine-tuning framework that learns general knowledge from reward-free offline trajectories and achieves excellent adaptation to unseen tasks. Instead of predicting a single deterministic future embedding, we design a novel probabilistic predictor to model the conditional distribution of future representations in the latent space, thereby capturing both predictive information and uncertainty about future dynamics. Theoretically, we show that 1) the JEPA objective seeks to maximize a variational lower bound on the mutual information between historical representations and future trajectories; and 2) predictive future-conditioned policy learning admits a finite-sample performance bound with an O(N-1/4) estimation term. Experiments on D4RL benchmarks demonstrate that DT-JEPA achieves efficient adaptation across varying offline RL tasks, outperforming traditional DT-based and other offline RL methods.

POETS: Uncertainty-Aware LLM Optimization via Compute-Efficient Policy Ensembles 2026-10-23
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Balancing exploration with exploitation is a core challenge in black-box optimization and reinforcement learning. We introduce POETS ($\textbf{Po}$licy $\textbf{E}$nsembles for $\textbf{T}$hompson $\textbf{S}$ampling), a novel framework that bridges uncertainty quantification and policy optimization. Our approach is grounded in the insight that policies trained with Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization implicitly encode an underlying reward function. Building on this, POETS bypasses the complex, nested process of training an uncertainty-aware reward model and separately fitting a policy to this model. Instead, we directly train a policy ensemble to capture epistemic uncertainty by matching implicitly encoded reward functions to online, bootstrapped data. To overcome the prohibitive compute and memory constraints of ensembling Large Language Models (LLMs), POETS utilizes an efficient architecture: the ensemble shares a pre-trained backbone while maintaining diversity through independent Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) branches. Theoretically, we show that POETS implicitly approximates KL-regularized Thompson sampling, a principled optimization strategy with optimal cumulative regret bounds of $O(\sqrt{T γ_T})$. Empirically, we demonstrate that POETS achieves state-of-the-art sample efficiency across diverse scientific discovery domains, including protein search, therapeutic molecular design, and quantum circuit design. POETS also improves the optimization trajectory and out-of-distribution transfer of reinforcement learning across mathematical reasoning and code generation tasks, proving particularly robust in off-policy settings with experience replay or in small dataset regimes.

Revisiting Temporal Regularization for Smooth Control in Deep Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-23
📝 Submitted to ICRA 2027. Project page: https://dmach1ne.github.io/CATS/
Show abstract

Deep Reinforcement Learning policies can produce nonsmooth action oscillations that hinder deployment on physical robots. Existing architectural and penalty-based approaches seek spatial smoothness by directly reducing sensitivity to changes in state inputs, but their broad constraints can degrade task performance as stronger smoothing is pursued. Temporal regularization instead constrains action differences along observed transitions, but has been considered unable to provide the spatial smoothness needed under observation noise. We revisit this assumption by proving that the temporal penalty bounds the expected action differences between current states sharing a next state, revealing a spatial effect that empirically extends to spatial smoothness. Building on this finding, we propose Conditioning for Action using only Temporal Smoothness (CATS), which combines a temporal penalty with linear ramp-up. We highlight temporal regularization's ability to provide spatial smoothness while better preserving task performance than explicit spatial regularization. Through linear ramp-up, CATS allows the policy to learn rewarding behavior before progressively smoothing its actions, improving return preservation and both temporal and spatial smoothness. Experiments in both simulation and the real world show that CATS substantially reduces action oscillation without degrading task performance, with little computational overhead.

Show abstract

Failure of applied Reinforcement Learning (RL) to solve a given problem predominantly stems from unaddressed specification gaps, where the intent of human experts in the problem's domain is inaccurately or incompletely translated by AI into mathematical objectives, constraints, and reward functions. Failure can also stem from unaddressed performance gaps, where human domain experts are unable to determine the validity of the RL based solution or where parameters (i.e., hyperparameters or weights on the reward terms comprising the reward) are poorly chosen. Chat Assisted Reinforcement Learning (CARL) bridges these gaps through a human-AI co-design interface architecture that enforces interactive specification prior to code generation and then enables subsequent verification and parameter alterations via iteration. More specifically, CARL leads domain experts through a structured ten-step workflow for each iteration using step-specific skill files, domain-aware suggestions via an assistant persona (called Trillian), and compact decision summaries that maintain context across iterations without context overload. Once the domain expert confirms the Markov decision process (MDP) specified by this procedure, CARL's autonomous tool-use loop generates, tests, executes, and debugs an end-to-end RL training pipeline, followed by an interactive post-training diagnosis and revisit step that allows for verification and parameter alteration before running a new iteration if desired.

When to Stop Penalizing Actions? Martingale GRPO for VLA Reinforcement Learning 2026-10-23
📝 31 pages, 9 figures
Show abstract

A failed vision-language-action (VLA) rollout does not imply that every action contributed to failure. Yet in group-relative policy optimization (GRPO), a single trajectory-level advantage is assigned to all actions in a rollout. Potentially useful recovery attempts can thus be penalized alongside the errors they aim to correct. Ideal stopping conditions for failure feedback are characterized using success-probability and policy-score martingales. At these stops, the removed gradient contribution has zero conditional expectation given the retained information. Truncation therefore preserves the expectation of a reference GRPO group-gradient estimator and can reduce its covariance. Partial observability is a notorious challenge in reinforcement learning (RL), due to the need to learn complex, history-dependent policies. Recent empirical successes have used privileged expert distillation--which leverages availability of latent state information during training (e.g., from a simulator) to learn and imitate the optimal latent, Markovian policy--to disentangle the task of "learning to see" from "learning to act". While expert distillation is more computationally efficient than RL without latent state information, it also has well-documented failure modes. In this paper--through a simple but instructive theoretical model called the perturbed Block MDP, and controlled experiments on challenging simulated locomotion tasks--we investigate the algorithmic trade-off between privileged expert distillation and standard RL without privileged information. Our results suggest new guidelines for effectively exploiting privileged information, potentially advancing the efficiency of policy learning across many practical partially observable domains.
