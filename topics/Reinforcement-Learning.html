DiaCap: Jointly Learning Who Speaks When for Audiovisual Dialogue Captioning 2026-11-24
📝 18 pages, 13 figures, 12 tables
Show abstract

We present DiaCap, an audiovisual LLM for comprehensive dialogue captioning, which produces the transcript, timestamps, and a visual description of the speaker for every utterance in a video. Considering that utterance timing and audiovisual diarization are tightly coupled, DiaCap unifies the tasks in a single model, trained in two complementary stages. First, we train DiaCap on 225K curated clips, using synthetic captions that densely label every utterance in detail. Second, we further refine the model via reinforcement learning on 2.9K human-corrected clips, using fine-grained feedback that directly evaluates utterance timing, speaker grouping, and speaker binding. On DiaDemBench, which we extend with utterance-level timestamps and subtitle redaction, both DiaCap with off-the-shelf transcripts and its end-to-end variant DiaCap-E2E achieve strong results. Specifically, DiaCap performs on par with Gemini 4 Argon in audiovisual diarization while clearly outperforming it in timestamp accuracy (76.59 vs. 42.76 TIME). It also maintains strong diarization on multilingual and minute-long videos, and transfers to audiovisual question answering.

CoT-Proxy: Cross-Format Transfer for Distilling Long-Horizon Policies 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Reasoning models become long-horizon agents through reinforcement learning or, more cheaply, through fine-tuning on a stronger teacher's trajectories, which contain the teacher's chain-of-thought as well as its actions. The standard fine-tuning approach imitates both, yet the strongest teachers are closed models whose chain-of-thought is hidden behind an API. We show that the chain-of-thought can be left out altogether and that the long-horizon policy can be distilled from the teacher's actions alone. The student is trained on these trajectories with its reasoning block left empty, while a KL loss on a replay buffer of its own generations limits drift from its initial model. Surprisingly, the policy learned from these trajectories with an empty reasoning block carries over to inference with thinking enabled, where the student reasons in its own style while acting like the teacher, an effect we call cross-format transfer. Distilling the actions of Qwen3.6-27B raises the task success of Qwen3-4B on AppWorld from 7% to 18% and that of Qwen3-32B from 12% to 25%. We further introduce CoT-proxies, short summaries of the teacher's reasoning placed after the empty reasoning block, which raise Qwen3-4B further, to 35%. Since neither actions nor CoT-proxies need the hidden chain-of-thought, the recipe applies unchanged to closed teachers. Distilling GPT-5.6 Luna from its visible outputs and the reasoning summaries its API returns raises Qwen3-4B from 7% to 48% on AppWorld and from 3% to 28% on SWE-bench Verified.

Reinforcement Learning Can Be Recovered From Its Own Rollouts 2026-11-24
📝 Preprint. 25 pages, 20 figures, 7 tables
Show abstract

Broadly speaking, reinforcement learning (RL) post-training converts compute into model capabilities by training on model-generated data. Typically, the rollouts are discarded after each update, and the knowledge acquired during RL resides in the model weights. In this work, we investigate how much of this knowledge is retained in the rollouts themselves. A natural first attempt is to replay the original RL algorithm on archived rather than freshly generated rollouts, but this leads to catastrophic collapse. We provide a simple explanation for this phenomenon, which in turn suggests a remedy: maximum likelihood on the archived rollouts. Surprisingly, starting from the same base model and replaying the rollouts in the order in which they were generated, without any distinction between correct and incorrect responses, is sufficient to recover the performance of online RL. In fact, a single maximum likelihood update per rollout batch closely tracks the entire learning curve of online RL. Furthermore, maximum likelihood on the rollouts of the last few RL steps often suffices to recover the final model's performance. This suggests that much of the compute invested in RL goes into discovering an improved sampling distribution rather than learning it from samples.

Can randomly parameterized neural environments supply useful experience for learning a reinforcement learning algorithm? We investigate this question using DiscoRL's rule-discovery framework, replacing human-designed training games with sixteen selected neural environments. Each game combines bounded vector dynamics with a random strategy-based reward and an independent outcome reward. After 224.8 million agent-environment steps, we froze the teacher and trained fresh agents. DQN outscored GREMLIN on most games, both generated and human-designed. Convex-Concave Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-24
📝 Code: https://github.com/UMass-SCALAR-Lab/Convex-Concave-RL
Show abstract

Policy learning drives many of the most consequential and heavily-invested applications of reinforcement learning today. Yet the core optimization problem it rests on (maximizing expected return) is notoriously non-convex, even under a direct policy parameterization, and the field has largely responded by avoiding it: optimizing convex surrogate approximations of the return under trust-region constraints (NPG, TRPO, PPO, AWR). We show that this seemingly unstructured problem is not actually structureless. In log-density-ratio coordinates $y := \log[π/π_n]$, the exact per-iteration objective, computable via per-decision importance sampling (PDIS), is a difference-of-convex-constrained difference-of-convex (DC-constrained DC) program. This structure lets us move beyond surrogate approximations: it recovers CPI, NPG, TRPO, and AWR as special cases along interpretable axes, and it opens a multi-step axis $k$ that couples consecutive decisions. We solve the per-iteration program with sequential convex programming (SCP), the standard solver for difference-of-convex problems, and establish inner-loop descent and limit-point stationarity for the theoretical algorithm, together with a conditional outer-loop performance bound. Empirically, multi-step Convex-Concave RL (CCRL) wins on diagnostic MDPs where credit must propagate across a horizon (its advantage growing with the dependency length), is competitive with a tuned PPO on classic control, and on a clinically motivated, stochastic, mid-horizon sepsis simulator converges markedly faster than tuned PPO to the same near-optimal survival, with an 11.3% higher area under the training curve. The code is publicly available at: https://github.com/UMass-SCALAR-Lab/Convex-Concave-RL

At the Limit: Autonomous Vehicle Drifting under Generative Policies 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Autonomous drifting requires agile control despite tire-force saturation and highly nonlinear vehicle dynamics. We propose an online reinforcement learning method for flow policies that combines stochastic differential equation (SDE) sampling for exploration with value-based policy optimization. This method supports both training from scratch and fine-tuning a policy pretrained on expert demonstrations. After training, control actions can be generated using either SDEs or ordinary differential equations (ODEs). AI-native radio access network (RAN) control involves interdependent decisions across network entities, protocol layers, and timescales. Understanding how AI and conventional methods can jointly support these decisions requires a common basis for comparing their functional roles. This survey introduces a technique-agnostic taxonomy of RAN decision-making organized around four complementary capabilities: Representation and Reasoning, Prediction and Simulation, Adaptive Control, and Decision Synthesis. MESSI: Multi-Expert Soccer Skill Integration for Physics-Based Character Control 2026-11-24
📝 Website: https://neu-3dvi.github.io/MESSI/
Show abstract

Physics-based soccer characters must perform diverse motor skills and transition smoothly between them, such as dribbling into a shot. However, skills that perform well in isolation may fail when executed sequentially, as preceding behaviors can leave the character and ball in unfamiliar states. We present MESSI (Multi-Expert Soccer Skill Integration), a framework that enables multiple soccer skill transitions through shared adaptation while preserving the original skill policy outside transitions. MESSI first trains eight task-competent experts using motion priors and task rewards, then distills them into a unified, skill-conditioned Transformer. Rather than retraining individual experts for different transitions, we train a shared, context-conditioned low-rank adapter on the frozen student through online reinforcement learning. Activated upon skill switching, the adapter helps the target skill handle unfamiliar entry states, while a learned exit mechanism determines when to return control to the original student. By disabling the adapter during native skill execution, MESSI preserves the original student policy by construction. Experiments across individual skills, skill transitions, and continuous skill chains demonstrate substantial improvements over direct switching, including an increase in dribbling-to-shooting success from 19.8% to 61.8%.

Online V-Learning with Adaptive Longitudinal Data 2026-11-24
📝 29 pages, 1 figure
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning methods for estimating optimal dynamic treatment regimes are increasingly applied in mobile health (mHealth), where treatment decisions are made continually. Luckett et al. (2020) proposed V-learning, which estimates an optimal policy from offline trajectories. In practice, mHealth policies are updated online. Batch updating, in which the policy is re-estimated after each block of new data, is a tractable middle ground between offline learning and updating after every observation. However, because each batch's treatment policy is estimated from data pooled across patients in earlier batches, the resulting data are adaptively collected and patient trajectories are dependent, a setting that offline theory does not address. We develop statistical theory for V-learning in this setting with varying, deterministic batch sizes. For each fixed batch, as the number of patients grows, we establish uniform consistency of the estimated value function, consistency of the estimated policy, a functional central limit theorem over the policy class, asymptotic normality of the estimated value of the learned policy, and a consistent variance estimator. To first order, the online procedure performs as well as an oracle that deploys the optimal policy after the first batch. With the number of patients fixed and the number of batches growing, the estimated value function and policy remain consistent under a persistent identifiability condition. In simulations, as the number of patients grows with the number of batches fixed, confidence intervals attain near-nominal coverage under adaptive assignment and the online procedure closely matches the oracle. With a small cohort followed over many batches, the learned policies improve steadily.

DynaMimic: A Physics-Informed World Model for Humanoid Motion Tracking 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Whole-body motion tracking enables humanoid robots to reproduce diverse skills from human demonstrations. Existing model-free reinforcement learning methods either distill motion specialists into a single policy at the cost of performance or train a generalist policy directly using cluster-scale computation. SuperMimic reduces this computational cost through a differentiable world model that implicitly learns the physics shared across motions by predicting the next state from the current state and action. However, this implicit formulation may not fully capture the underlying physical processes. Errors around changes of support can accumulate over imagined rollouts used to train the tracking policy. To address this challenge, we introduce DynaMimic, a differentiable world model that explicitly incorporates shared physics into state prediction. Specifically, its network predicts a contact wrench at each candidate support link and a residual generalized force. DynaMimic then uses these predictions to compute the next state through closed-form Lagrangian forward dynamics. Across 60 full LAFAN1 motions, DynaMimic reduces mean per-joint position error (MPJPE) by 18.6% relative to SuperMimic. For terrain tracking, we give both methods the same local height maps. Relative to terrain-conditioned SuperMimic, DynaMimic reduces MPJPE by 14.9% on A3 Parkour (66.15 to 56.28~mm) and by 10.8% on GRAIL terrain clips (141.88 to 126.57~mm). On a real A3 humanoid, the learned policies can perform diverse skills, such as floor recovery, cartwheel and a box vault.

Local Policy Manifolds for Efficient Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-24
📝 NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) aims at optimising several, often conflicting goals to improve the flexibility and reliability of RL in practical tasks. This is typically achieved by finding a set of diverse, non-dominated policies that form a Pareto front in the performance space. However, constructing such a policy set remains computationally demanding, as it requires finding not a single optimal policy, but a set of policies to cover a wide range of trade-offs among the objectives. We introduce LLE-MORL, an approach that traces policy manifolds by utilising the local relationship between the high-dimensional policy parameter space and the performance space. This structured representation enables an efficient search within contiguous solution domains via locally linear extrapolation, allowing for the rapid generation of high-quality solutions without extensive retraining. We also provide a theoretical analysis of the policy manifold structure in order to specify the applicability of the method. Experiments across diverse continuous control domains demonstrate that LLE-MORL consistently achieves higher Pareto front quality and efficiency than state-of-the-art approaches.

Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches Them to Act on What They Believe 2026-11-24
Show abstract

AI agents working on long tasks often commit to an answer before they have enough evidence, a behavior called laziness. In our evaluations, they frequently commit even while their stated belief still names several possible answers. An outcome reward at the end of the episode scores only the final answer, so it cannot teach the agent when to commit, because a lucky guess receives the same reward as an answer backed by evidence. During training, however, the simulator can compute the exact posterior over the answer at every step and tell whether the evidence is already enough. We propose Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB), which rewards the agent for taking further steps while this posterior still allows several answers and for committing once the evidence is sufficient. The reward needs no judge, critic or reward model, and it never reads the belief line the agent writes before each action. We also introduce Pro Worlds, a benchmark of visual puzzles where the agent views a large world through a small window. We evaluate three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of which are never seen in training. RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average. Under the outcome reward, GRPO and PPO fall below the base model on average. RLVB also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge by 9.3% on average while using 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours. The trained agent answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of the episodes on the three benchmarks, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% for the base model. Its accuracy gain therefore comes from answering more often at high levels of evidence. The belief line shows how the agent reaches this evidence: it acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and its line still names several answers, against 3 to 68% for the base model.

Reinforcing Diffusion Models by Instantaneous Trajectory Distribution Matching 2026-11-24
📝 Project Page: https://luo-yihong.github.io/iTDM/
Show abstract

Forward diffusion reinforcement learning (RL)---enabling deterministic ODE sampling without trajectory backpropagation---has grown increasingly popular for efficiently aligning diffusion models. However, we reveal that existing methods essentially rely on signed reward-weighted regression (RWR), regressing the trainable policy velocity onto rollouts from a behavior distribution. Unbiased baseline subtraction implicitly assumes that the policy velocity equals the behavior marginal velocity---an idealized assumption violated under classifier-free guidance (CFG) rollouts, offline/off-policy updates, and imperfect online self-consistency. This behavior--policy mismatch breaks baseline invariance and injects baseline-induced gradient drift, destabilizing training. To resolve this issue, we propose Instantaneous Trajectory Distribution Matching (iTDM), taking the infinitesimal limit of Trajectory Distribution Matching toward a time-local reward-guided target via the posterior-score identity to yield a sample-level gradient anchored at the behavior velocity without learning an additional reward-tilted target score. Tracking this velocity with an online auxiliary denoiser guarantees exact baseline invariance at the auxiliary optimum, effectively suppressing baseline drift across both online and offline RL. Extensive experiments across compositional generation, text rendering, and preference alignment show that iTDM achieves state-of-the-art in-domain and out-of-domain performance across group-wise online/offline RL and pairwise offline RL. Notably, on the challenging GenEval2 benchmark, iTDM converges nearly 3\times faster than competing methods while scaling smoothly to 0.87 without collapse. Furthermore, iTDM scales effectively to the 5.6B FLUX.3 model, achieving better performance across both in-domain and unseen metrics. Code is available at https://github.com/Luo-Yihong/iTDM

Breaking the Joint Action Space Dependence in Distributionally Robust Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-24
📝 preprint, work in progress
Show abstract

We study the sample complexity of learning robust coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) in finite-horizon, general-sum, distributionally robust Markov games with known rewards and a generative model of the nominal transitions. Each player evaluates a policy by its worst-case return over total-variation (TV) balls centered at the nominal transition distribution of every stage, state, and joint action; this is the standard $(s,a)$-rectangular uncertainty set. Existing generative-model guarantees for this model scale with the number $\prod_iA_i$ of joint actions, where $A_i$ is the number of actions of player~$i$. Since this number grows exponentially with the number of players, these guarantees suffer from the curse of multiagency. We design an algorithm whose sample complexity depends on the action sets only through the largest individual action-set size $A_*:=\max_iA_i$. The algorithm uses $\widetilde O(SA_*H^5D^4/\varepsilon^4)$ transition samples and, with probability at least $1-δ$, returns a Markov policy that is an $\varepsilon$-robust CCE from every initial state. Here $S$ is the number of states, $H$ is the horizon, and $D:=\max_i\min\{H,1/ρ_i\}$, where $ρ_i$ is the TV radius of player~$i$. Two ingredients make this possible: a plug-in estimate of the robust backup whose mean error does not depend on the number of states, and a single batch of samples per round that serves as bandit feedback for all players at once. The result shows that the dependence on $\prod_iA_i$ can be avoided without modifying the $(s,a)$-rectangular uncertainty set.

Quantile-Coupled Flow Matching for Distributional Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-24
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. v2: camera-ready version. 31 pages. Code: https://github.com/ori-goals/flowIQN
Show abstract

Distributional Reinforcement Learning (DRL) models the full return distribution, making it well-suited to uncertainty-aware and risk-sensitive decision-making. Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) critics are increasingly adopted in DRL, offering dense velocity supervision and a natural axis for scaling critic computation. Yet they carry a hidden metric mismatch: the distributional Bellman operator is a contraction in the Wasserstein distance, but CFM critics pair source and Bellman target samples arbitrarily, so their loss does not control the Wasserstein distance to the Bellman target. We show that this mismatch can be fixed with a single sorting operation. Our method, FlowIQN, sorts source and target samples within each mini-batch so that each source quantile is transported to its matching target quantile, approximating the monotone optimal transport coupling. We prove that this quantile coupling makes the CFM loss upper-bound, up to a constant, the squared 2-Wasserstein projection error. To our knowledge, this makes FlowIQN the first flow-matching critic whose training is an approximate Wasserstein projection of the Bellman target, and the guarantee extends to shortcut models for few-step inference. Empirically, the quantile coupling recovers multi-modal returns more accurately than an otherwise identical uncoupled flow with as few as two integration steps. FlowIQN improves substantially on the flow critic Value Flows in a fixed-policy diagnostic and is competitive on offline RL benchmarks across multiple policy extraction methods, identifying the coupling as a central design axis for flow-based critics. Reinforcement Learning under External Influence: Guarantees, Algorithms, and Sample Complexity 2026-11-24
📝 63 pages
Show abstract

In this paper, we study the problem of reinforcement learning under the influence of external events. For this, we consider Markov decision processes with continuous state and action spaces whose transition dynamics are perturbed by an external process in a non-Markovian manner. First, we establish the conditions under which the problem becomes tractable, allowing it to be addressed by considering only a finite history of events, based on the properties of the perturbations introduced by the exogenous process. To solve this problem, we propose and theoretically analyze a policy iteration algorithm that learns policies contingent on the current state of the environment and a finite history of prior exogenous events. Since this algorithm is not guaranteed to converge, we provide a guarantee for policy improvement in regions of the state space determined by the approximation error induced by considering tractable policies and value functions. We also establish the sample complexity of least-squares policy evaluation and policy improvement algorithms that account for approximations arising from considering only a finite history of temporal events. While our results are applicable to general discrete-time processes satisfying certain conditions on the rate of decay of the influence of their events, we further analyze the case of discrete-time Hawkes processes with Gaussian marks. We performed experiments to demonstrate our findings for policy evaluation and deployment in traditional control environments.

Load Balancing Neurons: Firing-Rate Control Sustains Plasticity in Continual Learning 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Neural networks in continual learning often lose plasticity: some neurons become inactive, while others fire almost constantly. This limits adaptation to shifting data and wastes capacity. Prior work mitigates this by periodically reinitializing low-utility units, but such resets can destroy previously learned features and do not proactively prevent low utility. We study a simple diagnostic measure: the firing rate of ReLU units, defined as the fraction of positive pre-activations. Low rates identify dead units, while very high rates indicate linearized, always-on units. Based on this view, we introduce a lightweight load-balancing mechanism that adjusts per-neuron thresholds to keep firing rates within a target range. Across continual ImageNet and class-incremental CIFAR-100, firing-rate distributions explain differences in plasticity across a range of approaches, including well-known techniques such as L2 regularization and non-affine normalization, and load balancing matches or exceeds reset-based methods on both benchmarks. The same mechanism carries over to reinforcement learning, improving Rainbow DQN on Atari over a LayerNorm baseline.

Are We Rewarding the Wrong Module? Module-Specific Policy Optimization for Multimodal Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) based on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has been widely adopted to enhance the multimodal reasoning of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). However, existing methods share a common limitation of Module-Level Credit Entanglement, in which the vision encoder, projector, and LLM modules are jointly optimized using the same advantage signals even when perceptual correctness and final-answer correctness differ. To address this limitation, we propose Module-Specific Policy Optimization (MSPO), a framework that separates perceptual and outcome-based learning signals across model modules. Specifically, MSPO uses responses to perception checklists comprising auxiliary questions about visual information to derive an advantage for training the vision encoder. From these responses, MSPO computes the Confidence-aware Perception Reward (CaPR), using a perception sign obtained by thresholding checklist accuracy to determine reward direction and posterior confidence from a Beta distribution over latent perception accuracy to scale reward magnitude. We then derive the vision advantage through group-relative normalization of CaPR and scale it using main-task success rates. This advantage guides updates to the vision encoder, while the projector and LLM retain outcome-based GRPO updates. Extensive experiments on seven mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that MSPO outperforms existing RL-tuned models on average at both the 3B and 7B scales.

Complex cooperative tasks require coordinated multi-agent behavior. Offline generative policies learn expressive cooperative behaviors from trajectories but remain limited by fixed datasets. Human-designed reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL) agents are frequently misaligned with the humans' true, unobservable objectives, and thus act only as proxies. Optimizing for a misspecified proxy reward function often induces reward hacking, resulting in a policy misaligned with the human's true objectives. An alternative is to perform RL from human feedback, which involves learning a reward function from scratch by collecting human preferences over pairs of trajectories. However, building such datasets is costly. To address the limitations of both approaches, we propose Preference-Based Reward Repair (PBRR): an automated iterative framework that repairs a human-specified proxy reward function by learning an additive, transition-dependent correction term from preferences. A manually specified reward function can yield policies that are highly suboptimal under the ground-truth objective, yet corrections on only a few transitions may suffice to recover optimal performance. To identify and correct for those transitions, PBRR uses a targeted exploration strategy and a new preference-learning objective. We prove in tabular domains PBRR has a cumulative regret that matches, up to constants, that of prior preference-based RL methods. In addition, on a suite of reward-hacking benchmarks, PBRR consistently outperforms baselines that learn a reward function from scratch from preferences or modify the proxy reward function using other approaches, requiring substantially fewer preferences to learn high performing policies.
