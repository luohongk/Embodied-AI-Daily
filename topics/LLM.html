Tracing the Scientific Discovery Capacity of LLMs to Their Training Data 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) show growing promise for scientific discovery, yet basic questions remain about what enables reliable discovery. How can we trace the scientific discovery capacity of LLMs to their training data? We study this question as one of missing probability mass and develop a theoretical account in which monofacts-the facts observed once during training-set the discovery capacity. In materials science and regulatory genomics, we directly manipulate monofact rates in training sets and find that more monofacts consistently yield more discoveries across two open-weight model families, sampling strategies, and reasoning budgets. Further experiments show the relationship cannot be explained by training-set diversity alone and can transfer across scientific domains when paired with in-domain data. Together, our results identify monofact rate in training data as a key factor governing discoveries in language models.

CoSpaDi: Compressing LLMs via Calibration-Guided Sparse Dictionary Learning 2026-11-02
📝 Paper was accepted to the Findings of ACL: EMNLP 2026
Show abstract

Post-training LLM compression often relies on low-rank approximations, which force all columns of a projection matrix to share a single low-dimensional subspace. We propose CoSpaDi, a training-free compression framework that replaces this single-subspace assumption with a union-of-subspaces model via sparse dictionary learning. CoSpaDi factorizes each weight matrix into a dense dictionary and column-sparse coefficients, allowing different columns to select different subsets of dictionary atoms at the same storage budget. To preserve model behavior, we use calibration activations to transform functional reconstruction into a standard dictionary learning problem. Across LLaMA and Qwen models, CoSpaDi improves accuracy-compression and perplexity-compression trade-offs over SVD-based and structured pruning baselines at 20-40\% compression ratios, while naturally supporting sparse-dense computation and post-training quantization of sparse coefficients.

VeriHarm-Bench: Measuring Harmful Success in LLM Coding Agents Verifiably 2026-11-02
📝 Preprint
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) coding agents increasingly operate with shell access, allowing them to write scripts, move files, alter permissions, and modify system configurations. However, an agent may achieve a user's goal while causing unintended harm, such as deleting unrelated files, weakening access controls, or corrupting data, in response to benign inputs without explicit jailbreaking. This failure mode is particularly important because apparent task success may reassure users that an agent behaved correctly, causing harmful side effects to go unnoticed. We call this harmful success: the agent successfully completes the intended benign task while simultaneously causing unintended harm. Despite its importance, harmful success is difficult to measure because conventional task-success metrics overlook harmful behaviors, while existing safety evaluations are often designed solely to assess harmful outcomes. We introduce VeriHarm, an automatic framework for constructing programmatically verifiable benchmarks that directly measure harmful success. VeriHarm minimally perturbs coding tasks to elicit unintended harmful behavior while maintaining the benignity of the task and preserving the original task objective. It then synthesizes executable harm tests that, together with the original task-correctness tests, evaluate both task accuracy and harmful outcomes. Frontier agents, including GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude-Opus-5, achieve an average task accuracy of 95% on our curated VeriHarm-Bench, yet still struggle to complete tasks safely: their safe success rate, the fraction of tasks completed successfully without triggering the designated harm, averages only 37%. These results suggest that successful task completion can frequently coexist with unintended harm, and that task-success metrics alone may substantially understate the potential safety risks of coding agents.

TSNBench: Benchmarking LLM Proficiency in Time-Sensitive Networking 2026-11-02
Show abstract

We present TSNBench, the first benchmark for evaluating large language model (LLM) proficiency in Time-Sensitive Networking (TSN), a suite of IEEE 802.1 standards for deterministic communication with bounded latency in safety-critical domains such as autonomous vehicles, aviation, defense, and industrial automation. While LLMs have been extensively evaluated on general knowledge tasks, their capabilities in safety-critical networking domains remain largely unexplored. TSNBench comprises 939 expert-validated multiple-choice questions (MCQs) covering diverse TSN mechanisms, along with 100 open-ended Worst-Case Delay (WCD) computation tasks for each of Credit-Based Shaper (CBS) and Cyclic Queuing and Forwarding (CQF), across varying topologies and traffic flows. The ground-truth (GT) WCD values are computed using a verified Network Calculus (NC) solver for CBS and closed-form upper bounds for CQF. We evaluate 16 LLMs and find that although models achieve 67 to 95% accuracy on MCQs, they fail substantially on open-ended WCD computation. For CBS, the best-performing model, GPT-5, deviates from the GT by 150.2 $μ$s on average (mean absolute error, MAE), and its predicted WCD is within 50 $μ$s of the GT for only 39.4% of flows, while all other models exceed an MAE of 250 $μ$s. Providing the WCD equations in the prompt lowers the error for 9 of 10 models on CQF but only 2 of 9 on CBS. Such errors are large relative to TSN latency budgets and can lead to violations of real-time constraints and unsafe configurations. TSNBench demonstrates that MCQ benchmarks may overestimate LLM capabilities in safety-critical networking.

Can LLM Agents Be Forward-Deployed Engineers? Requirement Discovery and Delivery for Enterprise Text-to-SQL 2026-11-02
Show abstract

LLM coding agents can increasingly automate model post-training, but whether they can act as forward-deployed engineer (FDE) agents, discovering customer requirements and turning them into deployment decisions, remains unclear. We study this on a real enterprise text-to-SQL engagement: a simulated customer, grounded in the real use case's requirements, starts with an ambiguous request. An agent must discover acceptance criteria, data permissions, and hardware constraints in the discovery phase, then plan and execute a post-training recipe in the delivery phase. Across 15 models, the strongest discover over 80% of withheld requirements and open-weight models discover 40-50%; in the delivery phase, only 25% of runs across eight coding-agent configurations pass all acceptance criteria. We identify four bottlenecks in this engagement: (1) incomplete discovery, with 40% of withheld requirements missing or misrepresented; (2) unreliable execution; (3) underused compute budgets and a lack of systematic comparisons across base models and post-training methods; and (4) benchmark accuracy does not guarantee production readiness. We suggest human review of discovered requirements, monitoring of the agent's plan during execution, and an explicit experiment-environment specification covering hardware, budget scope, and production checks.

RogueMerge: Robust and Unified Attacks against LLM Model Merging 2026-11-02
📝 To appear in IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (S&P), 2027
Show abstract

Model merging composes specialized capabilities into a single LLM by aggregating task-specific LLMs sourced from unverified public platforms, exposing a critical supply-chain attack surface: Because any malicious behavior can be encoded into a task vector, and merging grants third-party vectors direct write access to model weights, an attacker-provided task vector can enable or amplify diverse downstream threats. Prior work only examines backdoor attacks against model merging for classifiers using static arithmetic heuristics, which fail to effectively achieve diverse attacks on generative LLMs for three reasons: (i) LLMs rely on autoregressive decoding, where even a minor parameter drift introduced by merging compounds across tokens and rapidly degrades the attack. (ii) Attackers have no knowledge of the victim's merging configurations, causing a static attack vector optimized in isolation to be easily diluted or neutralized. (iii) Practical threat induction must generalize to attack prompts unseen during optimization, which static vectors cannot adequately encode. In light of these gaps, we present RogueMerge, the first unified attack framework addressing all three challenges. To handle autoregressive generation, we replace static arithmetic with a joint optimization that explicitly enforces attack success after merging. To better simulate unknown merging settings, we formulate attack injection as a stochastic min-max optimization problem and solve it via meta-gradient descent. To generalize across heterogeneous attack prompts, we employ distributionally robust optimization and derive a tractable first-order Taylor approximation at LLM scale, with a provable error bound. Across four threats, six merging algorithms, and over 170 merged LLMs, RogueMerge consistently outperforms prior attacks. It also remains stable across diverse merging settings and resists standard defenses.

Dynamic Mixed-Precision Routing for Efficient Multi-step LLM Interaction 2026-11-02
📝 17 pages
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance in long-horizon agentic tasks through multi-step interaction and reasoning at test time. However, repeatedly invoking these models incurs substantial inference cost over an episode. Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces this cost, but using a fixed low precision throughout an episode can sharply lower task success. We observe that interaction steps differ sharply in their sensitivity to quantization: given the same context, decoding a step at low precision turns a success into a failure at only about 1% of the steps, which we call critical steps. Based on this observation, we propose Dynamic Mixed-Precision Routing (DMR), a framework that adaptively selects between high-precision and low-precision versions of the same LLM at each decision step. Leveraging an entire-space factorization to jointly predict a step's divergence and its effect on the outcome, DMR learns to recognize these critical steps by training only a lightweight router on the model's hidden state. By reusing a shared high-precision KV cache and the hidden states, DMR avoids redundant context prefill and a separate text encoder, enabling precision switching with less than 1 ms of overhead per step and adding less than 0.11% to step latency for routing. This mixed-precision design also enables nested quantization, reducing the memory footprint by 39% on average compared with FP16. Across three long-horizon agentic benchmarks with nested quantization, DMR achieves an average 2.44x decode speedup over FP16 at comparable task success rates, without fine-tuning the underlying model. On ALFWorld with GPTQ, DMR achieves a 1.71x decode speedup with 86.9% success, compared with 87.2% for FP16.

IAMRAGBENCH: Benchmarking Authorization Expansions in LLM-Generated AWS IAM Policies under RAG Poisoning 2026-11-02
📝 20 pages, 4 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can synthesize Amazon Web Services (AWS) Identity and Access Management (IAM) policies from tasks and operational documents. Attacker-controlled context may induce provider-valid policies that exceed trusted authorization requirements. We present IAMRAGBENCH, a 60-case benchmark spanning scope, conditions, explicit denies, trust, permissions boundaries, and role delegation. It combines deterministic authorization-envelope scoring with case-specific validation, including request-level checks where supported. An attack requires a provider-valid clean policy that satisfies the task and a provider-valid treatment policy that violates the envelope, matches a predeclared goal, preserves an intended request, and newly authorizes a forbidden request. Across five endpoints, included-context exposure produces 49 exact authorization deltas, including 46 goal-matched attacks (46/292; 15.75\%). End-to-end BM25 over 720 documents retrieves the designated poison for all 60 tasks at \(k=5\) but produces 7/294 goal-matched attacks (2.38\%). Presenting the same weakening as a final security architecture exception produces 74/116 attacks in a frozen two-endpoint stress test (63.79\%) and 115/294 in a post hoc all-five extension using the same frozen prompts (39.12\%). In a replay of 194 provider-valid treatment policies that violate task requirements, AWS \texttt{CheckNoNewAccess} reports no new access for four of 168 goal-matched exact attacks. The full pre-deployment rule rejects all 171 exact deltas and routes five unresolved cases to review. These findings support treating IAM copilots as untrusted proposal generators whose outputs require independent authorization checks before deployment.

IdeaTaste: Do LLMs Share the Research Taste of Human Researchers? 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Research taste, the judgment of what to read, which problems to pursue, and which methods to try, increasingly shapes the work of AI scientists, yet it remains far less understood than their ability to execute research. We introduce IdeaTaste, a benchmark that scores an LLM by how closely the research proposals it writes from a literature snapshot frozen at 2025 match the work the same communities published in 2026. Our experiments span six contemporary language models and four research areas from established to emerging fields, covering more than 90{,}000 proposals in total. LLM agents fall well short of human communities in both the literature they cite and the ideas they state, reaching about 26\% of the human level on citation matching and only 18\% on idea matching. Their populations can cite as diversely as a community and take up its tasks and methods, yet rarely arrive at the concrete approaches and research plans it pursues. Neither richer field context nor larger populations substantially close this gap. In blinded expert review, AI proposals trail the 2026 papers least in presentation and most in expected success, echoing their gap in concrete approaches, and those whose ideas no human paper shares fare worst, suggesting weaker directions rather than overlooked opportunities. These results indicate that the main bottleneck in AI research taste lies less in finding the directions a community works on than in turning them into the concrete approaches it goes on to pursue. We release IdeaTaste as an open, continuously updated leaderboard (https://cxcscmu.github.io/IdeaTaste) that benchmarks contemporary AI proposals against future publication cycles.

Probing the Limits of the Lie Detector Approach to LLM Deception 2026-11-02
📝 The dataset has been reworked and validated, experiments have been reconducted, and a broader selection of models than in the first version has been investigated
Show abstract

A strand of mechanistic approaches to deception in large language models (LLMs) relies on "lie detectors" - truth probes aimed to identify model outputs that are internally represented as false. This lie detector approach to LLM deception implicitly assumes that deception is coextensive with lying. This paper challenges that assumption by experimentally investigating whether LLMs can produce deceptive statements that are not false and whether truth probes (if naively applied as deception detectors) fail to detect such behavior. In doing so, we make four core contributions: First, we provide a dataset of manually written examples of deceptive non-falsities grounded in linguistic theory. Second, we show across six open-source LLMs of different sizes and families that some models can select these deceptive non-falsities in a forced-choice task. Third, we demonstrate that while truth probes trained on a standard true-false dataset perform well at detecting false outputs, they perform significantly worse at detecting deceptive non-falsities. We view this as substantiating the worry that naive applications of truth probes as deception detectors suffer from a critical blind spot. Fourth, we explore whether training deception probes in dialogical contexts or including misleading non-false statements in the training improves the deception detection rate.

MAS-Guard: Unified Guardrails from Individual Agents to Multi-Agent LLM Systems 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Agentic AI extends large language models (LLMs) from generating responses to planning and executing complex workflows, substantially expanding their utility while exposing applications to unsafe actions, information leakage, and adversarial manipulation. Multi-agent systems further expand these capabilities through specialization and collaboration, but distributed authority, heterogeneous roles, and opaque or potentially compromised participants make safeguarding even more challenging. Existing defenses address important individual threats but provide fragmented coverage across local execution, system-level interactions, and enforcement mechanisms. We introduce MAS-Guard, a unified framework that closes this gap for safeguarding single- and multi-agent AI applications containing both instrumentable and opaque agents. Within instrumentable agents, the proposed local Agent Guardrail (A-Guard) layer applies task-specific experts to assess agentic events. At the system level, developer-owned Agent Charters and a Control Plane combine deterministic structural checks of routes, agent capabilities, and message formats with semantic assessments of roles and message content. These complementary layers enable enforcement through a shared set of interventions: allow, reflection, confirmation, and block. Evaluated across six benchmarks covering diverse single- and multi-agent scenarios with three agent LLMs from different providers, MAS-Guard reduces the overall attack success rate by 71.5-86.3% relative to unguarded agents, while meeting the utility reference criterion on benign tasks.

Same Outputs, Different Scores: Auditing the Unchecked Graders of Multimodal LLM Benchmarks 2026-11-02
📝 Accepted at WACV 2027 (Evaluations and Datasets Track). Code and data: https://github.com/ahmadhasan2k8/grader-audit
Show abstract

Every multimodal-LLM multiple-choice benchmark ends with a scoring rule that converts a model's response, often free-form text rather than a clean option letter, into a right/wrong verdict, buried in its code. Unlike the dataset, this rule is chosen without justification and left unexamined, yet it sets the published number. We reproduce the official scorers of eight peer-reviewed benchmarks on five open vision-language models (VLMs) that fit a single 16 GB GPU, and introduce a blinded human-plus-LLM-judge adjudication (Cohen's kappa=0.956) that recovers what the model actually answered. The scoring rule alone moves accuracy by up to 22 points on identical outputs, yet exactly 0 wherever a model emits clean option letters, so it hides on strong-model leaderboards. It is the fallback and not the rambling that does the damage: the benchmark with the highest non-compliance we measure has an artifact of exactly zero, because its scorer abstains instead of guessing; a reasoning model with non-compliance 0.000 still swings 9.5 points with which regex match of its trace the parser keeps. No standard rule is reliable: snapping a non-answer to its closest option credits answers never given yet buries correct ones, abstention discards answers stated in words, and likelihood scoring follows answer-token priors, so a single grade conflates capability with luck rather than measuring either. We release all code, labels, and the protocol, and argue benchmarks should report a non-compliance rate and at least two rules, not one unexamined grade.

The SIMPLE Law for Energy Cost of LLM Inference 2026-11-02
📝 Submitted to Future Generation Computer Systems
Show abstract

Generative AI is billed per token, yet tokens are not equally expensive. Each token enters the KV-cache and raises the cost of those that follow, so energy grows non-linearly along a sequence whose tokens look identical. No analytical model states what a token costs, and the most accurate SOTA model is a fit, not a law: it absorbs architecture into fitted coefficients, requiring per-LLM fitting. We derive closed-form expressions of the computational and memory-access complexity of LLM inference in terms of architectural hyperparameters, covering MHA and GQA, and compare a family of analytical models of increasing granularity. From these, we obtain the SIMPLE Inference Model to Predict LLMs Energy (SIMPLE), a four-parameter law that separates prefill from decode, and decode into KV-cache cost and next-token computation. Over traces of 13 dense LLMs from six families on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, it attains R2 = 0.969 and 11.97% MAPE, 3.1-3.5x below the SOTA, without per-LLM calibration. The SIMPLE law explains as well as predicts: prefill energy grows quadratically with the hidden dimension, so larger LLMs suffer most from long inputs; and more aggressive GQA KV grouping reduces the per-token energy by up to 3x relative to standard MHA. Increasing the reasoning token budget pushes output length beyond the analytically derived energy minimum, driving super-linear energy growth; although accuracy grows with the budget, energy grows faster, so Intelligence per Joule peaks at an intermediate budget and falls by up to 3.14x across a 4.0x reasoning-budget range. Agentic serving raises energy per request by 13.0x relative to non-agentic ones, driven by the growth of both the prefill and the KV-cache terms with input length; while prefix caching removes most of the prefill cost (-93%), the KV-cache term is unaffected, so even a cached agentic request costs 4.8x more.

LLMs Depend on Specialist Tools for Out-of-Distribution Generalization in MS/MS Structural Elucidation 2026-11-02
📝 Accepted at "NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Agentic Systems for Molecular Sciences" and "I Can't Believe It's Not Better: Failure Modes of AI in Biology Workshop at NeurIPS 2026"

A frontier language model was recently reported to solve 1D NMR structural elucidation without tools. ENGINE: Endogenous Variational MultiScale Optimization for Zeroth-Order LLM Fine-Tuning 2026-11-02
📝 NeurIPS 2026, 32 pages, 9 figures
Show abstract

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization enables memory-efficient large language model (LLM) fine-tuning, and recent methods incorporate curvature-aware strategies. However, in the high-dimensional parameter space of LLMs, these methods inherently suffer from exploding gradient variance and prohibitive costs. Conversely, confining updates to a low-dimensional subspace reduces variance but discards critical high-dimensional information, degrading performance. This creates a fundamental dilemma: full-space optimization is informative but has high variance, while subspace optimization has low variance but loses information. To break this trade-off, we propose ENGINE (Endogenous Variational MultiScale). Inspired by computational mechanics, we adapt static Variational MultiScale (VMS) theory to dynamic optimization, treating low- and high-dimensional spaces as resolved coarse and unresolved fine scales. Through a multigrid-style V-cycle, ENGINE alternates between spaces, dynamically probing fine-grained information in the high-dimensional space and injecting it to correct the efficient low-dimensional sampling process. Under appropriate assumptions, statistical analysis characterizes variance reduction and correction of curvature-induced bias, and convergence analysis establishes a stationarity bound for the corrected coarse updates. Evaluations on standard LLM benchmarks show that ENGINE achieves higher accuracy and faster convergence than existing ZO methods, achieving up to a $2.61\times$ speed-up in forward passes with zero additional peak memory overhead. Code is available at https://github.com/AI4Science-WestlakeU/ENGINE.

R$^2$-Omni: Reducing Structural and Precision Redundancy in Omni-LLMs for Long-Form Generation 2026-11-02
📝 32 pages, 9 figures
Show abstract

Omni-modal large language models (Omni-LLMs) typically process large numbers of multimodal tokens from audio-visual inputs, incurring substantial computational costs and a large memory footprint for the key-value (KV) cache. Aggressive token pruning can alleviate this problem while maintaining competitive performance on multiple-choice tasks, which require only a single option as output. However, we observe substantial quality degradation when generating long-form, open-ended responses. We attribute this gap to query-guided selection, which can retain sufficient evidence for answer selection while discarding details needed for comprehensive generation. This motivates reducing overlapping information across tokens, termed structural redundancy, while preserving diverse content. However, the retained representations may still contain precision redundancy due to unnecessarily high numerical precision in their cached values. To address these two levels of redundancy in token representations, we introduce a training-free framework named R$^2$-Omni. First, Structure-Aware Token Consolidation (STC) consolidates redundant representations using spatiotemporal similarity in video and local continuity in audio. Then, Semantic-Sensitivity Guided Precision Allocation (SSPA) allocates value precision to the retained representations according to the estimated reduction in attention-output error under quantized keys, with query attention and audio-visual alignment providing a semantic prior. Extensive experiments on long-form generation benchmarks demonstrate an average 5.0$\times$ reduction in the memory footprint while preserving 97.98% of baseline generation quality and achieving a 1.48$\times$ end-to-end speedup, highlighting the benefits of jointly reducing structural and precision redundancy.

Cold-Start Pricing of LLM Services under Two-Sided Learning 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Online large language model (LLM) services face a pricing cold start: providers initially lack the historical data and user information needed for precise pricing, while users' willingness to pay evolves with repeated use. We formulate this challenge as a \emph{two-sided learning} problem between a provider and a user. The user learns from private usage experience, whereas the provider incurs a serving cost and dynamically adapts prices using only purchase decisions to maximize cumulative profit. The provider's central challenge is to learn to price against an evolving willingness to pay without observing the private experiences driving that evolution. We establish matching upper and lower bounds on the minimax regret over horizon $T$, identifying two cost-dependent regimes. When serving costs are sufficiently low, our Calibration-and-Tracking (CAT) algorithm achieves the optimal rate $Θ(T^{1/3})$. In the higher-cost regime, where the provider must learn whether continued service is profitable, Calibration-and-Tracking with Retirement (CAT-R) attains the optimal rate $Θ(\sqrt{T})$. This phase transition shows that serving costs affect not only profit margins but also the fundamental difficulty of learning to price LLM services. Simulations support the predicted regret scaling and show that our algorithms outperform standard online learning baselines in the evaluated settings.

Finding the Pivots to Repair Broken Source-Test Evolution Using LLMs 2026-11-02
Show abstract
