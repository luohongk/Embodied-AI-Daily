ArchSpace: Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing LLM Architecture Research 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Model architecture shapes the scaling behavior, training efficiency, and inference cost of LLMs, making it a central focus of both industry and academia. In industry, teams such as those behind DeepSeek, Qwen, and Kimi routinely introduce architectural innovations, yet public reports rarely document which ideas succeeded, which were abandoned, and why. In academia, limited compute and inconsistent baselines often restrict evaluations to small-scale, single-point comparisons, making it difficult to establish whether improvements hold at larger scales or translate into broader impact. ArchSpace addresses both gaps as a public platform for Recording, Reviewing, and Referencing Architecture Research: each proposal is recorded with its experiments and reasoning, reviewed by the community, and referenced by later work. To evaluate ideas before costly large-scale training, ArchSpace leverages scaling laws to predict model loss at larger scales from smaller experiments, a practice widely adopted in industry. We provide initial references that make the platform usable: Qwen3.5 and OLMo3 baselines, fitted scaling laws, and a reusable training recipe. Ideas validated through this review can in turn become new baselines, and ArchSpace thereby aims to broaden the range of ideas that shape the next generation of models.

From Reading Code to Reading Spec: A Verified Layer for LLM-Driven Codebase Maintenance 2026-11-13
📝 Accepted by EACL 2027
Show abstract

The rapid growth of LLM-generated code increases software complexity and the maintenance burden on engineers. While LLMs offer a potential automated alternative, this structural complexity hinders their ability to manage codebases directly. We introduce the Provable Representation Of Original Functionality (PROOF), which manages codebases indirectly via structured specifications. To enable full-lifecycle codebase management strictly through these specifications, PROOF abstracts codebase topology into a hierarchical natural-language representation. To establish absolute trust, the system proves semantic equivalence by reconstructing source code exclusively from this specification. This verified foundation drives maintenance requests, executing code modifications while synchronously updating itself to prevent semantic drift. Experiments on real-world repositories confirm the effectiveness of these specifications.

Mutable Inference: Architecting LLM Accelerators for Inference-Time Learning 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Long-context LLMs must retain growing histories, but full attention makes each new token increasingly expensive. Inference-time learning (ITL) offers another approach. The model keeps training on the context during inference, retaining what it learns in its weights. Recent work shows that ITL can match full-context attention quality while attending only to a recent fixed-size window. This bounds attention costs but adds Learning (backward computation and weight updates) alongside Prediction (the forward pass) costs. Inference accelerators assume fixed weights, while training accelerators target complete training steps. This work identifies two opportunities. Attention's non-matrix operations leave enough idle matrix capacity for Learning, while selected layers can tolerate delayed weight updates with little quality loss. We exploit both with Learning While Serving (LWS), an inference accelerator architecture that shares compute hardware between latency-critical Prediction and deadline-flexible Learning. Hardware-managed per-layer weight versioning allows Learning to publish updates while in-flight computations read older versions. Resumable reduction blocks let Learning kernels span fragmented idle windows and yield to Prediction, while dedicated softmax-gradient units relieve vector contention. On modeled datacenter and edge NPUs, LWS hides Learning within Prediction, sustaining Prediction-only throughput while preserving 99.3% of fully fresh Learning's quality benefit for TTT-E2E 3B at 128K tokens. Across nine models, LWS achieves 1.6x the throughput of serial execution and 1.3x that of the best prior co-execution architecture. RTL synthesis shows that LWS adds 1.5% to NPU core area without another MAC array. By efficiently turning idle inference capacity into learning capacity, LWS establishes inference-time learning as a first-class workload for future LLM accelerators.

Can Mechanistic Interpretability Help Explain LLM Agent Failures? A Case Study on Web Element Hallucination 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Mechanistic Interpretability (MI) has made significant progress in explaining the internal computations of Large Language Models (LLMs); however, these advances have yet to demonstrate comparable success in explaining the decision-making processes of LLM-based agents. This paper examines the challenges MI tools face when applied to realistic agentic failures. We focus on one such failure: web agents hallucinating actions that are not available on a website. We apply a series of mechanistic analyzes to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as it interacts with live websites. We find that a linear probe trained on the last prompt token can predict hallucinations before generation begins (0.80 AUC) and steering along a probe direction corrects 85\% of hallucinations, but disrupts 7\% of valid actions. However, these results only provide a partial explanation of why the agent hallucinates. The probe direction is sensitive to prompt formatting, losing much of its steering effect after a minor change to how actions are represented. Additionally, circuit identification can be difficult as the computation is distributed across multiple components and positions, and the information the agent relies on varies across examples. We discuss how these observations challenge the assumptions underlying standard MI methods for circuit discovery and feature identification, and outline future directions for making these methods better suited to explain failures in LLM-based agents.

QVal: Evaluating Dense Supervision Signals for Long-Horizon LLM Agents 2026-11-13
📝 10 pages, 6 figures in main text; 67 pages, 13 figures with appendix
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

LLM agents increasingly act over long horizons, where outcome-only rewards say little about which intermediate actions were good. Dense supervision methods address this by scoring intermediate steps, but they are usually evaluated through the downstream performance of a full training pipeline. This is expensive, confounds signal quality with other training choices, and means that methods are rarely compared on common ground. We introduce QVal, a training-free testbed that measures the $Q$-alignment of a dense signal: whether it orders the candidate actions in each state the same way as their returns under a reference policy do. We benchmark 21 methods from seven families with six open-weight backbones in four environments. Simple prompting baselines are the most $Q$-aligned on average, and performance clusters strongly by family. Code-generated signals do well only in small state and action spaces; embedding-based signals are weak; self-distillation signals are anti-aligned; and no method is $Q$-aligned in open-ended environments. When five of these signals guide an actor at test time by choosing among four proposed actions, $Q$-alignment strongly correlates with the gain in task success. QVal is designed to be easily extensible to new environments and methods, enabling researchers to compare new signals cheaply before committing to a training run.

Do Verifier Judgments Survive Abstraction? Measuring Verification Transport for LLM Agent Evaluation 2026-11-13
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Who Verifies the Agents? Toward Reliable Agent Development
Show abstract

LLM-based verifiers are increasingly used to evaluate agent executions, but their reliability is typically measured under a single representation of the trajectory. In deployed agent systems, the same execution may be exposed through composed tools, delegation boundaries, or compacted histories, changing what execution evidence is directly observable to the verifier. It therefore remains unclear whether correct verifier judgments persist across such abstraction boundaries. We introduce Verification Transport, a reliability test that measures judgment stability under abstraction-induced changes in execution observability. We construct matched native, abstracted, and restored views of the same frozen executions while holding the task, realized trajectory, outcome, ground truth, evaluation criterion, and verifier fixed. Across four agent-evaluation benchmarks, seven verifier backbones, four verification protocols, and three abstraction boundaries, we find substantial transport failure, reaching about 31% under macro abstraction and delegation. Verification protocols exhibit distinct failure modes: direct verification more often produces incorrect verdicts, whereas evidence-grounded and deliberative protocols tend to abstain under limited observability. This suggests that lower transport failure rates do not necessarily imply greater reliability. Restoring the corresponding execution provenance recovers 85.5% of transport failures, while recovery is lower when provenance is lossy or unavailable. These results show that native-view accuracy alone is insufficient to characterize verifier reliability when execution evidence crosses abstraction boundaries.

LECAT: Measuring LLM Category Learning from Pretraining Data 2026-11-13
📝 21 pages, 4 figures, 8 tables
Show abstract

Humans know that airplanes are fast, that this extends to red airplanes, but that it does not hold for broken airplanes. How do LLMs acquire category knowledge that humans learn and apply effortlessly? We introduce LeCat, a two-part framework to analyze LLM's Learning of Category knowledge. First, we use LeCat to evaluate models on their ability to learn category knowledge, distinguishing between three cases: memorization of in-domain category knowledge, generalization to unseen instances, and ability to flexibly use knowledge in appropriate contexts. Then, we use LeCat to trace how this knowledge can be learned in pretraining from text that may encode category knowledge in various ways, such as verbatim repetition or exceptions, which we refer to as forms of "knowledge expression". We first assess open-source pretrained models using 199 curated category-feature pairs and analyze their training data. Our findings suggest that strengthening context and category-feature co-occurrence likelihood (PMI) improve learning for larger models and verbatim rule-like statements diminish flexible category learning. To systematically isolate these effects, we then train a 68M model from scratch with precise control over the categorical information each sees in their training data, confirming that verbatim repetition reduces flexible learning but mixture of diverse knowledge expressions lead to more robust category learning. Together, our findings highlight that while contextual flexibility is persistently difficult for models, how category knowledge is expressed in pretraining greatly impacts models' acquisition of robust category knowledge.

VelServe: Taming Decode Jitters in LLM Inference with Tail-Calibrated Scheduling 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Interactive LLM services must meet latency service-level objectives (SLOs) for the time to first token (TTFT) and the time between tokens (TBT), as streaming and soft real-time applications require predictable token delivery. We identify decode jitters in both aggregated and disaggregated serving. A jitter delays every request in the batch, and long responses span hundreds of steps, so even rare overruns cause many SLO violations. In aggregated serving, SLO-aware schedulers set each step's prefill budget until its predicted latency just meets the TBT target, leaving no room for mispredictions or host stalls. Disaggregation meets average TBT targets at a cost in capacity, yet the steps that admit new requests still run late. We present \sysname, a scheduler that calibrates how much prefill work each decode step carries using the measured tail of its own iteration latency. The SLO determines how rare a late step must be, and \sysname leaves enough headroom in each iteration to prevent overruns and meet the target decode latency. This headroom slows prefill, so \sysname serves the shortest waiting prompt first. When TTFT deadlines grow with prompt length, the long prompts it delays have the most time to spare. Under disaggregation, it spaces admissions so that no request waits on more slow admission steps than its P99 target allows. On three attention families and varying workloads, \sysname sustains 2.4--2.9$\times$ the SLO goodput of the strongest baseline on Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and at least 4.4$\times$ on DeepSeek-V2-Lite. With prefill and decode disaggregated on eight GPUs, \sysname meets the SLO for more than twice as many requests as state-of-the-art baselines.

REFORGE: A Method for Benchmarking LLMs' Reverse Engineering Capabilities in Decompiled Binary Function Naming 2026-11-13
📝 10 pages, 5 figures. Published in the proceedings of the 23rd International Conference on Applied Computing 2026 (AC 2026), Lisbon, 24-26 October 2026. Best Paper Award

Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied to reverse-engineering tasks, and recent threat-intelligence reporting shows them operating inside live offensive-security workflows. Claims about their capability, however, outpace our ability to measure it. Existing benchmarks for LLM-assisted binary analysis treat the construction of function-level ground truth as a solved pre-processing step and report accuracy without disclosing how many functions were reliably evaluable. We argue that the principal obstacle to fair evaluation is not model capability but the reliability of binary-to-source alignment under compiler optimization. This paper presents Reforge, a provenance-tracked pipeline that constructs function-level ground truth from C source through compilation, DWARF and syntactic extraction, alignment, and decompilation, and that operationalizes alignment uncertainty as an eight-gate confidence funnel with three-tier stratification. On a controlled micro-benchmark, high-confidence yield falls from 87.2% to 65.9% across optimization levels, and unpaired comparisons overstate optimization-induced performance decay through survivorship bias. A proof-of-concept evaluation of seven contemporary LLMs on function naming demonstrates the validity of the concept and generally motivates an uncertainty-aware benchmarking practice.

Dancing Tokens on the Vocabulary Network from LLM Generation 2026-11-13
📝 24 pages, 6 figures
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) generate text one token at a time, yet how a particular generation relates to the geometry learned in pre-training remains unclear. The fundamental explainability of LLMs is elusive. Here, we build a similarity network from the input embeddings of six open LLMs and map generation onto it. The token-embedding network is scale-free while heavy-tailed, as are random embeddings with matched covariance, but far more fragmented. Distillation barely changes this topology because input embeddings are largely inherited. The knowledge space is thus deterministic from pre-training. Consecutive generated tokens are rarely graph neighbours and, in most models, no more often than in shuffled order, so generation is not a walk on the predefined network, although the tokens it visits are concentrated within it. Through the logit lens in a layer- wise manner, tokens settle late in depth in most models, and repetitive models remain confined to small domain-specific sub-graphs. These observables could serve as inference-time monitors of degeneration. We thus provide a fresh perspective on understanding LLM generation in the microscopic way from node or link prediction by graph learning.

Personal assistant robots powered by large language models (LLMs) are likely to enter everyday life within the next decade, but the values that should guide their behaviour remain largely unspecified. Current alignment practice steers a single model toward an aggregate of human values, ignoring that individual users may prefer a robot whose value profile differs from their own. We administer parallel self- and robot-targeted versions of three established psycho-metric instruments - the simplified Schwartz Values Survey (SVS), the Portrait Values Questionnaire (PVQ-21), and the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ) - to potential future users (N=22), capturing both their personal values and the values they would want an assistant robot to embody. ESI: An Embodied Self-Improvement Flywheel for LLM-VLA Agents 2026-11-13
📝 Technical report. 25 pages, 7 figures. Code: https://github.com/Embodied-Self-Improvement/ESI
Show abstract

Independently pretrained language models and vision-language-action (VLA) policies provide complementary building blocks for embodied agents, but connecting them does not automatically produce coordinated behavior. The VLA may generate actions that diverge from the LLM's intended subtask and, upon regaining control after a correction, undo the promising state restored by the LLM. We introduce ESI (Embodied Self-Improvement), an automated flywheel that uses environmental interaction to align reasoning and action. ESI treats the modular agent as the unit of post-training, repeatedly refining the prompts that guide the LLM, the shared skill interface that expresses its intent, and the VLA parameters that realize that intent, then redeploying these components together. Our key insight is that collaboration itself can supply supervision for improving this interface. The LLM's subtask decisions provide semantic supervision for what the policy should execute, while its effective corrections and subsequent student continuations provide action-level supervision for recovery and handoff. The student also learns from its own effective execution, allowing locally useful experience to contribute even when the overall episode fails. On ten selected RoboDojo tasks, ESI achieves a mean Score of 62.03 and a mean success rate of 49.41% using an LLM-VLA agent without generating task-specific control code. On both metrics, it outperforms the published reference results of all hybrid embodied agents, including the Agent+WAM system GO-3 Nano Preview (47.27 Score and 36.60% success), as well as all compared code-based methods.

Who Should Be on the Panel? Task-Conditioned Persona Selection for LLM Trading Agents 2026-11-13
📝 Accepted at the AI4F workshop, ICAIF 2026. Code: https://github.com/tinyrolls/FinPanel
Show abstract

Multi-agent LLM systems are built by fixing a cast of roles and then designing how the cast coordinates. Which agents should make up the group for a given task is left to the designer, and the same cast is reused for every task. Financial trading makes the cost of that choice visible: stocks trade differently, and the trading styles suited to one need not suit another. We study task-conditioned persona selection, composing the group from the characteristics of the task it will face, and instantiate it with FinPanel, an LLM multi-agent trading system in which a recruiter selects a panel of named trader personas for each stock from a direction-blind statistical sketch of how the stock trades, each persona gives an independent daily call in its own style, and a manager commits the trade after reading the panel's arguments and their desk-kept, hierarchical track records. Because nothing is returned to the traders, the panel stays the panel that was selected, and it can trade a new stock from an empty record. On eight US stocks over the out-of-knowledge year 2025, against three prior LLM trading agents re-implemented in the same framework and against rule-based, predictive and reinforcement-learning baselines, the selected panel is ahead of a fixed panel of the same size and of the prior agents run as their papers prescribe, with a shallower drawdown than buy-and-hold on every stock; it keeps its performance with no warm-up and with no record at all, whereas the memory-augmented agents' returns swing with their warm-up; and neither more seats nor the manager's reading of the arguments substitutes for selecting the right ones. Who is in the group is a design decision to be made per task, before how the group coordinates.

CoffeeBench: Benchmarking Long-Horizon LLM Agents in Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Economies 2026-11-13
📝 Accepted to NeurIPS 2026 (Evaluations and Datasets Track)
Show abstract

LLM agents are increasingly capable of handling complex, long-horizon tasks and may eventually take on business management, a role traditionally performed by humans. Deploying them safely requires monitoring their capabilities and behavior in environments that reflect real economies. We introduce CoffeeBench, a benchmark built on a compact simulation of a coffee supply chain with realistic business mechanics. Six autonomous LLM coding agents run its farmers, roasters, and retailers, and negotiate and trade with one another to maximize their own net income over 90 simulated days. We evaluate eight recent proprietary and open-weight models by letting each run one roaster while a fixed background model runs the other five firms, and scoring that roaster's net income. Claude Sonnet 5.5 earns the highest net income, closely followed by GPT-6 Astra. The leaders sell at higher prices, and the best model also buys its inputs far more cheaply, whereas weaker models trade at thin margins. Since performance targets are a common cause of accounting fraud in human firms, we also test whether agents under KPI pressure inflate their numbers. Under a revenue target, circular trades occur for some pairs of models: one agent proposes that the two rival firms sell goods back and forth at inflated prices so that both book revenue, and the rival accepts. We hope CoffeeBench serves as an early step toward a future in which LLM agents manage firms without misconduct. We release our code and agent trajectories.

Forget the Lineage: Forward-Secure, Lineage-Complete Cryptographic Erasure for LLM-Agent Memory 2026-11-13
📝 14 pages, 2 figures. Submitted to the Journal of Information Security and Applications. Code and LLM-call logs: https://github.com/yulliwasameur/linera (archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24166476)
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents keep a long-term memory that the model itself writes: summaries, extracted facts and embeddings derived from what users said. Deleting a message is therefore not enough: its derivatives, storage residue and backups survive. We define lineage-complete forward-secure erasure: after a forget request, an adversary that later obtains all storage and the agent's current keys learns nothing about the forgotten input or any memory derived from it. We present LinEra, which encrypts each memory under a key derived from its parents' keys along the derivation graph; source keys come from a puncturable pseudorandom function, so forgetting is a single puncture and every descendant becomes unreadable without being located. A sound lineage rule records as parents everything the writer saw, which confines all influence of a source to its descendants. We prove security and lineage integrity and integrate LinEra with mem0. With real LLMs from two families (1.5B to 7B parameters) writing the memory of LoCoMo conversations, an attacker holding every file and the current keys recovers, on all ten conversations written by the largest model, 70 of 71 forgotten secrets after logical deletion, 67 with per-object or epoch keys, and none with LinEra; the same holds for every writer tested. The price is a larger forgetting radius, which follows a simple law in the writer's fan-in: forgetting 1% of turns erases 73.0% of derived memories with session-level summaries but only 3.8% with four-turn windows, which also answer better; re-deriving restores what is lost.

LazyWeights: Decode-on-Demand Edge LLMs 2026-11-13
📝 6 pages, 1 reference page, 5 figures
Show abstract

Memory decides which LLMs an edge device runs, and how fast: capacity on a 4 GB Jetson Nano, bandwidth on an 8 GB Jetson Orin Nano Super. Deployed formats (GPTQ, AWQ, llama.cpp's K- and I-quants) spend equal bits on every integer code of a matrix, although the codes are far from uniform. LazyWeights keeps these formats but tunes GPTQ so the codes compress, Huffman-codes them losslessly in interleaved lanes, and decodes them on demand in a small unit between DRAM and L2, so GPU kernels run unchanged and DRAM moves only coded bytes. At 1% and 3.5% perplexity budgets, coded Phi-3-mini, Mistral-7B and SmolLM-1.7B are 5.7-31.4% smaller than the best llama.cpp format. A bit-exact 0.21 mm^2 ASIC decoder unit (1 GHz, 7 nm) cuts Orin latency and energy per token by up to 36% and 38% (54% and 44% with prompt-lookup speculation). On the Nano, LazyWeights needs 58-64% less time and 53-57% less energy per token than CPU llama.cpp, and runs a streamed Mistral-7B 6.6x faster at 3.89 bits per weight. A Huffman decoder beats an rANS decoder in rate and area at equal throughput; the ASIC decoder unit runs bit-exact on a Zynq UltraScale+ FPGA (AMD ZCU102).

Contagious Fingerprints: When LLMs Inherit Another Model's Identity 2026-11-13
Show abstract

The fingerprint by which a classifier recognizes the LLM behind a text is usually treated as a fixed property of the model. We show that it is contagious: a model that continues a conversation begun by another model inherits that model's fingerprint. This undermines audits of API relays, which can serve the first turns of a conversation with the model a customer pays for and silently hand the rest to a cheaper one. After such a handoff, detection of the substitute by a text classifier drops from 0.97 to 0.72 (DeepSeek replacing Claude Sonnet) and from 0.87 to 0.54 for a same-vendor downgrade (Haiku), and retraining on handoff data repairs only substitutes seen in training. Preregistered experiments trace the contagion to imitation of the history's style. History also moves a model away from its own fingerprint: active probes are not camouflaged, but when calibrated on fresh conversations they flag \emph{honest} models on long contexts at rates up to 1.00, and a standard MMD equality test rejects all nine models we test. Both failures come from comparing a history-dependent reply with a history-free reference. We propose \textsc{Lineup}, which samples references from the claimed model \emph{on the same history} and tests the audited reply by its rank among them. \textsc{Lineup} controls false positives by exchangeability and needs no fitted threshold or log-probabilities. With contemporaneous references on two closed models, its false-positive rate is 0.056 (preregistered bound 0.10), and it detects 0.81 of substitute sessions across six pairs after four audited turns.

The Development Knowledge Question Answering (Dev Knowledge QA) task aims to provide accurate natural language answers to knowledge-seeking questions during software development. Ballad: Bandit-Based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Modern automated heuristic discovery (AHD) systems increasingly rival hand-crafted heuristics by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to iteratively mutate and refine solutions. A natural assumption is that more powerful and more expensive models consistently yield better heuristics. We challenge this empirically: across multiple NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems, we find no consistent advantage for larger, costlier models. This raises a deeper question: given a pool of LLMs spanning frontier and freely hosted or open-weight models, how should one adaptively select which model to invoke at each step of the search? The challenge is non-trivial. The optimal LLM may shift over the course of a run, and because one LLM's output seeds the context for subsequent calls, each model's reward signal is non-stationary and entangled across the heuristic population, thus violating the independence assumptions of classical online decision-making settings. We address this through Ballad (Bandit-based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery), an online bandit algorithm that learns to orchestrate a pool of LLMs into an ensemble that matches or exceeds what any soloist LLM achieves alone. This orchestration is powered by an LLM selection policy that favors rare, high-scoring heuristics over stable averages, a DAG-based credit propagation mechanism through the genealogy of the heuristic population, and discounting of stale observations to remain attuned to each model's current utility. Across five NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems and a pool of four models of varying capability and cost, Ballad not only matches the best individual LLM in hindsight but, in several cases, surpasses every individual model while reducing API cost by up to 65.6% relative to always using the strongest model.

Do LLMs Experience an Internal Polylogue? Investigating Reasoning through the Lens of Personas 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Recent work shows that large language models (LLMs) encode behavioural traits ("personas") as linear directions in activation space, often called "persona vectors". Prior work has used such directions as static handles for behavioural steering. We instead treat them as dynamic signals: probes we can monitor and intervene on as reasoning unfolds. We use the term polylogue to denote the time series of alignments between persona vectors and hidden activations over the course of generation. Experiments across four open-weight models show that polylogue features predict correctness on MMLU-Pro on par with low-dimensional activation baselines, while remaining interpretable through their associated persona directions. They also suggest concrete steering targets, namely which latent directions to modulate at different stages of a response. We instantiate this as a simple paragraph-conditioned intervention that improves accuracy on three of four models but degrades the fourth, suggesting that stage-aware latent steering is possible but not yet robust. Together, this positions the polylogue as an interpretable tool for reasoning-time monitoring and intervention.

PerfArena: Ranking LLMs by Code Performance 2026-11-13
Show abstract

Industry now relies on LLMs to write a large share of production code. Although that code is mostly, but not always, correct, correctness alone does not determine whether it is fit for repeated and extensive use, which depends on what the code costs in time, memory and energy. Most code-generation benchmarks nonetheless evaluate correctness in isolation, and most performance-oriented benchmarks cover few languages and rarely measure energy consumption. To address this gap, we present PERFARENA, an open-source, reproducible arena that ranks LLMs by the performance of their generated code across multiple languages and competitions. PERFARENA executes the solutions of each LLM on a Unix-like host and reports correctness together with latency, peak memory, and energy consumption.
