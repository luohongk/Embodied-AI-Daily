Measure Before You Train: Typed Decision Models vs. a Classical Baseline vs. a Generative LLM for Routing a Personal Multilingual Inbox 2026-10-23
📝 8 pages, 7 tables. Single-user case study. Code and protocol: https://gitlab.com/federicolucca/foyer-measure . The emails are private and not released
Show abstract

Personal email agents decide, for every message, what it is, whether it is urgent, whether to draft a reply, create a task, or ignore it. Their error rate is rarely measured: the stored labels were written by the system itself. We present Foyer, a self-hosted personal inbox agent in daily use, and a measurement protocol (stratified sampling, frozen snapshots, thread-grouped splits, pre-registered hypotheses, human labels in the agent's review loop) that yields ground truth before any training. On 739 real Italian/English emails labelled by the owner (231 unbiased, 500 adversarially sampled), we compare nine systems under thread-grouped cross-validation: the production keyword rules, a TF-IDF + logistic-regression baseline, local typed decision models (Tev1, Nimble), a hosted decision model (Nimble v4-max), and hosted generative LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma 4 26B/31B). Four results. The production rules score 28% on message type against a 58% majority class: the field shown most prominently carried no information. With ~200 labels, logistic regression has the highest accuracy on every field (type 0.79, urgency 0.74, needs-reply 0.83, is-noise 0.90), though it never predicts the two rarest classes and is statistically tied with Gemma 4 on type; few-shot prompting does not close the gap. The sender's relationship (client, supplier, service) is recovered from other emails of the same contact with 98.6% accuracy where the contact has been seen, and is not inferable from a single message by any model: it belongs in the contact graph, not the prompt. Finally, accuracy is not the product metric: composed into actions, the most accurate system most often ignores emails needing action (18% vs 4% for the best LLM); a cost-chosen threshold on its noise probability halves that rate and makes it the cheapest system.

VETTA: Coordinating Turn- and Token-Level Credit Assignment for Multi-Turn LLM Agents 2026-10-23
📝 18 pages, 6 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

Multi-turn LLM agents often receive sparse task feedback across several interactions, while generating each response token by token. This creates two related credit-assignment questions: which responses helped achieve the outcome, and which generation decisions mattered within each response? Existing methods typically focus on only one level: turn-level methods evaluate complete responses but do not distinguish the decisions within them; token-level methods can propagate feedback across turns but do not explicitly model credit for each response. These complementary limitations motivate learning credit at both levels and coordinating it in a single policy update. We introduce VETTA, a credit assignment method that jointly learns turn- and token-level values through separate heads on a shared lightweight critic. VETTA computes advantages along both temporal sequences and combines each turn advantage with a within-response-centered token residual for PPO updates. Furthermore, to reduce value-learning cost, the critic retains only early Transformer blocks from the pretrained checkpoint used to initialize the actor. Experiments on four agent benchmarks demonstrate VETTA's strong performance. With Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, VETTA achieves success rates of 95.5% on ALFWorld, 76.0% on WebShop, and 52.2% on ScienceWorld. With Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, it reaches 84.9% and 80.7% success on Sokoban under sparse and dense rewards, respectively. Critic-depth comparisons further show strong task performance with substantially lower critic-side computation. These results demonstrate that coordinating turn- and token-level credit with a lightweight shared critic improves agent performance while keeping value estimation efficient. Code is available at https://github.com/Jiaju-Chen/VETTA-official.

Monetizing Intelligence: Advertising and Subscription Design on LLM Platforms 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) platforms introducing advertising must decide how much capability ad revenue should fund and what consumers should pay to avoid ads. We study a monopolistic platform that jointly chooses costly response quality, advertising intensity, and subscription prices when advertising reduces user value but better service raises advertising returns. Three findings follow. First, when partly ad-supported and ad-free plans coexist, advertising can finance useful service for consumers who receive none under pure subscription. Lower effective quality in middle plans weakens the alternatives available to premium users, allowing higher prices for unchanged ad-free service. Some fully ad-supported users gain utility, while partly ad-supported and ad-free users lose: broader access can coexist with utility polarization. Second, an intermediate advertising cap can maximize consumer surplus by preserving some revenue that finances service while limiting degradation. Third, cheaper compute can raise quality and reduce ad intensity, yet sufficiently large cost reductions can increase aggregate advertising revenue above its initial level because better service raises the value of the remaining advertised interactions. An extension shows how advertising-funded upgrades can bring plans to the same maximum capability and advertising exposure, eliminating the basis for different prices. These results make menu design central to evaluating LLM advertising: the consequences extend beyond users who see ads to the alternatives and prices faced by those who do not.

Green Code or Costly Code? Evaluating the Energy Costs of Iterative LLM-Based Code Optimizations 2026-10-23
📝 Accepted at the 49th IEEE/ACM International Conference on Software Engineering (ICSE 2027)
Show abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used to optimize code for performance. This practice often assumes that improvements in execution speed naturally translate into overall lower energy consumption. We challenge this assumption empirically by measuring whether performance-optimized code produced by LLM self-refinement pipelines is also energy-efficient relative to canonical human reference solutions, and whether the energy cost of the optimization process can be offset by subsequent execution-time savings. We evaluate 11 open-source models across four decoding configurations on all 1,000 problems of EffiBench, a state-of-the-art performance benchmark, while collecting per-process, hardware-level energy measurements throughout. Inference energy spans 0.79 to 8.68 MJ across model-variant pairs, which is roughly equivalent to the energy of 250 to 2800 GPT-4.1 queries. Limiting the maximum number of output tokens has the largest impact, reducing energy use by up to 69% compared to the default configuration. On the execution side, 42 of 44 configurations produce code that consumes more energy per problem than the canonical human reference, with overheads ranging from +4% to +886%. The two exceptions achieve only marginal gains, with break-even points on the order of 10^6 to 10^7 executions. Mixture-of-Experts architectures consume substantially less inference energy than equivalently-sized dense models without sacrificing correctness, making architectural choice a more consequential sustainability lever than decoding parameters. Performance optimization and energy efficiency are not always aligned goals: LLM self-refinement consistently produces faster-seeking but energy-heavier code relative to what a human engineer would write. All artifacts are available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23598170.

Advancing SVD-based LLM Compression via Layer-Wise Error Model Search 2026-10-23
📝 Accepted at ICML 2026
Show abstract

Low-rank SVD-based compression offers a powerful strategy to reduce the computational costs of LLMs. However, existing methods face two key limitations: (i) global rank allocation, where uncalibrated error proxies fail to capture complex error propagation, and (ii) decomposition quality, where Fisher-based estimators suffer from severe rank collapse. In this work, we address these limitations by introducing Layer-wise Error Modeling Search (LEMS) and KFAC-SVD. LEMS advances rank allocation by introducing a layer-wise error surrogate that integrates local and global layer importance alongside a propagation bias, enabling effective global rank allocation via an ILP formulation. KFAC-SVD improves decomposition quality by utilizing token-wise statistics, mitigating the rank deficiency observed in prior Fisher-based SVD approaches. Across Mistral, Qwen3, and Llama 3 model families, we show that LEMS consistently outperforms existing search strategies, delivering significant zero-shot accuracy gains of up to 4.8 p.p. that generalize to model sizes of 70B parameters, while KFAC-SVD achieves an average perplexity improvement of 15%. Project page and code: https://lems-svd.github.io

Trusting the Tool: Asymmetric Deference of LLM Vulnerability Auditors to Static-Analysis Evidence 2026-10-23
📝 33 pages, 9 figures, 5 tables. Submitted to IEEE Access. Code: https://github.com/lzh0082-cloud/CODE-DETECTION
Show abstract

Pipelines that combine large language models (LLMs) with static analysis let the model consume the analyser's output as evidence, but whether the model distinguishes true from false evidence, and whether its scrutiny depends on what the evidence claims, has not been measured. We built an intraprocedural analyser for C/C++ that produces proof obligations and audited vulnerable and patched functions from PrimeVul under six evidence conditions that differ only in the notes, including fabricated assurances that a missing check exists and fabricated alarms that an added check is missing. The study covers 916 pairs in four disjoint sets, eleven model configurations from four vendors and 16,572 audits; no model is trained or fine-tuned. True evidence barely improved accuracy (+0.02). False evidence acted asymmetrically: relative to true notes about the same operations, false assurances lowered the detection of real vulnerabilities by up to 19 points, whereas false alarms never significantly lowered accuracy and were disputed far more often. An internally registered replication on seven new models confirmed that false assurances move verdicts towards their claim more than false alarms do (mean asymmetry +0.16, 95% CI +0.10 to +0.24). Instructions in the audit prompt did not reliably reduce deference; the best one failed to replicate. Verifying each claim in a separate call and showing the refuted claims raised accuracy under false assurances by 12 points in a pre-specified test (p=0.002) without loss under true evidence; for a second model the gain was smaller and not significant. Pipelines should therefore verify a tool's guarantees before the model relies on them, and studies should measure deference in a direction-aware way.

Do "True" and "False" Add Up to One? Complement Consistency in LLM-Agent Opinion Simulations 2026-10-23
Show abstract

In LLM-agent opinion simulations, each agent reports the probability that a claim is true after seeing its own previous probability and the probabilities reported by its neighbors, and that number becomes its state in the next round. If the number describes the agent's position rather than the sentence it was shown, then rewriting the claim as its complement and replacing every probability p with 1 - p should return the complementary answer, so the two reports should sum to one. We test this with three small open models: Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B. On 2,304 agent states taken from a running network simulation, Qwen's paired reports sum to 1.18 on average, and on 48 real fact-checking claims from AVeriTeC they again exceed one. A controlled benchmark of 24 worlds, each with exactly two possible outcomes, shows the same failure in all three models. The discrepancy is two to ten times larger than the change produced by a same-meaning paraphrase, and it persists under repeated sampling and across peer group sizes and report strengths. When the reports are fed back into two populations that start from complementary states, the populations do not remain mirror images: their divergence is positive after five rounds for every model. Averaging each answer with the complement of its paired answer restores consistency by construction and lowers Brier loss averaged over the two wordings, but at the final round it beats the better of the two original wordings for only one of the three models. A schedule-by-targeting experiment on the same model gives a small, stable interaction because such sweeps keep the claim wording fixed. Consistency under rewording therefore needs its own test before simulated probabilities are read as beliefs

AI Alignment for Digital Democracy: Fairer Voters Representation against LLM Biases 2026-10-23
Show abstract

AI representation is emerging in digital democracy, assisting in voter emulation and decision-making. Large language models (LLMs) behave differently depending on the model, the prompt context, and the voter information they receive, representing some groups more accurately than others and potentially making the emulation unfair to certain groups. We investigate demographic biases in LLM-emulated electorates: how accurately LLMs represent individual votes, whether some voter groups are represented more accurately than others, and whether AI alignment can reduce these disparities without sacrificing overall accuracy. Using three U.S. presidential elections, we compare a closed-source and an open-source LLM across voters' demographic features and test AI alignment strategies toward fairer voter representation. Removing gender from the voter profile reduces the largest group error of the models by 22-42%, and removing education reduces it by 64%, whereas removing self-reported race increases the mean group error by over 40%, all without lowering accuracy. Common alignment strategies, such as repeated queries or fairness instructions, do not reduce these errors and can double them.

Interchangeable in Role, Not in History: Replacing Members of Formed LLM Teams 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Multiagent LLM systems often treat a role as a slot: when a member is replaced, any agent competent at the role is expected to fill it. We test this view on teams whose members keep short written notebooks. A one-seat swap replays a formed, frozen team with its own member, with a same-role newcomer carrying another team's notebook for that role, or with a newcomer carrying none. In cooperative cooking, wildfire response and medical question answering, the experienced newcomer, averaged over seats, outscores the one without a notebook in every setting, so the usual comparison would approve it. Against the original team, the same replacement falls short in every setting, and the team spends more messages per unit of progress. On the hardest cooking level, the shortfall appears only after the team has worked together, persists across model families and decoding temperatures, and narrows by two thirds with added planning-only members. Because the team's history is written down, we can take it apart. In the default configuration on that level, notebooks from the same donor teams leave a newcomer that leads, holding the recipe, worse off than no notebook and one that follows better off. The notebook lines about the partner reproduce this whole difference between the seats. Whether an agent can replace a teammate depends on the seat and the team it enters.

Honeyquest for LLMs: Rethinking Cyber Deception for AI Attackers 2026-10-23
📝 20 pages, 4 figures, 2 tables

Show abstract

The empirical foundation of cyber deception relies on human-centered hypotheses, but the rapid emergence of autonomous, AI-enabled attackers challenges whether this foundation transfers to AI agents. To address this, we introduce an automated evaluation framework adapted from the Honeyquest instrument to assess LLM attacker judgment at scale. Our 21-LLM cohort spanned 10 providers, diverse architectures and specializations, open- and closed-weight models, and parameter scales from 8B to over 1T. We evaluated the performance of this LLM cohort (yielding 10,962 responses) against the 47-participant human baseline across an identical set of 174 reconnaissance queries. Our empirical evaluation reveals three key findings that establish LLMs as a distinct attacker class: (1) every model in our cohort falls for deceptive traps at a significantly higher rate than human attackers; (2) the defensive attention-diversion effect observed in humans is statistically absent in our LLM cohort; and (3) a critical recognition-action gap, where LLMs successfully articulate trap recognition in their reasoning but exploit the deceptive elements anyway 73.4% of the time. Across the 21 models, trap recognition in reasoning text did not predict fell-for-trap behavior (Spearman r = +0.08, p = 0.73). Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that human-centered deception hypotheses do not reliably transfer to AI attackers, highlighting the critical need for new research into AI-native active defense frameworks.

ActionRAG: Evidence-Refining Graph Diffusion for Long-Term Memory in LLM Agents 2026-10-23
Show abstract

LLM agents need long-term memory that can recover precise and mutually consistent evidence from growing interaction histories, particularly when answers depend on relations among facts rather than isolated semantic matches. Current memory pipelines remain vulnerable at two stages: indexing errors can fragment repeated entities or merge distinct ones, and online retrieval often treats initial similarity scores as final even when the surrounding graph provides evidence for revising them. We introduce ActionRAG, a graph memory architecture that couples cautious indexing with query-time graph retroevaluation. Its indexing pipeline reconciles type labels and applies tiered entity resolution. Given a query, dual-channel entity and triple retrieval forms an initial evidence set and induces a bounded local graph. Query-conditioned receiver-normalized diffusion refines triple scores using local path structure. Seed protection, hub-penalized flux, and triple materialization preserve direct evidence while recovering missed connections. We evaluate ActionRAG on MemoryAgentBench (MAB), which tests accurate retrieval, conflict resolution, long-range understanding, and test-time learning. Across its eight deterministic-scoring columns, ActionRAG achieves 55.1 average accuracy, improving over the strongest retrieval-based memory baseline by 5.4 points. Under MAB's query-time cost accounting, mean synthesis cost stays below \$0.001 per query on the four reported cost columns, and the sum of separately measured retrieval-side p99 components is 440.7 ms on our consumer Apple Silicon setup.

Same Answer, Different Confidence: Protocol Sensitivity in LLM Confidence Calibration 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Large language models (LLM) can state their confidence in an answer (verbalized confidence), or the confidence can be read from the probability of the answer's tokens (token confidence). Comparisons of the two ask which is better calibrated, but the token probability of an answer depends on the text that precedes it. Across five open LLMs and four question-answering datasets, we fix each answer, its verbalized confidence, and its correctness label, and vary how each score is measured. The scores themselves change with the protocol, and so does the comparison between them. Changing the context in which the token probability is read shifts the gap between the two scores by about the size of the gap itself (.287 vs. .304 in expected calibration error, ECE; .189 vs. .145 in AUROC). The 95% interval of the shift excludes zero in 19 and 17 of the 20 settings, and under AUROC the shift has the same sign in all of them, so the context changes how answers are ranked. The better score flips in 8 settings under each metric, counting only flips that the intervals support, and the shift also appears in four larger models of up to 72B parameters. Most of the shift appears with changes to the instruction, not to the position of the answer. Other measurement choices also matter, and stated confidence depends on how a supplied answer is written. We turn these results into a reporting checklist for LLM confidence and its comparisons.

The Butterfly Defects: Tracing Cooperation in LLM Agent Networks 2026-10-23
Show abstract

As large language models (LLMs) increasingly operate as interconnected agents, a fundamental question emerges: does individual cooperation translate into collective cooperation? We investigate this question through the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD), moving from dyadic interactions to homogeneous and heterogeneous networks of open-weight LLMs. Our findings reveal that cooperation is highly sensitive to model composition and seemingly minor linguistic changes. Models that cooperate with identical peers can shift toward defection in mixed populations. A simple uncertainty cue can increase or decrease cooperation, redistribute costs, and affect unexposed agents several hops away. Even reversing action-label order can dramatically alter behavior, with uncertainty framing sometimes amplifying this sensitivity. Together, these findings challenge the notion of cooperation as a stable model-level property, revealing how strategic behavior depends on interaction partners, network positions, and the language used to frame decisions.

Memory demands from data analytics and LLM workloads grow rapidly, driving up memory costs in cloud and data centers. SANE: Automated Scorer-Specific Metamorphic Testing for LLM Judges 2026-10-23
📝 15 pages, 9 figures. A shorter version was accepted for presentation at the non-archival NeurIPS 2026 TAE workshop
Show abstract

LLM judges increasingly mediate benchmark evaluation, model selection, data curation, and training. Their decisions are useful only when each judge responds reliably to changes affecting its scoring criterion. Existing methods for reliability testing perturb inputs with known expected effects on judge scores. Prominent LLM-judge testing harnesses combine human-authored perturbation recipes with synthetic test generation, leaving scorer-specific recipe ideation outside the automated pipeline. This limits their use to fixed perturbations and pre-assumed scorer types. We introduce SANE, an agent-based framework that encodes behavioral requirements for stochastic evaluators as reusable agent instructions. Given a scorer specification, implementation, and representative inputs, the pipeline first identifies scorer-specific capabilities, then automatically derives perturbation recipes and expected score relations for each. SANE also defines a scorer-independent validation framework for generated judge tests. It determines whether each recipe agrees with scorer intent and whether each instantiated case realizes the recipe and supports its expected relation. Because SANE generates a recipe inventory tailored to each scorer, the number of aligned recipes grows as scorers are added. Across five domains, SANE produces 3.7 times as many validated test families as the Judge Reliability Harness (JRH) at comparable end-to-end validity and detects scorer deficiencies in every domain, including professionalism and toxicity, where JRH detects none. On faithfulness, SANE achieves strong human alignment in judge ranking: its test-suite scores rank 11 judge models consistently with human-labeled FaithBench performance (Spearman rho = 0.852), while JRH does not (rho = 0.100).

InferSlice: A Reference Architecture for SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco Wide-Area Network 2026-10-23
Show abstract

LLM inference is increasingly deployed as a core service for enterprise and interactive applications, but sovereignty, compliance, latency and resource-locality requirements can limit the suitability of centralised cloud deployments. Telco operators offer geographically distributed compute and network infrastructure for addressing these constraints. However, existing LLM serving frameworks are designed for tightly coupled data centres and provide limited support for coordinating geographically distributed and heterogeneous resources. We present InferSlice, a reference architecture for Service-Level Objective (SLO)-driven LLM inference over a telco infrastructure, integrating network slicing, GPU partitioning, elastic serving and observability. Led by BT in collaboration with the University of Bristol and NVIDIA, and realised on the JOINER UK national-scale wide-area network (WAN), InferSlice is evaluated on service differentiation across tiers, elastic scale-out, client-edge bandwidth as a bottleneck, and the reach of tensor, pipeline, data parallelism, and prefill/decode disaggregation over the WAN. Our results show that, on a general-purpose telco network, tiers yield distinct latency-throughput regimes, and weight streaming shortens scale-out time. Replication is placement-insensitive but memory-bound; pipeline parallelism tolerates near- and mid-cross-site hops, while tensor parallelism and disaggregation fail beyond a single subnet hop. We share practical insights from operating SLO-driven inference on a telco network, lessons learned and open questions, and hope to encourage further research on distributed and heterogeneous LLM inference systems.

Probe Before You Edit: Probing-Guided Molecular Optimization for LLM Agents in Structure-Based Drug Design 2026-10-23
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. Revised manuscript
Show abstract

Structure-based drug design increasingly employs LLM agents to iteratively refine ligands against a target pocket, yet a viable ligand must satisfy two often-conflicting objectives---binding affinity and druggability---which single optimization steps rarely improve together. To quantify this difficulty, we introduce two diagnostic metrics: the first measures how often a single edit improves both objectives, and the second measures how often a gain on one objective comes with a loss on the other. Applying these diagnostics to current LLM-agent pipelines exposes a consistent failure mode: the agent performs molecular editing without knowing how the pocket-ligand complex responds to local modifications, thus rarely achieving joint improvement. Inspired by medicinal chemists, who probe the pocket-ligand complex with controlled analog edits before choosing an optimization direction, we propose \textbf{PROBE}, an optimization framework built around edit--response probing. PROBE first decomposes the ligand into editable sites and builds a pocket-specific \textbf{site map} that flags where joint gains are plausible, where the two objectives are likely in tension, and where liability substructures should be changed; it then performs controlled probe edits whose responses are distilled into an \textbf{EditManual}. Guided by the site map and EditManual, PROBE runs an iterative multi-agent optimization loop. On the CrossDocked2020 benchmark, PROBE achieves state-of-the-art performance and substantially mitigates the failure modes exposed by our diagnostics metrics. Codes are in https://github.com/yzf-code/PROBE/tree/main

LGMT: A Tool for Testing the Logical Reasoning Reliability of LLMs 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly serve as reasoning components in modern software systems, yet a model that answers a logical query correctly can contradict itself when the same problem is restated in an equivalent form. Static benchmarks evaluate queries in isolation and therefore fail to expose such inconsistencies. To address this limitation, we present LGMT, an open-source tool that detects reasoning inconsistencies through metamorphic relations grounded in first-order logic. Given a source test case in premise-conclusion form, LGMT generates logically equivalent follow-up cases and checks whether the model's entailment predictions agree across the pair, without ground-truth labels. The tool provides 20 built-in metamorphic relations, supports custom ones verified by the Z3 theorem prover, and runs from a CLI or a web studio, including an offline mock mode requiring no API key. Across six widely used LLMs on a bundled benchmark of 3,091 metamorphic groups, LGMT reveals inconsistency rates ranging from 20.84% to 29.44%. LGMT is publicly available on GitHub at https://github.com/MaxwelsDonc/lgmt-tool, with a demonstration video at https://youtu.be/gou4opptu1U.

From Uncertain Judgments to Calibrated Rankings: Conformal Elo Estimation for LLM Evaluation 2026-10-23
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Evaluating new large language models typically requires costly human annotation campaigns at scale. LLM-as-a-judge offers a cheaper alternative, but judge scores carry systematic errors -- such as position bias, self-preference, or intransitivity -- that can strongly miscalibrate the resulting rankings. We quantify the resulting judge-human disagreement at two complementary levels. At the local level, we estimate per-battle uncertainty from the judge's own score differences by propagating calibrated win probabilities rather than hard labels into the Bradley-Terry procedure. This alone provides a drastic improvement to Elo estimation accuracy, bringing LLM-derived ratings within 17.9 Elo MAE of human-derived ones when averaged over 55 held-out models on LMArena. At the global level, we apply split conformal prediction to the residual gap between LLM-derived and human-derived Elo ratings across held-out models, producing prediction intervals with distribution-free marginal coverage guarantees that account for irreducible LLM-human disagreement. Together, these two layers yield a low-cost evaluation tool that provides developers with calibrated Elo estimates and honest uncertainty bounds, without access to large-scale human annotations.

Automated Multi-Line Bug Repair Using Model Checker Aided LLMs: A Case Study on RISC-V Processors 2026-10-23
Show abstract

Functional verification of Register-Transfer Level(RTL) designs has become a critical bottleneck in modern semiconductor development, often consuming the majority of project resources. Debugging, in particular, remains an especially time-consuming task, requiring careful analysis of simulation traces or counterexamples. Recent work has explored applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to RTL debug and repair with great promise. However, the existing approaches typically are for single-line bugs, operate in open-loop, rely heavily on prompts or retrieval, lack scalability, and often produce incorrect fixes. In this paper, we propose a novel automated RTL bug repair methodology that combines LLMs, assertions, and a model checker to provide scalable, trustworthy semantic grounding and formal guarantees. Concretely, the methodology leverages failing assertions to guide the LLM in generating patches for multi-line bugs. Each candidate patch is formally verified using a model checker (JasperGold), ensuring only valid repairs are accepted. We evaluate the efficacy and scalability of our methodology on the BRISC-V RISC-V core using mutation testing, introducing several mutation types, and demonstrate that our methodology can repair up to 98% of single-line RTL bugs and 93% of the multi-line bugs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to show automated, LLM-assisted RTL multi-line bug repair results on a complex RISC-V processor design in a scalable manner.

Political sense-making in "individual" human-technology-world relations: the case of LLM-generated autobiographies 2026-10-23
📝 Reference: Longo, A., and Kudina, O. (in press). "Political sense-making in 'individual' human-technology-world relations: the case of LLM-generated autobiographies." In D. Michelfelder, & J. Kennedy (Eds.), Postphenomenology Meets Artificial Intelligence. Bloomsbury Academic
Show abstract

As AI systems increasingly mediate how people describe, recognize, and present themselves, their politics cannot be reduced to what remains hidden inside the "black box." This chapter argues that AI's political significance emerges within technologically, intersubjectively, and temporally mediated interpretive practices. Bringing Arendt's political philosophy into conversation with Gadamerian hermeneutics, the chapter develops a postphenomenological relational-temporal account of political sense-making across Human-Technology-World relations. Against the charge that postphenomenology remains individualistic and using the case of LLM-generated biographies, the chapter shows how seemingly private human-LLM encounters are worldly and collective. When users ask systems such as ChatGPT to describe them, they do not simply receive algorithmic representations. They interpret, contest, edit, share, and publicly test these descriptions. In doing so, people move interpretively among affective resonance, friction, public re-articulation, and the sedimentation of meaning. This means that the so-called "micro-level" of postphenomenological analysis is already saturated with publicity: affective uptake becomes communicable, reflection becomes accountable, and uses sediment into shared standards of appearance.
