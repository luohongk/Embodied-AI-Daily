AltRA-Test: An Alternative Annotator Test for LLM Judges Without Shared Annotators 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judges and graders to replace human annotators, raising the question of when such replacement is statistically justified. To support such decisions, the Alternative Annotator Test (Alt-Test) was introduced for dense settings where all items are annotated by the same annotators. However, in many large-scale annotation settings, items are annotated by different subsets of annotators. We therefore propose the Alternative Annotator Test with Resampled Annotations (AltRA-Test), a statistical test that does not require that all items are annotated by the same annotators. AltRA-Test uses a bootstrap that resamples items, draws k human annotations for each item, and substitutes one of them with an LLM annotation. This produces paired all-human and mixed human-LLM panels. A replacement is supported when the inter-annotator agreement (IAA) of the mixed panel is statistically non-inferior to the IAA of the all-human panel, i.e., when the LLM preserves the signal that the human annotators share. We establish that AltRA-Test is a consistent test of asymptotic level alpha. We then evaluate it through simulations and real-world experiments in which we test 18 LLMs and 5 prompting techniques on 20 datasets across text, image, audio, and video. Across these evaluations, about 100 items often suffice for the test. In simulations, where we know whether the LLM is a better or a worse annotator than the humans, AltRA-Test makes more correct replacement decisions than existing baselines. In real-world data experiments, LLMs pass the test in about half of the setups, depending mainly on the task. Since replacement is not always justified, we encourage researchers to test it for their task.

Proof or Bluff? Evaluating LLMs on 2025 USA Math Olympiad 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Recent mathematical benchmarks indicate that large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance in mathematical competitions such as AIME, with leading models attaining scores comparable to or exceeding those of top human participants. However, these benchmarks evaluate models solely based on final numerical answers, neglecting rigorous reasoning and proof generation which are essential for real-world mathematical tasks. To address this, we introduce a comprehensive evaluation of full-solution reasoning for challenging mathematical problems. Using expert human annotators, we evaluated several state-of-the-art reasoning models on the six problems from the 2025 USAMO within hours of their release. Our results show that all tested models struggle significantly, with none exceeding a score of 30%, and most achieving only trivial scores below 5%. Through a detailed analysis of reasoning traces, we identify the most common failure modes and find several unwanted artifacts arising from the optimization strategies employed during model training. Overall, our results underscore the limitations of current LLMs in tasks requiring deep mathematical understanding and emphasize the need for significant advances in reasoning and proof generation capabilities.

Routeability Before Routing: Routeability Audit Protocol (RAP) and RouteabilityBench for Audited LLM Model Selection 2026-11-25
📝 24 pages. Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

LLM routing aims to choose, for each prompt, which model in a portfolio is most likely to answer correctly. This only makes sense when the benchmark contains enough prompt-specific variation for different models to be best on different inputs. On coarse-scored LLM benchmarks, however, many models often tie for the best score, and the single best model may already leave little room for improvement. We therefore propose a routeability-first audit, instantiated as RAP and RouteabilityBench, a runnable claim-verification package. The audit is retrospective: it runs once the benchmark-portfolio score matrix exists and before router comparisons are interpreted as general evidence, rather than certifying a portfolio that has not yet been evaluated. Across four benchmarks and 66 LLMs, generic routing enhancements such as larger embedding sets, dimensionality reduction, selective abstention, clustering, and stronger regressors do not consistently outperform the single best model. The clearest positive evidence is concentrated in portfolio-dependent Omni-MATH settings, while GPQA, MMLU-Pro, and IFEval show different ways in which routing can remain hard or predictor-sensitive. Our practical recommendation is that routing gains should be reported together with routeability diagnostics, tied-best analysis, portfolio construction details, and family-corrected statistical tests.

MathArena: Evaluating LLMs on Uncontaminated Math Competitions 2026-11-25
Show abstract

The rapid advancement of reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs) has led to notable improvements on mathematical benchmarks. However, many of the most commonly used evaluation datasets (e.g., AIME 2024) are widely available online, making it difficult to disentangle genuine reasoning from potential memorization. Furthermore, these benchmarks do not evaluate proof-writing capabilities, which are crucial for many mathematical tasks. To address this, we introduce MathArena, a new benchmark based on the following key insight: recurring math competitions provide a stream of high-quality, challenging problems that can be used for real-time evaluation of LLMs. By evaluating models as soon as new problems are released, we effectively eliminate the risk of contamination. Using this framework, we find strong signs of contamination in AIME 2024. Nonetheless, evaluations on harder competitions, such as CMIMC 2025, demonstrate impressive reasoning capabilities in top-performing models. MathArena is also the first benchmark for proof-writing capabilities. On IMO 2025, top models achieve slightly less than 40%, demonstrating both notable progress and significant room for improvement. So far, we have evaluated over $50$ models across seven competitions, totaling $162$ problems. As an evolving benchmark, MathArena will continue to track the progress of LLMs on newly released competitions, ensuring rigorous and up-to-date evaluation of mathematical reasoning.

What Is a Theory Worth to an LLM Solver? Measuring, Inducing, and Merging Theories in Context 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Mathematicians solve problems faster when they know the right theory. We ask how much a stated theory is worth to a large language model (LLM) solver, and whether a model can write that theory itself. In a length-matched protocol, adding the right theory to the context of Qwen3-14B raises pass@1 on pattern waiting-time problems from 0.37 to 0.83 and cuts the median tokens of correct solutions from 10.2k to 6.3k, while worked examples of the same length do not help. The gain shrinks as the solver and its token budget grow (+0.46, +0.26, +0.07), and at a long budget it comes almost entirely from finishing within budget: solutions that finish are correct 98--100\% of the time in every condition. To separate induction from recall, we plant unpublished theorems in custom probability families and measure how much of the oracle theory's gain an induced theory recovers. A strong open model, required to explore the training problems with code before writing, recovers 60\% of the gain on one planted family, 48\% on a second, and almost none on a third, where it finds the right form but not the condition that makes the form computable. No single induced note is complete, but a strong model that merges several notes recovers 61\% of the gain on the classic family, above the best single note (47\%). Theories are worth the reasoning they save, and models can find their pieces before they can assemble them.

SOLVE: Sparse, Ordered Keyposes Make LLM Agents Versatile and Efficient Robot Manipulator 2026-11-25
Show abstract

LLM agents have made remarkable progress in robot manipulation, yet they suffer from expensive inference, dense step-by-step control, and tool bottlenecks. We introduce SOLVE, a training-free, versatile and efficient agent policy: a multimodal LLM agent writes a sparse, ordered sequence of 6-DoF end-effector keyposes per call, observes the result, and writes the next, while a motion planner interpolates between them. The agent decides the number and spacing of keyposes for both fast long-range motion and fine-grained manipulation. This granularity matches the sparsity of teleoperated manipulation: most demonstrations in DROID can be compressed into a few keyposes instead of dense actions. SOLVE grounds keyposes with sparse depth queries answered by noisy sources, such as multi-view triangulation, stereo, or monocular depth, and copes with that noise itself. It also improves from execution feedback, reflecting on its own rollouts and distilling a few rules per task. SOLVE reaches state-of-the-art performance of 74.5% on RoboDojo and 84.5% on RoboLab, with only $1.7 per episode on average, 12x less than previous dense-control agent policies. On a real robot, the same agent achieves an 88% success rate (75 of 85 episodes) over 16 challenging zero-shot tasks. Our project page is at https://serenos.github.io/solve-website.

How Much is Left? LLMs Linearly Encode Their Remaining Output Length 2026-11-25
📝 NeurIPS 2026 camera-ready version. 19 pages, 6 figures. Code: https://github.com/mamerzouk/llm-output-length
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

Large Language models generate tokens, without explicitly keeping track of the length of the generated text. This paper asks the question: can we estimate the number of tokens remaining in a generated response, using just a linear probe on the model's hidden states? Previous work on inference scheduling established that length is statistically predictable, using nonlinear methods. This paper contributes a linear representation method, using frozen hidden states of 7-8B instruction-tuned models, across seven datasets. We show that total completion length can be estimated, with performance stronger than statistical baselines, and stronger than the fair comparison to the prediction at the last token of the prompt. On the natural-language datasets, the probe's error lies between 0.57 and 0.88 of the statistical baseline's. Interestingly, when a retraction token occurs ("Wait"), the probe predicted length jumps upward by over 70 tokens, on average, compared to the baseline, where it drops by 0.7, on average. Moreover, the direction found can be used for length steering: adding to the probe direction increases completion length and vice-versa, on Llama-3.1-8B / GSM8K. These results indicate that LLMs have a linearly decodable internal representation of output length.

VirusCascade: Hijacking Collaborative Reflection in LLM-Powered Recommender Agents 2026-11-25
📝 Accepted at NDSS 2027
Show abstract

Advancing beyond traditional static scoring models, LLM-powered agentic recommender systems (LLM-ARS) instantiate users and items as autonomous agents, whose semantic states are dynamically refined through a recurrent process known as collaborative reflection. While this mechanism improves recommendation quality, it simultaneously introduces a systemic vulnerability: adversarial evidence injected into a single agent can be rationalised into a legitimate preference narrative, written back into memory, and propagated to other agents through interaction contexts. We term the local rationalisation process reflection laundering, and its system-wide escalation through collaborative reflection collaborative-reflection hijacking. Existing attacks on recommender systems, whether based on interaction-level data poisoning or text-level adversarial perturbations, assume static pipelines and thus cannot exploit this recurrent, multi-agent amplification pathway. To bridge this gap, we first conduct a controlled vulnerability analysis that establishes two exploitable properties underlying collaborative-reflection hijacking: reflective persistence and cross-agent propagation. Then building on these findings, we propose VirusCascade, the first black-box targeted promotion attack that jointly shapes semantic and structural attack surfaces: the former ensures the target item is naturally rationalised as satisfying broad user preferences, the latter positions it for system-wide propagation. Extensive experiments on four real-world datasets across diverse LLM-ARS architectures demonstrate that VirusCascade consistently achieves state-of-the-art targeted exposure under evaluated stealth constraints, reaching a mean E@20 of 0.384 and surpassing the strongest baseline by an absolute margin of +0.185.

The Open Proof Corpus: A Large-Scale Study of LLM-Generated Mathematical Proofs 2026-11-25
Show abstract

In recent months, large language models (LLMs) have made significant progress in mathematical proof generation, but further advancement is hindered by the lack of a large-scale, high-quality dataset of human-evaluated proofs. While expensive to create, such a dataset is essential for driving improvements in training and enabling a rigorous analysis of proof generation capabilities. In this work, we present the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a dataset comprising over 5,000 human-evaluated proofs produced by state-of-the-art LLMs. The OPC was specifically designed for broad applicability and downstream usage in proof generation research and is the first to include a substantial number of correct, LLM-generated solutions to problems from prestigious mathematics competitions such as the USAMO and IMO. Using the OPC, we explore critical questions in automated proof generation: (1) the performance gap between natural language and formal proof generation, (2) the discrepancy between final-answer accuracy and full-proof validity, and (3) the impact of best-of-n selection on proof quality. Finally, to showcase the utility of the OPC, we finetune an 8B-parameter model on the dataset, obtaining a model that performs on par with the best model, Gemini-2.5-Pro, on the task of evaluating proof correctness.

A Differential Computing Accelerator for LLM Inference Leveraging Bit-Slice Transition Sparsity 2026-11-25
📝 15 pages, 16 figues
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have become foundational to modern artificial intelligence. Driven by scaling laws, their rapid growth poses increasing computational and memory challenges for deployment. To accelerate the general matrix multiplication (GEMM) operations that dominate LLM computation, bit-slice architectures decompose quantized operands along the bit dimension, exposing slice-level sparsity and redundancy. However, existing bit-slice designs primarily exploit sparsity in high-order slices, while dense low-order slices still offer substantial untapped reuse opportunities. We observe that bit-slice vectors (BSVs) are highly clustered in Hamming space, with many BSVs lying only a few bit flips away from a small set of representative patterns. Based on this observation, we propose ISTS, an algorithm-hardware co-designed accelerator that leverages representative BSV patterns as shared Steiner points to reuse partial sums across slices, substantially reducing computation. Specifically, the partial sum of each target BSV can be reconstructed from that of its assigned Steiner point using only a few additions or subtractions. On the algorithmic side, we propose Steiner Absorption Table Construction (SATC), which selects a compact set of Steiner points to absorb BSVs with only a few bidirectional bit flips, while FlipPrune selectively prunes flips to further expand the reusable BSV population. On the hardware side, we design a lightweight accelerator that supports Steiner reuse for static linear layer weights and runtime-generated attention K/V tensors. Comprehensive evaluations show that ISTS achieves average speedups of $3.09\times$, $2.41\times$, $2.18\times$, $1.27\times$, and $1.41\times$ over the state-of-the-art accelerators ANT, BBS, LUT Tensor Core, Transitive Array, and MCBP-BRCR, respectively.

Do We Really Need an LLM for Weight-Space Programming? A Deterministic Forward-Only Paradigm and Its Capability Boundaries 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Recent work on Weight-Space Programming uses LLMs to edit neural weights with forward passes only, conflating LLM scripting with the underlying algebra of forward-only editing. We show that for core structured editing regimes, an LLM optimizer is unnecessary, and introduce Forward Weight-Space Programming (FWSP): a deterministic pipeline combining probing, localization, stratified operators (O1-O4), automatic blind routing (83.3% vs. 33.3% for a 27B LLM with the same tool library), and paired verification with zero additional per-token computation. For reasoning LLMs, we propose Protected FisherGate, a closed-form covariance-whitened readout edit orthogonal to normal-reasoning states: on DeepSeek-R1-0528-8B, a single frozen math-calibrated patch achieves 84.2% HumanEval pass@1 (1K budget; 75.4 +/- 9.4% across 3/5 Stage-4-passing subsets), beating 512-token-reserved budget forcing by +14.6 pp (-23.4% tokens) while transferring across HF-to-vLLM backends on full GSM8K and MATH-500. Across five modalities and thirteen models (50K to 27B dense and FP8 MoE), we further prove and verify two operator boundaries, subspace entanglement and frozen representation span, where FWSP-localized LoRA bridges forward-only and gradient adaptation.

Polyrating: A Cost-Effective and Bias-Aware Rating System for LLM Evaluation 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Rating-based human evaluation has become an essential tool to accurately evaluate the impressive performance of large language models (LLMs). However, current rating systems suffer from several important limitations: first, they fail to account for biases that significantly influence evaluation results, second, they require large and expensive preference datasets to obtain accurate ratings, and third, they do not facilitate meaningful comparisons of model ratings across different tasks. To address these issues, we introduce Polyrating, an expressive and flexible rating system based on maximum a posteriori estimation that enables a more nuanced and thorough analysis of model performance at lower costs. Polyrating can detect and quantify biases affecting human preferences, ensuring fairer model comparisons. Further, Polyrating can reduce the cost of human evaluations by up to $59\%$ for new models and up to $77\%$ for new tasks by leveraging existing benchmark scores. Lastly, Polyrating enables direct comparisons of ratings across different tasks, providing a comprehensive understanding of an LLMs' strengths, weaknesses, and relative performance across different applications.

SVDP: Training-Free Contextual Sparsity Predictors for Fast LLM Inference 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Contextual sparsity is one of the approaches used to reduce computational complexity in the inference process of large language models (LLMs). Existing techniques for efficient LLM inference acceleration based on contextual sparsity with minimal accuracy degradation require training sparse pattern predictors. This paper presents a framework for accelerating inference of ReGLU-based feed-forward networks (FFNs) within LLMs. The proposed framework provides a fast, training-free method for building sparse pattern predictors using truncation-aware singular value decomposition (SVD) of the gate projection matrix, along with a threshold calibration algorithm, and inference executors supporting conditional computation on CUDA and CANN devices. ACTR: Aligning Thoughts and Responses for Multilingual Safety in Reasoning LLMs 2026-11-25
Show abstract

Ensuring the safety of reasoning large language models (LLMs) across languages is essential for their reliable deployment. However, when exposed to jailbreak attacks in non-high-resource languages, these models may generate unsafe responses even when their reasoning traces identify safety risks. To address this issue, we propose aligning cross-lingual thoughts and responses (ACTR), a framework that improves multilingual safety alignment by strengthening the use of existing safety reasoning. Specifically, we first present the think gap score (TGS) to compare the normalized contributions of reasoning traces to attention outputs during response generation across languages, and use reasoning- trace substitution to measure the cross-lingual safety gap. Next, using a corpus of jailbreak queries, we assess neuron importance through changes in response representations caused by neuron masking and compare the high-importance neuron sets obtained with reasoning enabled and disabled to identify safety think neurons that support the use of safety reasoning. Finally, we devise neuron-selective consistency optimization (NSCO), which uses a frozen judge model to reward agreement between the safety categories of reasoning traces and responses while updating only the parameters associated with the selected neurons, requiring no human-annotated responses or preference data. Across two reasoning models, ACTR achieves lower average attack success rates than the evaluated state-of-the-art methods on AdvBench-X and MultiJail, with safety gains extending to unseen languages, while preserving or improving average performance on multilingual knowledge and mathematical reasoning tasks and limiting false refusals of benign requests. RepSelect: Robust LLM Unlearning via Representation Selectivity 2026-11-25
📝 Accepted to EACL 2027 (main conference)
Show abstract

When LLM weights are open or fine-tuning is available through an API, suppressing hazardous knowledge and tendencies is not enough: removal has to be deep enough that an adversary cannot restore it. Existing unlearning is shallow by this standard: fine-tuning or a handful of in-context examples brings the behaviour back, and it often degrades general capabilities in the process. We identify a root cause: existing methods edit representations shared with the retain set and lying in the subspace that a fine-tuning attacker recovers, making unlearning simultaneously easy to undo and disruptive. Leveraging this, we propose RepSelect (Representation Selectivity), which isolates forget-set-specific representations by collapsing the top principal components of the weight gradients before each unlearning update, preserving general capabilities while limiting what fine-tuning can recover. Across five unlearning datasets spanning both knowledge (biohazard, cyber, facts about real individuals) and tendencies (abusive, sycophantic), and three model families covering dense and Mixture-of-Experts architectures, RepSelect yields a 4-40x larger drop in post-relearning answer probability than five widely used baselines (GradDiff, NPO, SimNPO, RMU, UNDIAL). It is also near-perfectly robust to few-shot prompting. Our results show that unlearning needs to be selective about which representations it edits.

Any-Rate: Half-Bit Multi-Precision LLM Quantization with Coded Bit-Planes 2026-11-25
📝 41 pages, 17 figures. (Equal contribution : Hoonki Lee and Haechan Shin)
Show abstract

Nested multi-precision quantization derives multiple precisions from a single checkpoint by reading only the leading bits of each weight, which makes it attractive for on-device deployment under varying memory and energy budgets. However, existing methods offer only integer bits, leaving large accuracy gaps between adjacent precisions and severe degradation at 2 bits. To address this, we propose Any-Rate, which serves 2-8 bits from one checkpoint, in half-bit steps over 2-4 bits. Any-Rate restricts some bit-planes to extended Hamming codewords in 8-weight blocks (0.5 bits per weight), shares one LUT per row across precisions, and encodes all precisions jointly with a separate target for each, reducing the loss from sharing bit-planes. Across four LLMs, Any-Rate achieves state-of-the-art accuracy among multi-precision methods at 2 and 3 bits, keeping 69% of FP16 accuracy at 2 bits, and is within one point of the best at 4 bits. It decodes about as fast as existing kernels, and an LPDDR energy model estimates up to 27% less energy at equal accuracy. Code is available at https://github.com/hoonki2002/Any-Rate.

AgentLens: Graph-guided Security Auditing for LLM-based Agents 2026-11-25
📝 13 pages, 4 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

LLM-based agents are emerging as a key paradigm for automating software engineering by coupling LLM-driven decision-making with external tools, files, APIs, and execution environments. However, weaknesses in agent implementations and insecure interactions between the agent and external resources can lead to severe consequences, including remote code execution, arbitrary file access, SQL injection, and data leakage. This makes pre-deployment auditing essential for systematically identifying implementation weaknesses and validating whether they can manifest as concrete security risks in realistic agent workflows. Existing approaches either focus on specific vulnerability classes or lack the capability to validate whether identified static weaknesses correspond to executable exploits. In this paper, we propose AgentLens, a systematic auditing framework for LLM-based agents. AgentLens analyzes the target agent's implementation, component interactions, and built-in defenses, and organizes them into an Audit Knowledge Graph that captures how these factors interact to expose potential security risks. Based on this graph, AgentLens derives candidate attack paths, generates path-specific validation payloads, and constructs realistic multi-turn audit scenarios that place these payloads in task-relevant contexts to validate whether suspected risks manifest as security-violating behaviors during execution. Across five widely-used agent applications, each with over 24,000 GitHub stars, AgentLens identifies 44 vulnerabilities, and every vulnerability is validated by being triggered during execution. Compared with the state-of-the-art approach (AgentFuzz), AgentLens detects 3.67$\times$ more vulnerabilities. To date, 12 reported issues have been assigned CVE IDs.

Where Is Uncertainty Observed? A Mechanistic Understanding of Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs 2026-11-25
📝 Project Page: https://s2lab.org/UQ-Lenses
Show abstract

Knowing when a large language model's (LLM's) response can be trusted is critical for its safe use. Uncertainty quantification (UQ) can help LLMs recognize if their responses may be unreliable. Existing taxonomies organize UQ methods by uncertainty source or estimation mechanism. They leave unresolved the question of where uncertainty is observed in the model's computation. The answer matters because the observation point determines whether an estimator has access to enough information to recover a given target, and how well it can recover it. Drawing on mechanistic interpretability, we define four mechanistic observation lenses: the structural lens observes component updates, the representational lens observes hidden states, the distributional lens observes token probabilities, and the semantical lens observes distributions over meanings. We relate these lenses through the residual stream and order them by the information they carry, which gives a principle for selecting UQ methods for different targets. We survey existing methods through the four lenses. Furthermore, we distinguish evaluation targets, criteria, and metrics to clarify different UQ evaluation protocols. Finally, we identify open problems and research directions to connect uncertainty estimation to the internal computation and downstream use of LLMs.

Crucible: Evaluating LLM Agents in Executable Chemistry Research Environments 2026-11-25

PROVE: Mitigating Long-Horizon LLM Collusion with Reinforcement Learning from Verified Evidence 2026-11-25
Show abstract

When LLM agents verify each other's work over a long interaction, they drift into collusion: they come to accept work without the evidence that the verification protocol requires. Collusion is absent at the start and builds up episode by episode, which we trace to accurate feedback accumulating in memory. An agent that follows the rule is told that its verdict was wrong whenever its peer was right, so breaking the rule is the reward-maximizing choice, and agents talk themselves into it in their private reflections. Existing mitigations leave the agents unchanged; we train the behavior out. PROVE (Protocol Reinforcement from Verified Evidence) is a multi-agent reinforcement learning method that rewards verdicts by the evidence behind them rather than by their outcome, and credits every call an agent makes, including the calls that solve its own task, with the outcome that call controls. Unlocking the Power of Loops in Pretrained LLMs with Loop Residuals 2026-11-24
Show abstract

Looped Transformers iterate a shared block of layers to increase computation without adding parameters, offering stronger performance and test-time scaling. However, this power has so far required training from scratch and yielded only modest gains for LLMs. Finetuning existing models into loops is far cheaper, yet also brings only marginal gains over SFT. We propose LoopRes, an effective, scalable, and simple approach that turns loops into significant and consistent gains for pretrained LLMs. Building on Attention Residuals, LoopRes lets each looped layer attend directly to the outputs of earlier loops. Since these outputs are iterates of the same layers and become harder to tell apart, we give each a trainable loop-aware key that encodes its structural identity. Attending to all earlier loops also lets layers skip intermediate ones, so we restrict each layer to a residual window of the latest W loops, forcing every loop to build incrementally on recent ones. Across 6 pretrained LLMs from 3 model families and 9 benchmarks, LoopRes delivers significant and consistent gains: at 2x compute, it improves Qwen3-1.7B-Base by 29.1% over SFT, more than doubling the gain of the state-of-the-art baseline. The gains hold on larger models up to 30B and across all three families. More importantly, from 1.7B to 8B, LoopRes scales more steeply than SFT as compute grows, reaching a higher ceiling while needing less compute on long outputs. These results establish LoopRes as an effective looping approach, delivering both better performance and better test-time scaling.
