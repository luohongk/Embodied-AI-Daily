Can LLMs Uncover Hidden Physical Laws through Free Exploration? 2026-10-27
📝 https://zhenyangcs.github.io/LongChang-website/
Show abstract

Scientific discovery often begins with an unexpected observation that prompts a new research question. In a world with altered physical laws, can large language models (LLMs) notice unexpected behavior, such as heavier objects falling more slowly, and discover the law through experiments without being told what to investigate? To evaluate this ability, we introduce LongChang, an automated framework that creates simulated worlds with altered physics, where one or more laws differ from ordinary physics. The evaluated LLMs act as explorers, manipulating objects and building tools to investigate their own questions without research tasks or hints about these laws. Separate LLM agents generate and review scenes, review tool designs, and evaluate reports for correctness and experimental support using the altered physical laws and exploration records. These records guide scene evolution for further exploration, allowing the full cycle to run without human intervention. Among 12 LLMs, GPT-6-Astra achieves a discovery rate of 16.7%, while the other 11 score 0%. The exploration records reveal uninvestigated anomalies and only partial identification of the altered physical laws.

Rare, Relevant, and Still Wrong: How Memory Fails LLM Group Decisions 2026-10-27
Show abstract

When an LLM group's discussion is compressed into a short memory for a final decision maker, what should the memory keep? Two natural heuristics keep the rarest claims or the claims relevant to the decision criteria. On hidden-profile tasks both look attractive, because how widely a fact is held is confounded with whether it helps. We introduce CrossProfile, a diagnostic benchmark of 60 templates with automatically verified answer keys, in which the ownership of supporting, decoy, and irrelevant facts is crossed while their text is held fixed (480 tasks). Across Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and two DeepSeek models, both heuristics fail in predictable places. Selecting by relevance readmits decoys, because decoys are on topic: even a perfect relevance filter loses 23 to 31 points to rarest-first selection in the classic hidden-profile configuration. Rarest-first selection is no better than random selection on average and wins only where the rare facts happen to be the useful ones. In both cases accuracy follows the balance of evidence the memory keeps, not the policy that kept it. The decision model adds a third failure, a form of social proof: it gives extra weight to facts attributed to many agents. Removing the attribution tags, or reassigning them at random with line order fixed, raises accuracy in the classic configuration by 13 to 24 points. In natural discussions the evidence is lost before any memory acts: the complete transcript yields lower accuracy than no notes at all.

SCI-Defense: Detecting Persuasion-Structured Manipulation of LLM-Based Ranking in Generative Engines 2026-10-27
📝 13 pages, 5 figures, 14 tables
Show abstract

Generative engine optimization (GEO) has reshaped how sellers compete for visibility in LLM-generated rankings. By embedding persuasive arguments in the content they control, sellers can influence how generative engines rank products and web pages and push their own items to the top. Existing defenses either detect harmful intent or unnatural language, which fluent GEO content often lacks, or rewrite all candidates, which alters legitimate listings without identifying the manipulated ones. However, these approaches overlook the persuasive structure through which GEO attacks influence LLM-based rankings. To address this gap, we present SCI-Defense, a framework for detecting persuasion-structured ranking manipulation, in which an LLM judge scores five persuasion devices grounded in research on influence and deception: authority, social proof, scarcity and urgency, comparative claims, and narrative purposiveness. Two lightweight gates for unnatural strings and inflated relevance complement the judge, and flagged candidates are demoted rather than rewritten, each with a record of the candidate and the criterion that triggered the flag. On 1,152 optimized CORE attacks across six Amazon product categories and six MS MARCO web domains, SCI-Defense detects 89.6% of reasoning attacks and 96.2% of review attacks at a 1.2% false positive rate and blocks 96.1% of the attacks that pushed their target into the top three. On a 120-query subset, paraphrasing and truncation block a similar share of attacks but push 4.0--4.5% of honest items out of the top three, compared with 0.6% for SCI-Defense.

Orthogonal and Parametrized Multi-Attribute Steering of LLMs 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Activation steering offers an effective and efficient way to modify an LLM's behavior at inference time. However, it remains unclear how to apply steering such that the signal is consistently strong and the steering strength has a universally interpretable meaning. We address this first by identifying the most steerable interval for each steering vector and rescaling the steering range accordingly. Building on this, we introduce a more interpretable method for steering, called parameterized steering which instead of pushing activations in a direction, it sets them at the desired trait strength. Next, with multi-trait steering, we find that naively combining vectors causes them to interfere with one another. In response, we develop a method to orthogonalize the vectors; it decouples these traits, allowing a model to exhibit one behavior without inducing the others, thus enabling multiple traits to be steered simultaneously.

EffiBench-X: A Multi-Language Benchmark for Measuring Efficiency of LLM-Generated Code 2026-10-27
📝 NeurIPS 2025 DB Track
Show abstract

Existing code generation benchmarks primarily evaluate functional correctness, with limited focus on code efficiency and often restricted to a single language like Python. To address this gap, we introduce EffiBench-X, the first multi-language benchmark designed to measure the efficiency of LLM-generated code. EffiBench-X supports Python, C++, Java, JavaScript, Ruby, and Golang. It comprises competitive programming tasks with human-expert solutions as efficiency baselines. Evaluating state-of-the-art LLMs on EffiBench-X reveals that while models generate functionally correct code, they consistently underperform human experts in efficiency. LLMs show better efficiency in Python, Ruby, and JavaScript than in Java, C++, and Golang. For instance, DeepSeek-R1's Python code is significantly more efficient than its Java code. These results highlight the critical need for research into LLM optimization techniques to improve code efficiency across diverse languages. The dataset and evaluation infrastructure are submitted and available at https://github.com/EffiBench/EffiBench-X.git and https://huggingface.co/datasets/EffiBench/effibench-x.

Request-Adaptive Execution for Efficient Hybrid LLM Inference 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Hybrid large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising way to combine the modeling capability of the Attention mechanism with the efficiency of state space models (SSMs). However, most existing hybrid LLMs still inherit a static design philosophy: once the Attention-to-SSM layout is chosen, every request must follow the same hybrid execution. This one-layout-fits-all design is inefficient for real-world serving workloads, where different requests rely on contextual information in different ways. This paper presents Morph, a serving system that co-designs the model architecture and runtime for request-adaptive hybrid LLM inference. Its model architecture pairs Attention and SSM layers within each block and uses prefill-oriented gating to select a request-specific layout that remains fixed throughout decoding. Its serving runtime preserves batching efficiency under divergent layer choices through selective batching and enables correct prefix reuse through hybrid-aware context caching. Experiments on six datasets show that Morph achieves a better quality-efficiency trade-off than existing static hybrid baselines. System-level evaluation further shows that Morph improves the serving throughput by up to 3.87x over baselines while substantially reducing inference latency.

📝 GitHub: https://github.com/peng-weihan/HEART-BENCH
Show abstract

LLM agents in interactive entertainment and social applications, including companions, role-play characters, and persona clones, need decisions grounded in personality and past experiences. We introduce HEART-Bench to evaluate memory-grounded persona-consistent response selection. The benchmark combines 11 Big Five-anchored synthetic characters, each with 1,000 autobiographical memories, and 64 DIAMONDS-guided decision scenarios into 673 multiple-choice questions. Domain experts independently compare candidate responses, with a senior expert resolving disagreements and revising responses when needed; other characters' adjudicated responses to the same scenario serve as distractors. Memory-content ablations show the importance of retrieving experiences that match both the target character and the decision context.

The Envelope Layer: An Underestimated Surface of LLM Agent Defense 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Prior work framed the envelope layer as the channel through which malicious content enters an agent, and established it as a defense surface independent of the LLM layer and the agent layer; however, that work only tested the envelope layer briefly and did not examine how label strength, envelope authenticity, scaffold differences, or existing memories affect defense effectiveness. Following its GDA Measurement methodology and the A/B/C/D outcome taxonomy, this paper takes the A2A-TIBA attack as the test vehicle, uses the semantic breach rate as the primary indicator with the semantic refusal rate as its complementary reference, and characterizes the security boundary of the envelope layer through a two-by-two factorial experiment and a memory experiment. The results show that: when the envelope is authentic, strong labels consistently reduce the semantic breach rate, whereas weak labels are not necessarily better than an unlabeled baseline; when the envelope can be forged, the benefit of strong labels is weakened and its direction varies across frontends; and memory has a larger effect than any label manipulation: success memory raises the semantic breach rate while failure memory lowers it, and most combinations that contain both experiences return to the no-memory baseline. Accordingly, this paper refines the envelope layer from a single admission channel into two levels, an admission channel plus a context assembly surface, and argues that its security boundary is not a fixed property of labels but a dynamic outcome jointly shaped by label claims, frontend permissions, and historical memory.

Uncovering Why LLMs Fail: From Internal Mechanisms to Adversarial Reasoning Trajectories 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are usually evaluated by final-output correctness or safety, yet an endpoint label does not reveal how a failure develops throughout the reasoning process. We therefore ask whether ordinary reasoning failures and adversarially induced failures can be characterized using the same internal coordinates. Towards this end, we in this paper study a white-box diagnostic framework CauTrace to disclose the root-cause of why LLM fails from three fundamental inspections: Functional States, Propagation of Representation, Temporal-State Coordination and Reasoning Trajectory. First, seven functional states are creatively defined to represent the support and constraint processing in LLMs. Second, the propagation of representation change measures how the changes among the seven states persist across subsequent Transformer blocks. Third, temporal-state coordination and trajectory topology characterize when the commitment and verification occur relative to the support, and how the dominant state sequence evolves over the full inference process. Finally, we evaluate CauTrace on both clean reasoning failures and paired adversarial perturbations. Across Qwen and LLaMA-family models, the failed traces show weaker Attention-MLP agreement on support-state updates and less verification activity relative to commitment, while the exact representation-level signatures and timing shifts vary by architecture. Held-out repair and steering tests demonstrate consistent operational signals that were in line with our claims for certain models and specific settings, with aligned steering producing the predicted directional changes in process measures. Overall, CauTrace provides a unified diagnostic framework for analyzing failure dynamics through changes in support, commitment, and verification, which would shed light on the in-depth research on LLM security.

DYNASHIELD: A Black-Box Moving Target Defense for LLMs via Dynamic Decoding Customization 2026-10-27
📝 Accepted by The 29th International Symposium on Research in Attacks, Intrusions and Defenses (RAID) 2026
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks in which adversarial prompts induce harmful outputs. Existing defenses often require access to the model internals or additional training, limiting their applicability for service providers deployed through black-box APIs. In this paper, we propose DYNASHIELD, a moving target defense framework that improves robustness by customizing decoding hyperparameters and system prompts at inference time. DYNASHIELD includes two key steps: (1) it identifies decoding configurations that reduce attack success probability, and (2) it probabilistically samples from a weighted configuration pool to introduce controlled variability in model behavior. We evaluate DYNASHIELD across 7 open-source LLMs under 4 state-of-the-art jailbreak attacks, using adversarial prompts from AdvBench. Because DYNASHIELD operates solely through exposed runtime controls, it requires no retraining or model-internal access. These results suggest that safety-aware dynamic decoding is a promising and practically lightweight defense mechanism for black-box LLM deployments.

Explanations of LLM outputs are considered an important way to make model behavior legible, helping human users make good decisions on downstream tasks. However, default training approaches for improving explanations target model task performance or human preference rather than human decision quality. To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{HD-RL}, a reinforcement learning framework for optimizing LLM explanations for human decision making. AnalogScout: Simulation-Efficient PVT-Aware Analog Sizing with LLM Agents 2026-10-27
📝 5 pages, 5 figures, 1 table. Submitted to IEEE ISCAS 2027
Show abstract

Analog device sizing must satisfy all specifications across process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) corners to ensure tape-out success, but simulating every candidate at all corners dominates the sizing time and slows design iteration. Conventional surrogate-based optimizers search the sizing space directly, while recent large language model (LLM) agents add circuit knowledge to designs but still delegate the sizes to global optimizers. Both learn the circuit response from repeated multi-corner SPICE simulations and optimize on top of it, which becomes expensive under a limited simulation budget. It also attains lower power than Bayesian optimization on all three OTAs and than two re-implemented LLM-driven flows on the two OTAs where they were tested.

EdgePace: Deadline- and Freshness-Aware LLM Scheduling on Unified-Memory Platforms 2026-10-27
📝 15 pages, including the reference and some interesting finds in a four-page appendix
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly run on edge devices alongside tasks that continuously process sensor data. In many applications, these foreground tasks prepare observations as inputs to the LLM, whose outputs guide subsequent processing or actions. The observations must be processed by foreground deadlines, and LLM feedback must arrive while its source observations remain useful. Yet the CPU and accelerators share a unified-memory system, so LLM inference can delay foreground processing through memory-bandwidth contention even when compute resources remain available. We present EdgePace, a bandwidth-aware runtime that protects foreground deadlines while sustaining timely LLM feedback. EdgePace creates safe suspension points across prefill and decode, monitors foreground memory demand and stalls at low cost, and schedules inference according to contention risk, deadline slack, and output freshness. We evaluate EdgePace on a OnePlus 13 and three NVIDIA Jetson platforms using multi-camera processing and robotic target tracking. Relative to unmanaged inference, it reduces foreground deadline misses by 24.1%--96.1% while retaining 64.9%--98.5% of LLM throughput. All measured LLM outputs arrive within their task-defined freshness windows. On OnePlus 13, for example, camera deadline misses fall by 91.3% while 90.4% of LLM throughput is retained.

Local Entropy Fluctuations for Black-Box LLM-Generated Text Detection 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Zero-shot detection of machine-generated text is vital for mitigating the risks of large language models (LLMs) misuse. While the state-of-the-art dual-model detectors and resampling-based detectors outperform single-model statistical detectors on unprompted continuations, they suffer a catastrophic breakdown on instruction-conditioned text in the black-box setting, where the source model is unknown. We analyze this failure and trace it to poor prompt conditioning for resampling-based methods and diminished cross-model discrepancy for dual-model detectors. Motivated by recent studies that human writing maintains a steady, uniform information flow, whereas machine-generated text exhibits sharp, localized entropy fluctuations, we propose a new metric, the Relative Peak Prominence Rate (RelPPR) to capture this structural contrast along the entropy trajectory and further leverage it as a signal for LLM-generated text detection. Where Should the Evaluation Budget Go in LLM-Driven Agent Scaffold Evolution? 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM)-driven agent scaffold evolution improves agent workflows through iterative candidate generation and evaluation. Evaluation both guides subsequent search and determines the final scaffold, but evaluating every candidate on the full task set incurs substantial execution costs. We investigate how evaluation budgets can be allocated across these two roles. Our experiments show that sparse feedback can sustain productive evolution, while additional targeted evaluation improves final selection. Replay analyses further show that shared informative items and a common scoring reference support comparisons among closely matched candidates. Motivated by these findings, we propose \method, an evaluation allocation framework that combines sparse search-time feedback with targeted final confirmation. A short fully evaluated warm-up establishes a shared informative core and calibrates a response model whose item parameters remain fixed during search. After search, \method refits the model and applies paired successive halving, reusing accumulated observations and executing only missing evaluations. Across four benchmarks and two evolution frameworks, \method achieves higher mean performance than full evaluation in most settings while using approximately half as many evaluation cells.

Grounding LLM Agents in Business Processes: Process-Aware Knowledge Graphs for Reliable Multi-Step Service Invocation 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents that call enterprise APIs must select the correct endpoints, follow process order, use valid payload keys, and choose coded values from predefined lists. However, current retrieval-augmented service discovery treats endpoints independently and therefore misses process and schema constraints. We address this limitation by integrating Open Data Protocol (OData) metadata and Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) reference processes in a typed property graph. A hybrid retriever identifies candidate endpoints using dense embeddings and follows process edges to retrieve subsequent endpoints. We evaluate the method on 42 enterprise services with process-chain ground truth. For queries with low lexical overlap between the query and downstream entities, it improves Recall@3 by 5.7 points (n=74), with consistent gains across embedding models and cut-offs. These results show that exposing process and schema constraints during retrieval improves the reliability of agent-driven API calls.

AutoRSE: Evaluating LLM Agents as Recommendation Engineers for Self-Evolving Recommender Systems 2026-10-27
📝 12 pages, 7 figures. Code: https://github.com/Ray-Tiann/AutoRSE
Show abstract

Recommender Systems Engineer Agents (RSE-Agents), which use LLM-based agents to autonomously improve recommendation algorithms, are emerging as a promising paradigm. However, their capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood due to the lack of a unified, standardized evaluation framework and systematic trajectory-level analysis. To address this gap, we introduce AutoRSE, an evaluation framework spanning six representative industrial-grade recommendation tasks, and conduct a large-scale empirical study of three frontier LLMs across three widely used agent harnesses. AgentSentry: Mitigating Indirect Prompt Injection in LLM Agents via Temporal Causal Diagnostics and Context Purification 2026-10-27
📝 Accepted at the Network and Distributed System Security (NDSS) Symposium 2027. 18 pages, 5 figures, 4 tables
Show abstract

Tool-augmented large language model (LLM) agents aggregate dialogue history, tool outputs, and retrieved content into an accumulated runtime state to complete multi-step tasks. This execution model exposes agents to indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks, in which attacker-controlled instructions embedded in external artifacts enter the agent state and later influence tool use. Multi-turn IPI is especially challenging because injected content can persist across steps and trigger unauthorized state-changing actions only after merging with user intent. This creates a source-attribution problem: existing inference-time defenses can often detect suspicious behavior but do not directly distinguish whether a pending action is supported by the user task or influenced by injected content. They therefore resort to conservative blocking, termination, or broad filtering. We propose AgentSentry, an inference-time defense that operationalizes temporal causal diagnostics through boundary-local counterfactual re-executions at tool-return boundaries. It substitutes the mediator under a restored snapshot, records whether the pending proposal survives, and aggregates user-view and probe scores across adjacent boundaries. When a proposal is mediator-sensitive, AgentSentry purifies the implicated mediator content while preserving task-relevant evidence and resumes execution from the sanitized state. We evaluate AgentSentry on AgentDojo across four task suites, three IPI attack families, and three black-box LLM backbones. It records no observed successful attack in the evaluated cases (ASR = 0%) and achieves an average Utility Under Attack (UA) of 76.10%. Over the strongest evaluated baseline, it improves average UA by 1.84--7.60 percentage points, with benign FPRs of 2.06%--5.15%. We further evaluate long-horizon and adaptive attacks, imperfect purification, runtime overhead, and an OpenClaw case study.

Is Your LLM Relay Serving What It Claims? A low-cost and high-power test for LLM Relay Auditing 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Relay and reseller APIs have become a common way to access large language models (LLMs), yet users cannot directly verify whether they receive the models they request and pay for. This opacity motivates LLM relay auditing, but existing methods struggle to combine low cost, robustness to benign deployment changes, and statistically interpretable decisions under black-box access. To address these limitations, we propose distributional fingerprint auditing (DFA), a statistical method that requires only textual responses from suspect and trusted reference endpoints. DFA repeatedly queries semantically simple finite-choice probes and uses the resulting categorical response distributions to construct endpoint fingerprints. It compares the suspect and reference fingerprints probe by probe and aggregates the resulting evidence using the Cauchy combination test, yielding an approximate endpoint-level $p$-value for audit decisions at a prespecified nominal significance level. Using registered fingerprints of candidate models, a likelihood-based formulation further supports model attribution under full substitution and joint estimation of substitute identity and substitution ratio under constant-ratio mixed routing. Our benchmark includes 16 proprietary and open-weight LLMs from 10 model families. Across 240 ordered full-substitution pairs, DFA achieves high detection rates at a nominal significance level of $α=0.05$, using five probes and 250 relay requests per audit with stored reference fingerprints. Configuration overrides can lead to rejection even when the served model remains unchanged, with effects varying across models and configurations. Simulations based on stored empirical fingerprints demonstrate accurate model attribution and estimate substitution ratios, with estimation error decreasing as the audit budget increases.

Blindfold: Keeping the Audit Coin Secret from LLM Agents 2026-10-27
Show abstract

Audit-based certification bounds the violation rate of an LLM agent by auditing a random fraction $\varepsilon$ of actions and reweighting the audited labels; its validity is meant to rest only on the defender's audit coin. We show that it also rests on a confidentiality property: the coin must not interfere with the agent's view before the action commits, at a time the agent chooses. We call this pre-commit noninterference (PNI) and measure its violation by $D_{\mathrm{A}}$, the total-variation distance between the agent's views under the two coin values. PNI is necessary and sufficient for an unbiased certificate against adaptive agents, the worst-case bias is $(1-\varepsilon)D_{\mathrm{A}}$, and no procedure that sees only audited labels can pay less. The Inbox as a Coordination Primitive for LLM Agent Societies 2026-10-26
Show abstract

Language-model agents increasingly coordinate in large groups, and current agent protocols focus on delivering messages. For these agents, however, the scarce resource is not delivery but reading: every message costs tokens, and the context is finite. Current infrastructure works by push: the sender decides when a message enters the recipient's context, so the recipient reads in arrival order and drops the oldest messages once its context is full. We study the alternative, pull, in which messages wait in an inbox outside the context and the recipient's own rules decide what it reads and keeps. We argue that the inbox is a coordination primitive: each agent sets its rules privately, and those rules decide whether other agents' requests reach it. In simulated societies of up to 16,000 agents, with every decision an LLM judgment, we report three findings. First, push fails as an agent takes part in more coordination at once: an agent leading 20 negotiations with a full memory proposes options already rejected 97% of the time, because it has dropped the oldest replies. Second, an inbox is necessary under heavy, noisy traffic. Where first come, first read misses every request, an inbox that first reads messages carrying a refundable deposit still finds about 60% of the agents a request needs, at every size from 1,000 to 16,000. On a real email network, losses compound: when half the agents adopt such an inbox, forwarded search recovers only 5% of full adoption, against 42% for one-hop search. Third, an inbox comprises four decisions (what to read, what to keep, what a sender pays, and what a reply may say), and the best rule for each depends on the traffic and the agent's role. Multi-agent coordination under load requires an inbox layer designed for the traffic each agent receives.

Multi-turn jailbreaks turn dialogue safety into a trace property: a violation may become identifiable only when user intent and assistant behavior are considered across multiple turns. Existing guard models largely classify individual messages or exchanges and return risk scores or labels, without executable safety semantics or a formally checkable decision trail. We present SpecGuard, a training-free, specification-driven framework for monitoring multi-turn LLM interactions. Experiments on multi-turn safety benchmarks show that SpecGuard consistently outperforms representative open-source guard models while maintaining controlled false-positive rates.
