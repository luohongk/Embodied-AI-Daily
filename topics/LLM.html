SatMend: LLM Agents for Automated Software Repair in Satellite Constellations with Intermittent Ground Contact 2026-11-22
Show abstract

Satellite constellations rely on onboard software to sustain services across cooperating spacecraft. Maintaining this software requires handling related component versions and different failure causes while delivering repairs through intermittent ground contact. Automated software repair in satellite constellations remains underexplored, particularly the coordination of repairs across related versions with verified onboard recovery under intermittent ground contact. To address this gap, we propose SatMend, a software repair method driven by ground-based large language model (LLM) agents. SatMend organizes related versions into persistent sessions to retain diagnostic context and combines source and runtime evidence to select code repair or state recovery actions. It validates each version separately and submits ready candidates during continued repair, allowing ground preparation to overlap with onboard delivery and verification. Experimental results show that across configurations with 16 to 64 satellites, SatMend restores 192 of 224 faulty applications within 600 s, compared with 40 for SWE-agent, 77 for Agentless, 124 for RepairAgent, and 115 for SWE-Search. These results demonstrate SatMend's effectiveness in restoring applications and supporting service continuity in satellite constellations with intermittent ground contact.

Tenlai-TTS 2.0: Continuous-Representation LLM-Based TTS System with End-to-End Training 2026-11-22
Show abstract

Autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) with continuous speech latents avoids quantization-induced information loss, but typically relies on a latent space that is learned separately and then frozen before language-model training. Such a space must satisfy two competing requirements: it must retain enough acoustic detail for high-fidelity reconstruction, yet be sufficiently structured and regularized to support autoregressive prediction by a language model. We present Tenlai-TTS 2.0, which learns continuous speech representations through end-to-end training to balance acoustic detail with autoregressive predictability. The model comprises a speech encoder with a capacity-controlled Gaussian bottleneck, a large language model (LLM) with an autoregressive diffusion transformer (AR-DiT) head, a flow-matching (FM) mel-spectrogram decoder, and a multi-task recognition model (RM). With energy normalization and fixed-variance Gaussian noise, the bottleneck has a closed-form upper bound on its information rate. Through joint optimization with reconstruction, recognition, and next-patch prediction objectives, we learn continuous speech representations that suit all three tasks and train the LLM and AR-DiT for autoregressive generation. The LLM and AR-DiT are further optimized using differentiable reconstruction and recognition feedback on their predicted latents through the FM decoder and RM. On Seed-TTS-eval, this feedback-based optimization reduces character error rate (CER) from 0.82% to 0.74% on test-zh and from 8.32% to 7.08% on test-hard-zh, and word error rate (WER) from 1.32% to 1.27% on test-en, compared with joint training alone. Subsequent reinforcement learning further reduces CER to 6.29% on test-hard-zh and WER to 1.08% on test-en, with speaker similarity scores of 0.811, 0.766, and 0.784 on test-zh, test-en, and test-hard-zh, respectively.

IteraSong: LLM-Guided Iterative Symbolic Music Creation in a Web DAW 2026-11-22
📝 AAAI 2027 Demonstration Program (accepted). Extended version with appendix
Show abstract

Music creation is inherently iterative, yet many symbolic generation systems still center on producing or regenerating isolated outputs. We present IteraSong, an interactive web-based Digital Audio Workstation (DAW) for LLM-guided iterative symbolic music creation. Unlike one-shot generation systems, IteraSong supports a generate-audition-edit-refine loop over a persistent multi-track project. Users initialize a fully labeled pop draft, then progressively refine it through natural-language instructions. The LLM maps requests to a hierarchical action space of editing primitives exposed via Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools, spanning global, track, section, and bar/note scopes. The demo enables attendees to edit multi-track compositions in natural language, apply deterministic and generative edits, and undo/redo changes in real time.

Unleashing the Value of Raw Point Clouds for 3D Understanding with LLMs 2026-11-22
📝 12 pages, 4 figures. Tailai Chen and Jiawei Yu contributed equally
Show abstract

Standard 3D point cloud language models typically rely on pretrained point encoders to extract 3D features and project them into representations processable by large language models (LLMs). However, these encoders are often originally optimized for pre-defined tasks, resulting in a representation bottleneck that may lead to a substantial mismatch with downstream 3D understanding. Moreover, existing models largely follow a single pass closed-book generation, limiting their ability to actively inspect task-relevant 3D regions or retrieve necessary external knowledge. In this work, we present PointTool, an encoder-free and tool-augmented framework for 3D reasoning. PointTool introduces Alignment-Free Patch Tokenization (AFPT) to locally aggregate raw point clouds while preserving fine-grained geometric structure, and Decoupled Point-Text LoRA (DPL) to facilitate global aggregation and 3D knowledge internalization within a frozen LLM. Building upon these designs, Program-Supervised Tool Reasoning (PSTR) enables the model to explicitly operate on raw 3D data and external resources through executable tools, supporting interactive 3D reasoning grounded in spatial evidence. We further introduce PointCloud-TR, a benchmark comprising 13K 3D objects, 39.8K questions, and 35.6K tool trajectories to facilitate training and evaluation of complex 3D reasoning. Experiments demonstrate that PointTool achieves strong performance on both standard and complex 3D understanding tasks, validating the effectiveness of the proposed framework.

Medical Specialty Prediction from Bangla Patient Narratives: A Comparative Study of ML, DL, Transformers, and LLMs 2026-11-22
📝 Accepted and presented at the 3rd International Conference on Sustainable Development Through Excellence in Higher Education: Contemporary Global Perspectives
Show abstract

Timely access to appropriate medical specialists remains a bottleneck in Bangladesh's healthcare system, as patients describe symptoms in informal Bangla rather than clinical terms, leading to ineffective referrals. This paper compares four model families for predicting medical specialties from Bangla patient narratives: six machine learning (ML) models using hybrid TF-IDF features, four deep learning (DL) models using Word2Vec embeddings trained on the corpus, four fine-tuned transformers, and five large language models (LLMs) evaluated zero-shot. From a corpus of 3798 patient records covering 20 specialties, we retained the five with the most records (Medicine, Gynecology, Dermatology, Pediatrics, Gastroenterology) and split the resulting 2730 records 80:20 into training and test sets shared by all families. The best transformer was XLM-RoBERTa (86.26% accuracy, 86.21% macro-F1), the best ML model was SVM (84.98%, 84.92%), the best DL model was Bidirectional GRU (80.77%, 80.83%), and the best LLM was Gemini (90.29%, 90.15%). Holm-corrected paired McNemar tests show SVM significantly outperforming Bi-LSTM (adjusted p = 0.004), whereas the Gemini-versus-Grok gap does not survive correction (adjusted p = 0.104), and neither the strongest transformer versus the weakest LLM (Grok) nor SVM versus the strongest transformer reaches significance. A tuned classical model is thus statistically indistinguishable from the strongest transformer, so accuracy rankings alone should not be read as evidence of one family's superiority. The findings provide a comparative baseline for Bangla medical text classification.

When Does Decomposition Help? Empirical Study on Workload-Architecture Affinity in LLM Inference Serving 2026-11-22
📝 8 pages
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) inference can be organized as independent monolithic replicas, tensor- or pipeline-parallel serving groups, or disaggregated prefill and decode services. Although decomposition is often presented as a route to higher efficiency, its benefit depends on workload shape, model fit, and whether phase-specific resources can be independently scaled. Existing comparisons frequently change several of these factors at once. We isolate the architectural effect by evaluating five SGLang serving organizations under the same four-A100-40GB hardware envelope, pinned Qwen3-8B model, software stack, prompts, and arrival schedules. Our evaluation contains 150 audited conditions over short-chat, decode-heavy, and 32K prefill-heavy workloads. Four independent one-GPU replicas achieve the lowest end-to-end latency for short chat and long output and are the only architecture stable in every condition. Pipeline parallelism reduces 32K time to first token by 58-59% at non-stress rates, while hybrid pipeline-based disaggregation achieves the best 32K end-to-end latency at 0.4 requests/s. In contrast, B's single-VM static TP2+TP2 split, without independent phase scaling, does not improve tested latency over replication and collapses at higher 32K load. These results reveal a workload-architecture affinity: pipeline decomposition favors prefill-heavy computation, whereas replication favors decode-heavy and concurrent traffic. More broadly, decomposition is not a monotonic optimization; its value depends on matching parallelism granularity to the dominant phase and preserving queue stability.

Do LLM Agents Compromise Like Humans? Risk and Responsibility in Dyadic Decision-Making 2026-11-21
📝 22 pages, 8 figures. Accepted at SocialAgent: Second Workshop on Large Language Models for Social Reasoning and Simulation, NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM)-based agents are increasingly deployed in settings where they must collaborate to make joint decisions. We investigate how this collaboration unfolds under risk, asking whether agents adjust their choices to accommodate their partners, how they assign responsibility for shared outcomes, and whether responsibility attribution shapes subsequent adjustment. We designed a series of agents with systematically varying risk preferences and paired them in a repeated joint decision-making task previously conducted with humans. Like humans, agents converged toward their partners' choices. However, similar aggregate behavior arose from different dynamics: agents showed little evidence of reciprocal adjustment, tended to weigh rewards more, and their responsibility attribution was decoupled from compromise. Similarly, while they showed bias in their responsibility attribution, the outcome's valence shaped the attribution differently than in humans. Our results reveal important differences between human--human and agent--agent joint decision-making, demonstrating that similar social outcomes can emerge from different social dynamics and reasoning.

Do You Even Know What I Want? Improving LLMs Ability to Detect Hidden User Intent 2026-11-21
Show abstract

As with conventional AI agents, a common source of misalignment between Large Language Models (LLMs) and their human users is misspecified goals. Often, this happens when the human has skewed beliefs about the system's model and provides it with information that causes behavior different from what they expected, with potentially unwanted side effects. To ensure goal alignment, one solution would be for the LLM to ask the human questions about their objectives. However, it has been well-documented that LLMs most often provide default responses to user requests rather than try and ask questions to clarify their true intent. In this paper, we address these challenges by adapting a novel formulation for the objective misspecification problem to work with LLMs. We then test if LLMs that are prompted to ask questions about a user's true underlying objectives are better at identifying them compared to those that do not. We evaluate our method using four state-of-the-art LLMs tested on five standard IPC domains, showing that for some domains, asking questions greatly improved model performance.

Watermark Claims Under Test: Lessons from Auditing a Production LLM 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Can we tell whether an LLM is watermarked without knowing its secret key? Published black-box tests suggest that carefully designed prompts can reveal the statistical footprint of a watermark. This paper examines what happens when those tests move from controlled reference implementations to a deployed model whose watermarking parameters are undisclosed. We present an audit of the Red-Green watermark presence test on Claude. Across ten initial configurations, the published test never rejects on a model the provider states is watermarked, despite detecting a local reference at comparable sample sizes. Controlled experiments expose a failure mode: including the prompt in the watermark's repeated-context history suppresses marking exactly where the test measures it. The watermark remains active, but the probe becomes blind. Attempts to repair the probe reveal further confounds. Constructed contexts can trigger native digit-to-word preferences, while comparisons within a single response can measure how the model plans its answer. Tokenization changes which contexts the probe actually controls. These findings show why validation on a surrogate model does not establish validity on the model under investigation. A redesigned probe finds a substantial context-dependent shift on Claude Fable that is absent on two sibling controls. However, the pre-registered decision remains inconclusive because selective refusals violate the protocol, and the effect's watermark origin is unresolved. We show how to design controls, distinguish statistical rejection from evidence of watermarking and interpret a null without treating it as proof of absence. We provide researchers and practitioners working on LLM evaluation, watermarking, and provenance with an audit checklist grounded in concrete successes and failures. Code, prompts, and parsed responses are available for reproducing the analyses.

Mind the Trap As LLMs Guess the Data Model: Grain-Aware Guards Against Fan-out in Text-to-SQL and Its Benchmarks 2026-11-21
📝 17 pages, 14 figures, 9 tables. Code, data and benchmarks: https://github.com/ahmed-mahran/misqal
Show abstract

Text-to-SQL systems built on large language models (LLMs) often aggregate over one-to-many and many-to-many joins, a classic trap: when the joined rows repeat an entity, a SUM or COUNT counts it more than once, yet the query runs and returns a plausible number. We characterize these fan-out errors by one condition on the relation an aggregate reads, which depends on two facts a schema does not show: each measure's grain, and how many rows a join on each key repeats. An audit that tests the condition on the data finds distorted gold answers for 4.2% of the dev questions of BIRD, a widely used benchmark, and BIRD's later, independent revision changed 44% of the answers the audit flags as definite fan-out, against 16% of unflagged ones. We release BIRD-FanOut, 486 BIRD questions with reviewed gold, and GrainTraps, synthetic questions that set this trap and related ones. Across eight LLM agents, functional dependencies (FDs) computed from the data help no more than per-column statistics on GrainTraps, but grain-aware FDs, which also state each measure's grain and the repetition per key, cut fan-out errors on BIRD from 18.4% of runs to about 13%, also on held-out questions and without LLM judges. Accuracy rises less, because most remaining errors are not fan-out and BIRD's hints often prescribe the over-count. Text-to-SQL models should be given the grain of the data, and benchmark gold should be audited on the data. Code, data and benchmarks are available at https://github.com/ahmed-mahran/misqal

Persuasion from Within: Model-Based Insider Attacks in Multi-Agent LLM Debate and Consensus 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems for collective decision-making, such as multi-agent debate and consensus games, in which agents exchange messages and update their answers over multiple rounds and a resolution mechanism aggregates them into a final outcome. Most multi-agent LLM frameworks assume that every participant shares the system objective. In practice, however, a malicious agent can join as a legitimate member of the group and persuade the other agents toward a hidden adversarial goal. We formalize such attacks as a sequential decision-making problem in which the insider seeks to degrade the utility of the collective outcome. We propose a model-based insider attack that learns to persuade a group of agents: the insider first learns a world model of the benign agents from offline trajectories and then uses it both to train a persuasion policy and to plan its messages. In two case studies involving a numerical consensus game and a multi-agent debate, the learned insider is the most effective and the stealthiest attack we evaluate, outperforming both prompt-based and trained LLM persuaders from prior work. The attack also transfers to other target models, larger groups, and unseen datasets, and remains effective under existing Byzantine-resilience defenses. The learned insider also enables a new defense: adversarially training benign agents on the attack data largely removes both our attack and the strongest prior persuasion attack. These results show that combining world models with reinforcement learning and planning is an effective route to both attacking and hardening language-based multi-agent systems.

PROSPER: LLMs as Sparse Retrievers for First-Stage Product Search 2026-11-21
📝 Accepted at WSDM 2027(oral)
Show abstract

Product search is a crucial component of large-scale e-commerce platforms, where first-stage retrieval should achieve high recall while ensuring efficient online deployment. Sparse retrieval remains attractive for this setting due to its interpretability and deployability, yet it suffers from severe vocabulary mismatch challenges, especially for short queries and product titles. Large language models (LLMs) offer strong semantic capabilities, but directly applying them to sparse retrieval in product search introduces new challenges: (i) semantic expansion may distort critical literal signals such as brand names and model identifiers; and (ii) learning meaningful sparse representations is difficult in the high-dimensional vocabulary space induced by LLMs.To address these challenges, we propose PROSPER, a framework that uses LLMs as sparse retrievers for product search while explicitly preserving lexical fidelity. PROSPER combines (i) a literal residual network that alleviates lexical expansion hallucination by reinforcing underweighted literal terms through a residual compensation mechanism; and (ii) a lexical focusing window that uses top-k selection for rapid early sparsification and effective training initialization. Extensive offline evaluations and large-scale online A/B tests demonstrate that PROSPER consistently improves over existing sparse retrievers, and provides revenue gains for industrial hybrid retrieval systems.

Internalized Experience: How Training Data Shapes Memory Formation in LLMs 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Parametric memory enables large language models (LLMs) to retain information from past experience and reuse it in subsequent tasks. Yet how training data supports the internalization of reusable task rules remains unclear. We introduce FSMem (Finite-State Machine Memory), a state-transition framework for constructing training data and studying how data content and organization shape memory formation. We evaluate rule reuse across synthetic worlds, classical planning, and code execution through valid-action identification and execution-outcome prediction, complemented by next-operation prediction in code. Our experiments reveal several systematic learning patterns. Under matched training conditions, even unchanged task rules become harder to internalize when learned alongside a larger variety of action types; by contrast, scaling the training data to provide broader state-action coverage generally improves rule learning. We further find that training at a single execution depth produces depth-specialized behavior, with models performing best when training and test executions have similar lengths. Motivated by this finding, we construct cumulative corpora that combine short and long trajectories, which can outperform long-trajectory-only training while using fewer training tokens. Together, these results demonstrate that the same task rules can be internalized differently depending on how execution experience is constructed, and that understanding these learning patterns can guide the design of more effective training data for parametric memory.

BitShare: Sensitivity-Aware Binning and Bit Sharing for Compacting LLM Weight Storage 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Decoding with large language models at small batch sizes is limited by memory bandwidth, so the bits stored per weight largely set its cost. Group-wise integer quantization stores every weight of a group at the same precision, although the input channels of a group differ in sensitivity. We present BITSHARE, a codebook-free weight format that keeps the integer grid and the dequantizer of group-wise quantization but varies precision within each group. Sensitivity-aware binning places the channels of each group into three bins at fixed positions, and static bit sharing with optimal bit-value allocation lets the less sensitive weights share their lower bits, each stored once per group or once per pair of weights. A sharing-aware version of GPTQ chooses every stored value, with no fine-tuning and no codebook. BITSHARE has two modes. BITSHARE-U gives every group the same layout, so its decoder routes bits by fixed wiring, while BITSHARE-N chooses a layout per group within the same bit budget. With calibration on the Pile and at the same storage, both modes have lower perplexity than GPTQ at 3 and 2 bits on WikiText-2 and C4. At 2 bits across Llama-2 and Llama-3 models, BITSHARE-N lowers WikiText-2 perplexity by up to 70% against SliM-LLM and up to 35% against GPTAQ. At the 2-bit perplexity of SliM-LLM, BITSHARE-U stores 13.6-19.4% fewer bits per weight, cutting DRAM weight traffic by the same fraction. A verified hardware decoder implementation synthesized in SkyWater 130 nm reconstructs BITSHARE weights at the throughput of INT-n and SliM-LLM decoders.

AutoRSE: Evaluating LLM Agents as Recommendation Engineers for Self-Evolving Recommender Systems 2026-11-21
📝 12 pages, 7 figures. Code: https://github.com/Ray-Tiann/AutoRSE
Show abstract

Recommender Systems Engineer Agents (RSE-Agents), which use LLM-based agents to autonomously improve recommendation algorithms, are emerging as a promising paradigm. However, their capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood due to the lack of a unified, standardized evaluation framework and systematic trajectory-level analysis. To address this gap, we introduce AutoRSE, an evaluation framework spanning six representative industrial-grade recommendation tasks, and conduct a large-scale empirical study of three frontier LLMs across three widely used agent harnesses. Our results show that RSE-Agents consistently enhance the performance of backbones on five of the six tasks, with an average relative improvement of 16.1%, indicating that they can already autonomously improve recommendation performance. However, trajectory-level analysis also reveals that current RSE-Agents function primarily as engineering optimizers rather than algorithmic improvers: their gains are largely driven by hyperparameter tuning, training strategies, and model ensembling, while genuine algorithmic innovations are rare. Since the largest gains in recommender systems often stem from algorithmic advances, this behavior underscores a fundamental limitation of current RSE-Agents. To probe this limitation, we perform idea-injection experiments and show that agents equipped with innovative ideas from frontier research or human experts achieve substantially larger improvements than those relying on self-generated ideas. These results suggest that idea generation is a central bottleneck of current RSE-Agents, highlights a key direction for the future recommendation system research. Our implementation is publicly available on GitHub: https://github.com/Ray-Tiann/AutoRSE.

Active Learning for Communication Structure Optimization in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Optimizing the communication structure of large language model based multi-agent systems (LLM-MAS) has been shown to improve downstream performance and reduce token usage. Existing methods typically train on randomly sampled tasks. We show that this optimization is sensitive to the choice of training tasks: downstream accuracy varies widely across random task sets, and adding more random tasks does not consistently improve it. To actively identify the most valuable tasks from a pool of task-answer pairs, we propose an ensemble-based information-theoretic task selection framework. It estimates task informativeness by how much a candidate task changes the distribution over graph parameters, using ensemble Kalman inversion as an efficient and derivative-free approximation of the Bayesian update. The resulting estimator is especially suitable for black-box and noisy multi-agent systems and remains effective with ensembles much smaller than the parameter dimension. To further reduce the number of tasks whose informativeness must be evaluated, we introduce representative pre-selection and surrogate modeling. We validate the framework in benign and adversarial settings and across multiple task formats. It outperforms random training, demonstrating more effective task selection and greater overall cost efficiency.

Right Verdict, Right Reason? An Execution-Grounded Study of Whether LLM Vulnerability Detectors Understand Vulnerabilities 2026-11-21
📝 25 pages
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to detect vulnerabilities, and their benchmark scores are often read as evidence that they understand the vulnerabilities they report. We test this reading against program execution. For 515 C and C++ functions from BigVul and PrimeVul whose vulnerability we reproduce with a proof of concept, we identify the line at which the fault occurs and the statements on which the faulting run depended. These facts ground a ladder of five tests, from telling a vulnerable function from its fixed version, through locating the fault and reacting to its removal, to judging execution-confirmed edits and predicting the outcome of a concrete run. Each test is paired with a baseline that needs no understanding, such as a rule that picks lines with risky calls, and we repeat the study on 195 vulnerabilities published after the training cutoffs of the models. Four findings emerge. First, telling a vulnerable function from its fixed version is hard. The open-weight models separate no more pairs than chance, although five of them show a weak sensitivity to the fix, and the commercial models still label many screened fixed versions vulnerable. Second, the commercial models locate the fault beyond a keyword rule, while removing that evidence changes the verdicts of the strongest model only on the newer vulnerabilities. Third, given the program input, the commercial models predict whether a run faults better than a rule that looks for added checks, and the prediction follows the input. Fourth, on the newer vulnerabilities this advantage disappears and the lead of the strongest model narrows, while localization persists. We release the framework, the per-function evidence, and an execution-confirmed counterfactual benchmark.

Surprising Success, Repeated Failure: Entropy-Guided Credit Assignment for Exploration in LLM Reasoning 2026-11-21
📝 Project page : https://eapo-explore.github.io
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) enhances reasoning in large language models (LLMs) through outcome-level feedback, yet recent approaches to finer-grained credit assignment often require auxiliary models, additional sampling, or privileged information. Although policy entropy provides a readily available signal, prioritizing uncertain positions under both reinforcement and penalization concentrates penalties where failed responses still retain alternatives for recovery, which can suppress opportunities for exploration. To address this, we introduce Entropic Advantage Policy Optimization (EAPO), an entropy-guided credit assignment method that treats success and failure asymmetrically. Specifically, motivated by the observation that success under uncertainty is less repeatable while confident failures tend to recur, EAPO couples normalized policy entropy with the sign of the response advantage to reinforce surprising success and correct repeated failure. It assigns stronger reinforcement to high-entropy decisions in successful responses and stronger penalties to low-entropy decisions in failed responses, while attenuating penalties at uncertain positions to preserve opportunities for recovery. By redistributing the response advantage across tokens, EAPO derives token-level credit directly from existing rollout signals without additional supervision. We validate EAPO on a range of reasoning tasks across both base and reasoning backbones, demonstrating that it achieves the best overall performance. We further show that EAPO promotes more effective exploration, broadening problem coverage and diversifying successful reasoning strategies.

Beyond Unit Tests: Path-Aware Rubric-Guided LLM Autograding of Notebooks 2026-11-21
📝 Accepted at IEEE TALE 2026
Show abstract

Jupyter notebooks support open-ended, multi-step assessments in which students build one logical flow across interdependent steps, combining code, written analysis, and generated outputs such as plots. These reflect authentic scientific practice but are hard to grade consistently, since students may make different yet equally valid choices at each step, so a later answer can only be judged in light of the choices a student made earlier. We present an LLM-based autograder for such flow-structured notebook questions. It generates a rubric from a reference solution, then applies a softening pass that relaxes criteria tied to the reference so legitimate alternative paths are not penalized. Each question group is graded in a single model call, so later answers are judged against the student's own earlier choices, using question-type tags and schema-constrained, low-temperature outputs. Evidence is scored multimodally: code, text, numerical outputs, and figures from code cells. We deployed the tool in a machine-learning course, integrated with Gradescope, with a pre-submission format check and prompt-injection defenses, and evaluate it both in that live course and against archived submissions graded by staff in earlier terms. We report agreement, cross-model variance, cost, student perceptions, and injection resilience. With low-temperature models, agreement with human scores is often strong, including on the open-ended items the design specifically targets, while run-to-run stability depends sharply on the model's temperature support.

Selective ROM Weight Storage for Responsive LLM Control on Edge Robots 2026-11-21
Show abstract

Edge robots increasingly use large language models (LLMs) for complex tasks, demanding efficient inference alongside timely control. Limited memory capacity constrains model deployment, while repeated weight transfers during decoding compete with activations and KV cache for external bandwidth. Sequential generation of robot actions further delays decisions and periodic control on shared hardware. We present a hybrid memory architecture with heterogeneous scheduling to address these bottlenecks jointly. Banked ROM stores selected fixed weights and supplies a standard compute array through a dedicated datapath. LPDDR retains the remaining weights and mutable state, preserving model flexibility. Coordinated operand delivery and preemption at output slice boundaries accelerate inference while limiting interference with periodic control. A structured decision interface scores candidate phases in a single forward pass, avoiding sequential token generation for routine choices. Together, these mechanisms improve memory service, compute access, and decision execution for edge robotic workloads. DisSparse: Pipelined Top-$p$ Sparse Attention for Long-Context LLM Serving 2026-11-21
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. Code: https://github.com/nnurlan008/DisSparse
Show abstract

Long-context LLM inference is bottlenecked by the memory demands of the key-value (KV) cache, whose size scales linearly with context length. Dynamic sparse attention reduces this bottleneck by fetching and attending only to a critical subset of tokens. The dominant primitive in dynamic sparse attention research and deployment is fixed-budget top-$k$ selection, which is favored because its deterministic block count aligns with the static memory allocation that systems like vLLM rely on. Top-$k$ uses a fixed budget regardless of how attention mass is distributed across heads, layers, and decode steps, leading to either wasted bandwidth on heads with peaky attention or dropped context on heads with diffuse attention. Top-$p$ (nucleus) selection adapts naturally to this variation but has seen limited adoption in production serving systems due to two system-level challenges: variable block counts that break deterministic block allocation and computationally expensive selection kernels that exceed the cost of attention itself. We present DisSparse, a serving system that integrates top-$p$ sparse attention into vLLM end-to-end. DisSparse decouples block selection from the scheduler's critical path, hides selection latency behind layer computation via a two-stream pipeline, and introduces a single-pass histogram-based top-$p$ kernel. We further observe intra-block sparsity, the inflation of selection budgets caused by mixing high- and low-importance tokens within fixed-size KV cache blocks, and address it with a token relocation kernel guided by accumulated prefill attention scores. Across long- and medium-context benchmarks on multiple models, DisSparse achieves 1.17x to 1.80x speedup in sparsity analysis (block selection) over state-of-the-art top-$p$ methods with negligible accuracy loss and supports up to 8x larger maximum batch sizes than dense vLLM under the same memory budget.
