Decision Models for Multilingual Emotion Classification: A Comparative Study Against LLMs and Encoders 2026-11-20
📝 36 pages, 5 figures
Show abstract

Decision models receive a question with a closed list of answers and return a probability for each answer instead of free text. They promise low latency and robustness to prompt wording, but no study has compared several of them with fine-tuned encoders across languages. We ask whether systems used without training on labelled data (zero-shot) can replace fine-tuning, and what the decision format adds over a general-purpose large language model (LLM). We compare three decision models (Jev, OpenAI Decisions, Strands Decider 2B) and two LLMs (GPT-6 Luna, Qwen3.5-2B), given Italian instructions, with two fine-tuned multilingual encoders (tabularisai, XLM-EMO) on four emotions (joy, sadness, anger, fear) in 10 social-media datasets in five languages, with paired difference and equivalence tests. The three best zero-shot systems (two decision models and one LLM) score on average about one macro-F1 point below an encoder fine-tuned on the target dataset; our test sets cannot exclude a gap of 4-5 points, and on single datasets either approach wins by up to 22 points. They beat encoders fine-tuned on another dataset by 8-15 points, though against XLM-EMO this may not extend to new datasets, and instructions in each text's language change their results by under 1 point. Exploratorily, they beat the strongest public emotion model used as is by about 6 points. OpenAI's decision endpoint matches GPT-6 Luna, the LLM it runs on, at a fifth of its median latency, and a 2-billion-parameter decision model beats a same-size general LLM by 10 points, owing to its training, not the decision format. The best zero-shot systems, the LLM included, have an SD of about 1 point across prompts. Without labelled data they are a strong default, and decision models add lower latency and, unlike generative LLM use, per-option probabilities. Fine-tuning won by a wide margin only on the largest dataset, GoEmotions.

FOREST: Coordinating Tree-based HBF, RRAM, and HBM for Efficient Speculative LLM Inference 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Speculative decoding (SD) accelerates large language model (LLM) inference by combining lightweight drafting with parallel target-model verification. However, its stages impose distinct resource demands: target verification requires high-throughput access to large weight sets, sequential drafting executes a compact model under tight latency constraints, and long-context attention processes growing key-value (KV) caches. Conventional GPU-centric SD systems serve these workloads through shared HBM-centric memory and general-purpose compute, creating mismatches in capacity, bandwidth, and computation. These mismatches constrain deployable model-context configurations and limit the efficiency of bandwidth-bound and latency-sensitive operations. We propose FOREST, a heterogeneous memory-centric architecture coordinating HBF, 3D-stacked RRAM, and HBM memory-compute trees for efficient SD. It features: (1) compute-aligned 3D RRAM, combining ultra-wide, low-frequency reads with clock-decoupled interfaces and overlapped SRAM-DCIM weight refills for latency-sensitive drafting; (2) cross-tree numerical co-design, moving block-scaled Q/K/V preprocessing to HBF and RRAM root plates and enabling block-floating-point (BFP) attention with pipelined softmax reduction in the HBM tree; and (3) HBF-aware SD design-space exploration, revealing how bandwidth-compute provisioning and model scale determine performance, energy, and cost trade-offs. Evaluated across six SD configurations spanning 32B-405B target models, FOREST achieves up to 5.5x end-to-end speedup, 11.46x higher decode throughput, and 16.5x higher decode energy efficiency over NVIDIA A800 GPU-based SD baselines. Under a modeled total cost of ownership, FOREST delivers up to 6.73x higher token-generation cost efficiency.

Report2Alpha-Bench: Evaluating LLM Agents on End-to-End Quantitative Research 2026-11-20
Show abstract

We evaluate whether LLM agents can independently carry out a several-hour quantitative research project: read a brokerage research report, make a plan, and over 7 rounds of iteration propose hypotheses, write factor code and improve it from backtest feedback, seeing only 2022-2023 market data. Like Terminal-Bench-Science, the task evaluates overall research ability; unlike it, there is no directly checkable unique answer: the model must repeatedly propose hypotheses on very noisy data, revise its understanding from experiments, disentangle multiple factors and design ablations without overfitting in-sample, much like complex real-world decisions with many interacting factors. After the research, a fixed rule using only 2022-2023 results selects a low-correlated factor pool from all candidates the model produced, and scores it on unseen 2024-2025 data after removing common style returns such as size and momentum; the same rule on round-0 candidates alone gives a report baseline that "implements the report without iterating". We run gpt-6.1-sol (reasoning effort xhigh), gpt-6.1-terra (xhigh), muse-spark-1.3 (xhigh) and deepseek-v4.1-flash (max) once on each of 7 reports. Of the 28 runs, 19 factor pools pass the usability threshold; the average score is 48.4 (0-100 scale), against 25.4 for the report baseline and 35.9 for the models' own picks. From similar report baselines (17.0-31.0), the models separate after iteration to 74.7 (gpt-6.1-sol), 56.6 (gpt-6.1-terra), 35.8 (deepseek-v4.1-flash) and 26.2 (muse-spark-1.3); their candidates look equally good in training and diverge only in testing, with the leaders proposing more diverse ideas per round and ending with more effective factors in the pool. Evaluating research agents thus requires scoring on frozen future data after removing styles, and measuring "what the research produced" separately from "what was finally picked".

Potential emergence in LLM agents: a statistical physics perspective 2026-11-20
📝 21 pages, 5 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

There has been significant recent interest in understanding the behaviour of LLM agents from the perspective of human preferences or corpus statistics. In this paper we propose agent states as intermediate variables through which post-training can be measured and predicted. From a statistical physics perspective, we study the emergence of a global potential which governs the transition of state in LLM agent reasoning. We introduce a partition function to model the ideal KL-regularised transition distribution and track potential structure, mean reward and state entropy. We demonstrate our approach in two Qwen3.5-9B tasks: safety-test selection and physics-reasoning auditing. We observe that the potential structure strengthens during post-training as predicted from our framework, while joint changes in reward and state entropy provide consistency evidence. States thus provide a quantitative description of how post-training organises agent behaviour.

PRISM: Validation-Free Online Batch Selection for LLM Fine-Tuning via Preconditioned Conic Ghost Matching 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Online batch selection lowers the cost of fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) by replacing each candidate batch with a smaller informative subset. Existing selectors typically depend on a held-out validation or proxy set, or else rely on Euclidean facility-location objectives over unpreconditioned gradients whose diameter-shifted similarities are distorted by heavy-tailed gradient norms and cross-layer anisotropy. We introduce PRISM (Preconditioned Ray-Isotropic Submodular Matching), a validation-free online batch selector that aligns candidate gradients in a factored second-moment metric and casts subset construction as conic facility location over positive ray projections. Computing the required pairwise geometry through factored preconditioned ghost inner products avoids materializing per-example parameter gradients, while the conic objective remains normalized, nonnegative, and monotone submodular---guaranteeing a $(1-1/e)$ greedy approximation whose additive error scales with total batch gradient energy rather than the worst-case outlier diameter. Assigning each candidate to its best-aligned selected ray induces closed-form radial projection weights that automatically shrink orthogonal or noisy examples toward zero. We derive a nonconvex convergence bound showing that optimization error is governed directly by the conic representation cost. Across four LLM fine-tuning configurations spanning 1.5B to 8B parameters, PRISM outperforms full-batch training, Euclidean facility location, and zeroth-order selectors, and rivals or surpasses validation-guided baselines without held-out data.

Exploring the Potential of Ultra-Low-Bit Inference on LLM Accelerators 2026-11-20
📝 8 pages, 13 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

Vector quantization (VQ) enables ultra-low-bit compression of large language models (LLMs) by encoding groups of values as compact codebook indexes. However, reconstructing these values for computation requires frequent codebook lookups, which conflict with the dataflows of conventional LLM accelerators and limit the performance gains from VQ compression. Consequently, prior work has resorted to VQ-specific accelerator designs, which either incur substantial additional hardware overhead or support only a limited range of inference scenarios. In this work, we argue that existing systolic-array-based LLM accelerators already possess the essential hardware resources for efficient VQ inference, but lack the mechanisms to effectively exploit them. To bridge this gap, we present VQACC, an architectural enhancement that jointly redesigns the existing operand buffer's access logic and data mapping to reuse its storage and port resources for codebook lookups, seamlessly integrating dequantization into the normal operand-addressing dataflow. Evaluation shows that VQACC achieves a 2.63$\times$ speedup over the EVA baseline, with additional hardware occupying only 0.17\% of the compute array's area.

Bringing AI Assistance to the Beznau Nuclear Power Plant: A Hierarchical System of LLM Agents for Reactor Monitoring 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Beznau Unit 1 in Switzerland, in operation since 1969, is the most experienced nuclear reactor still running worldwide. Five decades of incremental modernization have left its monitoring systems, alarm logs, variable catalogs, technical specifications and investigation reports in separate silos that were never designed to be queried together. As a result, investigating a core instrumentation anomaly means slow, manual gathering of knowledge scattered across databases, documents, and experienced colleagues. We present a hierarchical multi-agent assistant built on large language models (LLMs) to support these investigations and reduce the need for tedious manual searches in SAP systems. An orchestrating agent breaks down an engineer's question and hands the parts to specialized sub-agents, each with read-only access to one source. Domain expertise is written as natural-language procedures that plant engineers can read, review and revise. The system is strictly advisory, with every answer traced back to its sources. We also report organizational lessons from introducing agentic AI into a culture built on safety, traceability and conservative change. Beyond the technical architecture, the Beznau case study examines how an assistant can be introduced into a setting where evidence must be traceable, changes carefully reviewed, and operational decisions retained by plant personnel, paving the way for the responsible use of agentic AI in industrial plant monitoring.

No Audio, No Transcripts, No Problem: LLM-based ASR Adaptation Using Only Domain Documents 2026-11-20
📝 Accepted at IEEE SLT 2026
Show abstract

Can a speech recognizer be adapted to a new domain with no target audio and no transcripts, using only written documentation? This is the realistic onboarding setting: a new customer hands over raw documentation, not the transcripts that text-only adaptation normally assumes. We turn a domain's documents into adaptation text in two ways, directly as written sentences or rendered by a large language model (LLM) into synthetic spoken dialogs, and feed them to a recently proposed denoising-based text-only adaptation method. We show that, across in-domain, out-of-domain, and cross-domain settings on two corpora, document-only adaptation can match or closely approach real transcriptions; whether written or conversational rendering is best depends on speaking style and the amount of text generated. To our knowledge, this is the first study of document-only adaptation of LLM-based ASR. As no existing corpus pairs audio, transcripts, and documents, we also built and released a document-grounded benchmark.

Cleave: Severing Guardrails and Auditing Spectral Coupling in Open-Weight LLMs 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Open-weight Large Language Models (LLMs) are widely downloaded for their strong coding and reasoning capabilities and deployed locally beyond the provider's control. To prevent misuse, developers embed safety guardrails into these models before release. However, we find that these guardrails rely on the residual writing weights to trigger refusals. Based on this insight, we propose Cleave, a training-free attack that directly edits these weights to remove safety restrictions while preserving general capabilities. With Cleave, malicious users can either remove all guardrails or selectively lift only cybersecurity restrictions. Because this selective removal retains up to a 99% refusal rate on other harmful requests and avoids weight rank collapse, it evades both behavioral spot-checks and static weight audits. Across five open-weight LLMs from 7B to 320B parameters, Cleave achieves up to a 67.97% attack success rate on seven safety benchmarks, substantially outperforming existing white-box baselines. On 356 cybersecurity tasks and 15 live vulnerable services, it further boosts executable exploit generation by up to 24.0 times and compromises up to 66.7% of the targets. To counter this threat, we propose a dual-stage defense that combines pre-release subspace diagnostics with post-release weight-coupling audits, achieving 100% detection recall at a 1% false-positive rate.

Jagged Robustness: Uneven Safety Transfer and Emerging Vulnerabilities under LLM Quantization 2026-11-20
📝 Code Repository: https://github.com/LucianoXu/QuantBeyoundEfficiency
Show abstract

Quantization enables efficient inference in language models through model parameter compression. This work audits the impact of quantization on capability and safety domains including model safeguards, instruction-following, ethics and bias. Through benchmarks, we find that model safeguards and bias can degrade under quantization, while other capabilities such as truthfulness remain more stable in Qwen3-4B. We define this uneven transfer of LLM capabilities under quantization as Jagged Robustness. We find multiple factors driving Jagged Robustness: task complexity, evaluation method and generation coherence. Moreover, we verify the degradation of model safeguards by observing increased compliance with unsafe requests under quantization, and demonstrate universal adversarial suffix attacks that are benign for the full model but jailbreak quantized models. The findings indicate that it is risky to assume that the performance and robustness can transfer evenly across different capability domains from full models to quantized models.

AMARIS: Merging Generalist and Specialist LLMs via Adaptive Subspace Inheritance 2026-11-20
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Domain specialization of large language models (LLMs) often improves target-domain performance but degrades broad capabilities. Model merging is an attractive training-free alternative, yet common parameter-space strategies (for example, interpolation and sparsified averaging) provide limited control over task-specific interference. We introduce Adaptive Model Alignment via Ranked Inherited Subspaces (AMARIS), a covariance-driven framework that transfers specialist updates only along selected activation directions. AMARIS formulates merging as budgeted subspace inheritance: it seeks high specialist activation capture under a regularized base-output perturbation budget, then builds a low-rank projection gate from ranked generalized eigendirections. This yields a single static merged checkpoint that concentrates transfer in high-utility, low-interference directions. We evaluate AMARIS on Qwen3-based merges (8B and 32B) across finance, instruction-following, and multilingual SEA specialization. In our evaluated settings, AMARIS reaches Pareto operating points that are competitive with strong training-free baselines, including 98.37% specialist retention with 95.16% core retention for the 8B finance setting, 103.55% core retention with 90.28% specialist retention for the 8B instruction-following setting, and 99.20% core retention with 94.80% specialist retention for the 32B SEA setting. These results support subspace-constrained merging as a practical route to compositional capability integration without additional training.

Breaking the Long Horizon: Short-Horizon Process Supervision for Long-Form Generation in LLMs 2026-11-20
📝 Submitted to AAAI 2027. 17 pages, 8 figures
Show abstract

Long-form generation is increasingly important for agentic LLM systems, yet current reinforcement learning methods typically optimize an entire document with a single outcome-level reward. This delayed and coarse-grained supervision makes it difficult to localize errors and assign credit across semantically distinct parts of the output. To address this, we propose a short-horizon framework that reformulates long-form generation through semantic decomposition into segment-level decisions. Each segment is generated with prefix-conditioned context, allowing cross-segment coherence to be preserved while keeping supervision local. The generated segments are then scored with rubric-based rewards for adherence, coherence, and relevance, enabling explicit local supervision at the segment level. These local rewards are used to compute step-wise advantages, yielding localized policy updates. To isolate the effect of supervision granularity, we instantiate both outcome-level supervision and our short-horizon process reward method (SH-PRM) under the same GRPO framework. Across multiple LLMs and two long-form generation benchmarks, SH-PRM consistently outperforms outcome-level supervision and strong inference-time baselines under the same optimizer and comparable budgets. Further analyses suggest that these gains primarily arise from improved credit assignment. Overall, our results suggest that aligning supervision granularity with the semantic structure of long-form generation is an effective design principle for long-form LLM training.

Cruella: A Unified Microscaling and Floating-Point Dot-Product Unit for LLM Inference on FPGAs 2026-11-20
📝 32 pages, 13 figures, 7 tables
Show abstract

The number formats that accelerators execute natively are fixed at fabrication, yet large language model (LLM) checkpoints are released with MXFP4 weights in the Microscaling (MX) formats standardized in 2023. FPGAs adopt new formats through bitstreams. However, to the best of our knowledge, no deployed FPGA LLM accelerator executes MXFP4 weights alongside BF16 products. We characterize the decoding of MXFP4 checkpoints, whose layers alternate between MXFP4$\times$BF16 products and BF16$\times$BF16 products of attention and BF16 layers. With a BF16 KV cache, BF16$\times$BF16 products take 56%-77% of the datapath time, during which a separate MXFP4 unit idles. We present Cruella, whose unified dot-product unit (UDPU) executes both product types on one DSP array and reduction pipeline. First, per-group pre-alignment keeps this pipeline at the BF16 width and changes perplexity by at most 0.7%. Second, pre-adder packing places two signed MXFP4$\times$BF16 products in one DSP48E2 multiplier with an exact correction. On an Alveo U55C, the UDPU needs 23% fewer DSP slices and 36% fewer LUTs than separate MXFP4 and BF16 units of equal throughput. On this board, MXFP4 weights speed up decoding by 1.15$\times$-1.87$\times$ over BF16 weights, and an NVIDIA A40 GPU decodes faster but spends 0.92$\times$-2.01$\times$ the board's energy per token.

RegimeCouncil: Paired-Evidence Routing For Memory Conflict Governance In LLM Agents 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Alongside memory storage and retrieval, a fundamental challenge in long-term LLM agent systems is how the agent should act when stored memories conflict with an evolving world. Facts often go stale without explicit deletion, so the conflict surfaces before the store is refreshed: delayed feedback shows that acting on one outdated record hurt, but not whether suppression should stay on that fact (a local policy) or extend to other stored facts (a global policy). Existing systems hard-code one of these scopes at design time. We formulate this online choice, under hidden validity and delayed feedback, as memory conflict governance, and propose RegimeCouncil, a lightweight router that keeps both scopes as advisors and arbitrates between them without retraining the LLM. Each memory-assisted response is scored against a no-memory anchor, by off-path paired comparison or by inverse-propensity estimation from the executed action alone. A Local Advisor tracks only the queried fact, a Global Advisor pools recent outcomes across the store, and an asymmetric Meta Governor follows Local by default, switching to Global only when pooling yields a strictly positive recent utility margin. We prove that using delayed outcomes escapes the prompt-only regret floor, that neither fixed scope suffices alone, and that this margin gate switches within bounded time. On LongMemEval streams, historical drift in TempLAMA and TAQA, delays $τ\in [1, 96]$, and sparse or bandit feedback, with Qwen3-30B-A3B and GPT-4o, RegimeCouncil outperforms the better fixed scope in all 30 held-out settings, by 5.2-7.3 points on disagreement episodes and +27.9 points over standalone cold-start fallback under clustered shifts, while cutting harmful stale-memory use at microsecond overhead.

Learning Adaptive Defense Strategies: Fine-Tuned LLMs for Network Security Games under Network Mobility 2026-11-20
📝 Submitted in ICNC
Show abstract

Large language models(LLM) show promise for strategic reasoning, yet their ability to compute game-theoretic equilibria under dynamic conditions remains largely unexplored. This work investigates whether fine-tuned LLMs can serve as solver-free surrogates for online equilibrium computation in honeypot allocation, a setting in which network mobility continuously reshapes the underlying attack graph and forces repeated re-solving of a two-player zero-sum game. We construct a dataset of 270 security games on networks of 8--25 nodes with solver-grounded equilibrium labels, and evaluate four classes of predictor over 1{,}524 held-out mobility events: non-learned baselines, six open-weight architectures evaluated zero-shot, the same six after LoRA fine-tuning, and four proprietary models as an external reference point. Predictions are scored without post-hoc repair against exact game-theoretic criteria: exploitability against an optimal attacker best-response, equilibrium support accuracy, and distributional distance. Fine-tuning reduces exploitability across every architecture, and fine-tuned open-weight models of modest size outperform every zero-shot predictor, including proprietary ones; no zero-shot model at any tier reaches the floor set by uniform allocation. Stratifying by mobility type exposes a qualitative shift that aggregate metrics conceal: zero-shot models do not condition on whether a departing node lies on an attack path, while fine-tuned models recover this distinction. We further find that validation loss is negatively rank-correlated with task performance, and that output validity, rather than predictive accuracy, is the binding constraint on further progress.

Tokens are All You Need: Dual-purpose Semantic IDs for Achieving LLM-Level I/O Efficiency in recommendation systems 2026-11-20
📝 RecSys 2026
Show abstract

Large-scale recommendation systems face "Memory Wall" bottlenecks due to massive, dense embedding tables. While generative retrieval uses discrete tokens for IDs, high-dimensional context still relies on inefficient dense formats. Inspired by computer vision data compression, we propose Dual-purpose Semantic IDs to achieve LLM-level I/O efficiency. Our methodology uses hierarchical quantization to condense continuous embeddings into discrete Semantic IDs performing two concurrent roles: (1) Collaborative Identity: modeling user-item interactions via learnable embedding table; and (2) Content Reconstruction: using a lightweight Semantic Decoder for on-the-fly embedding approximation. This approach replaces massive vector storage with on-demand reconstruction, reducing system overhead and data footprints. We demonstrate the efficacy of our framework through offline evaluations and successful online deployment in production-scale ranking and retrieval systems at a major video sharing platform, showing that discrete tokens are indeed all you need for highly efficient, content-rich recommendation.

Do Senior Agents Do Their Job? Measuring Role Integrity in How LLM Agents Use Subordinate Material 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Multi-agent LLM systems increasingly divide long tasks among subagents whose reports a senior agent turns into the final answer. Such a division of labor pays off only if each agent, and above all the senior agent, carries out the duties of its role. Current evaluations, however, mostly score the final answer, and a fluent, well-cited answer cannot show whether the senior agent weighed its subagents' reports or merely aggregated them. To assess whether a senior agent meets its assigned responsibilities, we define role integrity as the preservation of an agent's functional responsibilities when it acts on its subordinates' material. To measure it, we introduce RI-Bench, a behavioral benchmark grounded in the structures and public records of three institutional hierarchies: peer review, appellate adjudication, and parliamentary inquiry. We choose these hierarchies because their rules explicitly assign the senior role a duty toward subordinate material, and together they cover three basic duties: to weigh, to verify, and to relay. Rather than scoring output correctness, we measure how senior agents' outputs respond to controlled changes in subordinate material, using rule-based checks and a rubric-guided LLM judge calibrated against human annotation. Across eight LLMs and over 12,000 agentic episodes, senior agents rarely adopt a planted procedural error (2.9% of rulings) but often follow a majority of generic reviews (up to 71% of decisions) or drop witnesses' stated limitations (up to 65% of cited claims). Obligation prompts reduce these failures without removing them, and they also shift behavior in the control conditions. These findings motivate direct, obligation-specific evaluation of role integrity rather than inferring it from general capability or explicit role instructions. Code and data are available at https://github.com/jxtse/ri-bench.

CoEvoBench: Can LLMs Drive Recursive Co-Evolution of Agentic Systems? 2026-11-20
📝 28 pages, 6 figures. Code: https://github.com/AlibabaResearch/CoEvoBench
Show abstract

The performance of an LLM agent depends on both its model and its harness, the code that mediates its interaction with tools and environments. Can LLM researchers drive this co-evolution as a recursive improvement process, deciding round after round which component to change when each change alters the value of the other? Existing benchmarks evaluate post-training or data research with the harness out of scope, or harness optimization with the model fixed, leaving this joint decision unmeasured. We introduce CoEvoBench, a benchmark that evaluates LLMs as researchers co-evolving the model and harness of a target agent across five agentic domains. Researchers start from a common base model and initial harness, train and revise within a 24-hour budget, and submit a model-harness pair that is scored on separate test tasks. Across five frontier researchers, the strongest closes more than four fifths of the gap to an instruction-tuned reference system, and co-evolution succeeds on interaction tasks but rarely on long-horizon coding. These results suggest that current LLMs can make a strong first improvement, but cannot yet turn feedback from an improved system into the next one. CoEvoBench provides a controlled testbed for measuring the research capabilities that recursive co-evolution requires.

When Do LLM-Elicited Priors Help? A Controlled Audit of Language Models as Priors for Small-Sample Bayesian Regression 2026-11-20
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly asked where to center the coefficients of a small-sample Bayesian regression, but the evidence is unclear: benchmarks are often built to match the prior and models may remember them. We audit this practice with one frontier and one flash-tier model, using hidden feature names, post-cutoff housing data and a pre-specified test that gives features the wrong descriptions. First, the models share their errors (correlation $0.92$--$0.99$ across draws, vendors and paraphrases): averaging $20$ draws is no more accurate than one, and treating draws as independent creates overconfidence. Much of this error comes from reporting each feature's effect alone, not with the others held fixed. Second, against the best classical prior, the LLM center helps clearly on one of four benchmarks (RMSE $-5.8\%$), marginally on two, and hurts mean RMSE on the fourth. Third, the LLM's stated uncertainty is unreliable; letting the data set the prior scale cuts the harm of a wrong center four-fold against a unit-variance prior. A pre-specified external test returned the verdict \textsc{partial}: on $18$ rule-selected problems the center helps on six and hurts on four, and a hard trust gate trusts a harmful prior while an evidence-weighted mixture is safer.

Protecting Private Values in Tool-Using LLM Agents via Recoverable Context Projection 2026-11-20
📝 5 pages, 2 figures
Show abstract

Memory-augmented and tool-using agents may expose exact private values when a remote LLM processes retrieved memory, tool actions, and observations. One-way masking limits initial exposure but removes values needed for trusted execution and may allow them to reappear in later observations. We propose Trustworthy Virtual Memory (TVM), a closed-loop runtime that exposes only projected context to the remote model, resolves exact values locally for trusted execution, and reprojects subsequent observations. A Survey on Long-Term Memory Security in LLM Agents: Attacks, Defenses, and Governance Across the Memory Lifecycle 2026-11-20
📝 22 pages, 3 figures, 4 tables. Expanded version of the EMNLP 2026 paper. Adds evidence on persistent representations, skill memory, authorization changes, and recovery. Table 4 compares lifecycle security claims and their evaluation conditions
Show abstract

The emergence of writable, cross-session persistent memory in LLM agents introduces a qualitatively different threat landscape from conventional input-centric security concerns, characterized by three properties: persistence, statefulness, and propagation. To systematically characterize this landscape, we propose a Memory Lifecycle Framework that organizes attacks, defenses, and their cross-phase dependencies along two axes: six lifecycle phases (Write, Store, Retrieve, Execute, Share & Propagate, Forget & Rollback) and four security objectives (Integrity, Confidentiality, Availability, Governance). Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved widespread adoption because of their strong reasoning and query-response capabilities. However, deploying them in embedded and edge computing environments remains challenging because of strict latency, memory, and energy constraints. Their large parameter counts and computational demands hinder efficient execution on resource-constrained platforms. Although model pruning has emerged as a viable solution for reducing scale while preserving performance, jointly optimizing layers, attention heads, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) dimensions remains highly complex. Exhaustively exploring this combined design space is computationally expensive and often leads to local optima or unstable configurations. To address these limitations, we propose a hardware-aware, multi-objective structured pruning framework. The proposed two-stage method explicitly targets latency and model size for efficient deployment on edge devices. In the coarse-grained stage, multi-objective depth pruning removes entire attention and MLP blocks to reduce computational load and memory usage. In the subsequent fine-grained stage, Parallel Bayesian Optimization (PBO) searches for the optimal layer-wise pruning ratios for pruning under latency constraints, while importance-based strategies rank the specific components to be pruned within each layer's allocated budget. Experimental results show that our approach reduces model complexity with minimal impact on commonsense reasoning tasks and zero-shot performance. Our method achieves a favorable trade-off among accuracy, latency, and model size, making it suitable for edge deployment. Across multiple LLMs at 37.5% and 50% pruning ratios, the proposed approach achieves better performance on commonsense reasoning tasks than existing methods while significantly reducing inference cost.

BlockJack: Cross-Tenant Redirection Fault Attacks on Paged KV Cache Address Translation in LLM Serving 2026-11-19
📝 6 figures, 5 tables

Large language model (LLM) serving frameworks accelerate inference by retaining previously computed states in a key-value (KV) cache. Paged serving frameworks such as vLLM organize this cache through block tables analogous to OS page tables, which map each request's logical KV blocks to physical KV blocks, forming a software-managed address translation layer. Although fault attacks such as Rowhammer have exploited OS page tables to redirect memory accesses, prior fault attacks on LLMs have largely targeted the data plane, i.e., model weights or KV-cache contents, leaving KV-cache mapping unexplored. We present BlockJack (i.e., Block Hijack), the first KV cache address-translation fault attack in LLM inference. By corrupting block-table entries, BlockJack redirects a request's KV cache reads to blocks assigned to another tenant's request without modifying model parameters, prompts, or cached KV states. It achieves both confidentiality violation (victim information appears in attacker responses) and integrity violation (attacker-supplied context steers victim answers). We study paged KV cache mappings in vLLM and formally characterize when redirect targets are predictable. Across seven models spanning the Qwen, Llama, Gemma, and Phi families, a faulted mapping exposes victim context. For the integrity attack, we examine how topic proximity, intra-topic divergence, and model behavior shape redirected responses. On a 248-scene medical benchmark, the integrity attack causes up to 41.1% of responses to recommend less urgent care, while still appearing consistent with the victim's question. We demonstrate physical feasibility of BlockJack with Rowhammer and subsequently evaluate assignment validation as a defense. Following responsible disclosure, vLLM and Sonar confirmed the vulnerability and proposed patches that we reviewed and validated.

Automated analog circuit sizing methods, from black-box optimizers to recent large language model (LLM) agents, typically search the design space with a SPICE simulator in the loop, whereas an experienced designer works out a design by hand analysis. This paper presents CRAFT, a flow in which an LLM agent designs a given topology by calculation, as a designer does, and without an initial sizing. The agent works only through DeX, a service that uses pre-computed lookup tables to provide device characteristics and to solve the whole netlist for its operating point and small-signal response. It thus designs without SPICE, and every final design is verified independently in HSPICE. We test CRAFT on four circuits in a commercial 28 nm process; the largest is a 37-transistor three-stage amplifier with 53 design variables, 20 specifications and 74 saturation constraints. With Claude Opus 5.5, CRAFT meets every specification and constraint in all 20 runs, five per circuit. On that amplifier, with DeX in place of HSPICE and an identical request for both, the same agent passes in 10 of 10 runs instead of 6 of 10 within an hour and is 2.0x faster at the median.

Enterprises are moving agentic workloads from closed APIs to their own open-weight model deployments. Large language model (LLM) serving requires substantial memory to store model weights and KV caches. Agentic workloads compound this pressure through repeated interactions over growing contexts, making it important to retain KV cache for reuse. High-bandwidth flash (HBF) offers a way to expand accelerator memory capacity for LLM serving, but its access costs and limited write endurance complicate its use. We evaluate HBF for high-throughput agentic serving across system design and scheduling choices to understand when additional capacity improves serving performance and energy efficiency. We introduce an HBM-HBF-host hierarchical storage system and buffered cache-aware scheduling, and use trace-driven simulations to analyze their effects on performance, energy consumption, and HBF write lifetime. Across the evaluated workloads, the fastest HBF-augmented systems reduce completion time by 21.1-86.9% relative to HBM-only systems. Modeled energy savings reach 62.4%, with benefits depending on the workload and weight placement. Buffered cache-aware scheduling extends estimated HBF write lifetime from 0.99 to 13.63 years in the evaluated configuration. These results demonstrate the importance of coordinating data placement and scheduling in serving LLM with HBF, positioning HBF as an actively utilized lower-level memory tier rather than passive storage.

HabitusMem: Belief Memory over Evolving User Preferences for LLM Agents 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Personalized LLM agents must act on a user's current preferences, which change across interactions and are rarely stated in full. Existing memory systems store and retrieve content from the interaction history and leave the agent to work out which preferences still hold and where they apply. Such content does not indicate whether a preference is still current or where it applies, so agents often act on outdated or misapplied preferences. We instead treat memory as the assistant's belief about the user. Following Bourdieu's account of habitus as durable but revisable dispositions, we model the user as a latent preference state that every interaction, including the current request, both reveals and updates. The belief summarizes what the history supports about this state, and memory consists of the individual beliefs that bear on the agent's current decision, each with its evidence, scope, and status. We implement this view in HabitusMem, a trained writer that states these beliefs as text records for a frozen agent. Across two agent families, HabitusMem achieves the best results among memory systems on both PersonaMem-v2 and VitaBench 2.0. Controlled analyses show that learned request conditioning drives the answering gain, while auxiliary training tasks improve tool use.

Large language models (LLMs) can provide voters information about political issues, party positions, and even advise on which party to vote for. However, no previous study explores whether off-the-shelf LLMs could be used as voting advice applications (VAAs) to improve voter-party congruence. In this article, we address this gap by examining how Gemini 3 Flash advises a sample of 6,022 German survey respondents on which party to vote for, based on their policy preferences and priorities alone. In brief, we find that for most respondents, following the LLM's advice would benefit voter-party policy alignment (i.e. congruence), with a significantly stronger remedial effect among voters with low levels of political interest and formal education. Pipelines that let a large language model (LLM) make discrete decisions need to know which decisions they may act on unattended, and a record that explains each one. We study a small, fully specified reconciliation contract: commit the target model's greedy answer (the anchor), draw one or two greedy checks that reach the answer by a different route (an alternate prompt, or a model from another family), and let a pure function return one of five outcomes, of which only Agree is acted on. On 48,000 model-item evaluations over 6,000 questions, with every method given access to verbalized confidence as a tie-breaker, the main finding concerns where calls come from: pooling another family's samples raises semantic entropy's AUROC by 1.7 to 4.0 points at every budget, and a same-model temperature check is the weakest design. Incentivizing User Data Contributions for LLM Improvement under Withdrawal Rights 2026-11-19
Show abstract

As large language models (LLMs) become a common source of information, users may contribute less original knowledge to the public communities on which model development depends. Platforms seeking user contributions must account for privacy and effort costs and the incentive to rely on contributions from others. We study this dilemma in a threshold public-goods model with heterogeneous user costs. Under uniform per-unit subsidies, users can be paid even when aggregate contributions fall short of the improvement threshold. To address this subsidy leakage, we develop a mechanism that combines verifiable cost disclosure, personalized contribution assignments, and voluntary withdrawal. We show that rational assignment leads either to profitable provision or to no collection. Simultaneous withdrawal minimizes contribution costs when both protocols succeed, while small-first sequential withdrawal can sustain provision under a wider range of cost conditions. Numerical experiments quantify the effects on provision probability and social welfare. In an exploratory dialogue experiment, we evaluate LLM agents based on 50 anonymized American Community Survey profiles under all three mechanisms, generating 150 trajectories to compare contribution and withdrawal decisions under matched conditions. These findings clarify how assignment rules and withdrawal timing can support collective data provision while preserving user control.

O-RAN enables a disaggregated baseband stack with programmable functions that communicate over standardized open interfaces. The same openness that enables multi-vendor composition also expands the attack surface across logically decoupled tiers that make up the compute continuum. Resolving entity conflicts is a critical step in Pipeline-based NLU architectures, especially when multiple extractors provide overlapping or conflicting outputs. Traditional rule-based entity conflict resolution methods often lack flexibility and are unscalable. In this work, we aim to study if the broad knowledge of LLMs can be leveraged to resolve entity conflicts in domain specific NLU pipelines. We present an experimental comparison between a traditional rule-based conflict resolution method, and two LLM-based approaches: a multi-shot prompting method using the Llama3.1-8B-Instruct model and a LoRA fine-tuned Llama3.1-8B-Instruct model. Hardening JavaScript Programs against LLM-Based Vulnerability Detection 2026-11-19
Show abstract

JavaScript programs are often distributed as source code, so attackers can analyze them for vulnerabilities. LLM-based vulnerability detection agents now make this analysis much easier. To mitigate this emerging threat, we present AntiAgent, a defense that lets developers harden their programs against such detection agents. AntiAgent transforms a program before release to impede an agent's analysis. To find effective transformations, AntiAgent evolves a corpus of programs with known vulnerabilities and traces how a detection agent analyzes each evolved version. It keeps the versions on which the agent misses or misreports the vulnerability and extracts their transformations into reusable rules. To protect a new program, AntiAgent carefully divides the task between an LLM and the rules, so that the transformations adapt to the program while preserving its semantics. We evaluate AntiAgent on real-world JavaScript applications against four detection agents, including Claude Code and Codex. AntiAgent reduces the number of vulnerabilities that each agent detects by 52.0-60.7%, outperforming the conventional obfuscator JS-Obfuscator and the LLM-based transformation method FPA on every agent. The transformed programs pass all project tests, indicating that AntiAgent preserves the original program semantics. The transformation rules also remain effective on vulnerability types absent from the corpus and on programs written in another language like Python.

Polar: A Benchmark for Evaluating Political Bias in LLMs 2026-11-19

Political bias in large language models (LLMs) is increasingly significant, but difficult to measure reproducibly across political and linguistic contexts. We introduce Polar, a 4,026-instance multiple-choice benchmark that measures political bias through option-level likelihoods rather than prompt-based generation. Polar covers two ideological axes and eight issue categories derived from the Manifesto Project, and evaluates models in parallel across U.S. and South Korean political contexts. Across 38 LLMs, measured bias varies systematically with political context, issue category, model group, and presentation language. All models lean left-progressive on U.S. political content, but show more centered and mixed patterns on South Korean content. Translation experiments further show that presentation language alone can shift measured bias. These findings highlight the need for multilingual and cross-contextual evaluation of political bias in LLMs. The code and dataset are publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/mcrl/Polar) and Hugging Face (https://huggingface.co/datasets/thunder-research-group/Polar).
