Leaving, Staying, or Living There? Duration Commonsense Reasoning with Human-LLM Co-annotations 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Gardening in the backyard implies living there, while checking into an Airbnb more likely means staying for a few days. Such duration judgments are important for understanding human mobility, travel behavior, and social context. However, duration is rarely stated explicitly in social media posts and must instead be inferred from noisy textual, visual, and contextual cues. In this paper, we investigate whether vision-language models possess such duration commonsense. We create a new corpus of geotagged tweets co-annotated by humans and LLMs to serve as a testbed for this task. We find that state-of-the-art models struggle with duration inference, even when guided by multi-turn Chain-of-Thought prompting. Error analyses further reveal that models often fail to integrate critical contextual information, or they overly rely on prominent visual details while overlooking subtle hashtags and semantic nuances. These findings underscore the significant challenges remaining in context-aware multimodal understanding.

AtomTree: A Hierarchical Framework for State-Aware Embodied Instruction Following with LLMs 2026-10-28
📝 Accepted at IROS 2026
Show abstract

Embodied instruction following in interactive environments requires agents to interpret natural language commands and complete long-horizon tasks. Existing planners either rely on large labeled trajectories, or use few-shot and zero-shot LLM prompting that produces a whole plan in a single serial reasoning process. These approaches depend on linguistic common sense, commit to a plan from the initial instruction, and only update after failures, which weakens robustness and state awareness. We present AtomTree, a hierarchical framework for state-aware task planning with LLMs. AtomTree incrementally builds a tree of subgoals and actions, where the LLM decides at each step, from the current symbolic state, whether to further decompose or to output a grounded low-level action. A lightweight memory module summarizes past outcomes for reuse in later decisions. When a low-level action stalls, AtomTree triggers hierarchical backtracking replanning to return to the parent node and regenerate the subtree plan from the latest state, preventing continued drift along an incorrect trajectory. In zero-shot evaluation on the ALFRED benchmark, AtomTree outperforms prior LLM-based planners. Project page: https://zhaihaotian.github.io/atomtree-website/.

Quantum-Inspired Pairwise Ranking Aggregation via Active LLM Feedback 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Ranking aggregation from multiple retrievers is a powerful yet under-explored technique in modern retrieval and RAG systems. We introduce LLM-IRA, the first extension of the quantum-inspired interactive ranking aggregation framework to document reranking. By leveraging large language models (LLMs) solely for pairwise judgments on high-disagreement document pairs, our method learns fusion weights in a principled, interactive way. The final ranking is a simple, ultra-fast pointwise linear fusion of scores, no full pairwise matrix or expensive listwise prompting is required. Experiments on the BEIR benchmark and TREC Deep Learning tracks with an open-weight instruction-tuned judge (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) show that LLM-IRA matches or numerically exceeds strong static fusion baselines on 5 of 6 evaluated benchmarks. We report two weight-update variants: momentum blending and a full accumulated-history refit, which together reach statistical significance (Wilcoxon signed-rank, p<0.05) over the best unsupervised aggregator on 4 of 6 benchmarks (DL19 under momentum; DL20, DBpedia, and NFCorpus under accumulated refit, which is the stronger of the two configurations overall). On the benchmarks we profiled (DL19, DL20), it already clears the best unsupervised baseline within as few as 5 LLM calls per query, well before exhausting its typical ~25--30 call budget. LLM-IRA also achieves cost-effectiveness comparable to state-of-the-art zero-shot LLM rerankers at an order of magnitude fewer LLM calls, and requires no training data or model fine-tuning.

Whom Do AI Agents Serve? Role-Induced Sponsorship Bias in Decision Models and Generative LLMs 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Consumers increasingly delegate product search to AI shopping agents powered either by generative large language models (LLMs) that deliberate in natural language or by non-generative decision models that output calibrated choice probabilities in a single forward pass. Because retail platforms monetize through sponsored placements that shoppers typically discount, we examine whether an AI agent's evaluation of paid listings depends on who delegates the agent across both model families. Across three studies with no instructions to favor advertisers, naming a booking platform (vs. a traveler) as the agent's principal significantly attenuates the penalty applied to sponsored listings. Study 1 establishes this sponsorship bias across generative LLMs, prompt wordings, and reasoning efforts, showing that platform delegation weakens pre-choice skepticism and that naming the consumer as the beneficiary eliminates the bias. Study 2 establishes the bias across six commercial and open-weight decision models on calibrated choice probabilities and shows that consumer-aligned fine-tuning of an open-weight decision model (Kev 9B) across non-hotel retail categories eliminates the bias zero-shot on hotel choices. Study 3 decomposes the disclosure label across both model families, showing that attributing the placement to the platform triggers the sponsorship bias, whereas stricter disclosure wording ("Sponsored" vs. "Promoted") lowers paid-listing evaluation without eliminating this bias.

"Close enough?" An investigation into multiplication errors in LLMs 2026-10-28
📝 Accepted to Neurips 2026 NewInML workshop
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) frequently fail at multi-digit arithmetic, but the underlying mechanisms governing these failures remain poorly understood. While recent work demonstrates that addition errors follow a "noisy quantisation" model localised around carry boundaries, whether this framework generalises to multiplication remains an open question. In this work, we evaluate multiplication capabilities across several small base LLMs on a dataset of 16,991 problems spanning varied operand lengths. We show that multiplication errors do not conform to the addition-style noisy quantisation model. Instead, we uncover a novel error regime in small Qwen models: errors in lower-order columns predominantly preserve parity. Conversely, higher-order digit errors shift toward off-by-one carry failures. Our findings suggest that arithmetic computation in LLMs relies on distinct, multi-periodic internal representations where parity is tracked more robustly than exact digit identity, providing new mechanistic insights into numerical reasoning in transformers.

When LLM Architectures Meet Smart Contract Vulnerability Detection: a Replication Study 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Smart contracts are immutable programs deployed on blockchain platforms, where vulnerabilities can translate directly into financial loss. Among the techniques proposed for their detection, recent work has relied increasingly on Large Language Models (LLMs) guided by carefully crafted prompts. However, the evidence accumulated so far rests almost entirely on the dense Transformer architecture behind the GPT line and on model snapshots already scheduled for deprecation, leaving open the question of whether the reported behaviors reflect a general property of LLMs or an artifact of that specific design. This paper presents an extended replication of the empirical study by Chen et al. on LLM-based smart contract vulnerability detection. We replicate the original protocol on GPT-4 as a baseline, followed by a differentiated replication, executing the protocol on representative models based on Mixture-of-Experts and RNN-like designs, together with an attempted preliminary evaluation of State Space Models. The exact replication confirms the findings of Chen et al., including the high-recall, low-precision detection pattern they report on GPT-4. Nevertheless, the differentiated replication shows that this pattern does not transfer uniformly across the evaluated models. Those from the same architectural family may also exhibit different behaviors, indicating that architectural family alone is insufficient to predict the resulting detection profile. We observe the same qualitative dense-Transformer profile in both GPT-4 and Gemma-3, despite a gap of roughly two orders of magnitude in parameter counts, whereas two Mixture-of-Experts models from different lineages exhibit qualitatively divergent behaviors, indicating that architectural family alone is insufficient to predict the detection profile.

Think Before You Act: Decision Gate for LLM Agents 2026-10-28
Show abstract

LLM agents increasingly perform consequential actions through external tools, yet their proposed actions are often executed without independent verification of policy compliance or task completion. We introduce JevAct, a runtime verification framework that intercepts selected agent actions before execution and enables feedback-driven correction. JevAct compiles domain policies into executable state checks and structured questions evaluated by a prefill-only decision model, which produces probability-based judgments without relying on LLM generation. By combining deterministic verification of tool-state facts with probabilistic assessment of conversational context, the framework identifies potentially invalid actions and premature task termination, providing targeted feedback for bounded revision. JevAct operates independently of the underlying agent model and requires no modifications to its parameters or tools. We evaluate JevAct across eleven LLM agents on $τ^2$-bench Airline and Telecom tasks and BFCL. Results show improvements in agent reliability across multiple models and benchmarks, with particularly consistent gains on Telecom and BFCL, while performance on Airline is more mixed. Overall, these findings suggest that combining structured decision models with executable verification can improve the reliability of tool-using LLM agents.

Repairs for Whom? Evaluator-Executor Mismatch in Selecting Repairs for Failed LLM Agents 2026-10-28
📝 17 pages, 4 figures, 11 tables
Show abstract

When an LLM agent fails, repair methods choose which action to replace and what to put in its place. They often score candidate repairs with greedy replay, a stronger model, an environment oracle, or a judge that reads the failed log. We ask whether these evaluators pick repairs that help the agent that will actually execute them. Holding failed trajectories and candidate single-step repairs fixed, we label every candidate with independent rollouts of the deployed policy and measure how much deployment value each evaluator's choice loses. On failures not screened for repair value, pre-specified tests show that greedy replay loses 0.08-0.13 in expected net success gain in ALFWorld and 0.06-0.13 in net reward in WebShop, relative to selection with the deployed agent's own rollouts. At an equal rollout budget, selecting repairs for Qwen2.5-7B with Qwen2.5-14B rollouts loses 0.25 in ALFWorld and 0.07 in WebShop, twenty 14B rollouts per candidate are worth no more than two of the 7B agent's own, and fresh rollouts confirm that the two models prefer opposite repairs on selected disagreement cases. Between similar-sized models from different families, the losses appear in both directions in both environments (0.07-0.17). The scoring executor also changes repair-location labels and method rankings. Repairs should be verified with the executor that will run them, and repair labels should name their executor.

Efficient Group-Penalty k-means for Text Clustering with Noisy LLM-Generated Constraints 2026-10-28
Show abstract

k-means provides a simple and efficient foundation for text clustering, but embedding alone can miss task-specific semantic boundaries. Must-link (ML) and cannot-link (CL) constraints can correct such errors, yet conventional constrained clustering assumes that these relations are already supplied. LLMs can generate them without task-specific labels, but exhaustive querying is costly and their judgments can be incorrect. We present GPKmeans, an efficient Group-Penalty k-means method for text clustering with noisy LLM-generated constraints. GPKmeans uses LLM as an offline local pairwise annotator, querying selected anchor-candidate pairs for sparse ML links and constructing set-valued CL evidence without all-pairs queries. It converts connected components of the ML-link graph and the generated CL sets into groups with normalized, bounded soft penalties, limiting each group's influence independently of its size. Cached coordinate descent is proposed to efficiently solve the constraints clustering problem. We prove finite-step convergence and derive deterministic and probabilistic oracle-referenced bounds that relate objective distortion to the penalty coefficients and to the group-level error probabilities of the generated constraints. Across nine datasets, GPKmeans uses fewer LLM calls and yields more accurate constraints. It achieves the best average ACC, NMI, and ARI while running approximately 30 times faster than state-of-the-art constrained clustering methods.

Rethinking LLM Jailbreak Evaluation: Continuous Risk Landscapes Beyond Binary Outcomes 2026-10-28
Show abstract

A binary success indicator is a natural representation for discriminative tasks such as image classification under adversarial perturbations, where each input is mapped to a single prediction. However, generative tasks are fundamentally different: an LLM jailbreak prompt induces a distribution over open-ended trajectories, while a sampled response reveals only one path. Yet LLM jailbreak evaluation typically retains the binary response indicator underlying attack success rate, obscuring the surrounding structure of possible generations. We introduce a continuous, context-conditioned risk landscape over first-token branches, estimated by fixing candidate openings and repeatedly sampling their continuations. This continuous risk-landscape perspective reveals two complementary properties: current decoder exposure and safe rerouting capacity, which have been obscured by binary-indicator-based ASR. The resulting representation also provides a common basis for comparing clean prompts, attacked variants, and attack strength against a known adaptive defense. Across multiple target models and jailbreak attacks, we uncover risky branches beneath apparently safe outputs, identify qualitatively different ways attacks reshape the model's opening distribution, and show that attacks strongest before a defense do not necessarily remain the strongest after the adaptive defense reacts. These findings recast jailbreaks as deformations of the surrounding continuation-risk landscape, establishing a more informative foundation for understanding jailbreak behavior and developing safer language models.

A Survey on Open Dataset Search in the LLM Era: Retrospectives and Perspectives 2026-10-28
Show abstract

High-quality datasets are typically required for accomplishing data-driven tasks, such as training medical diagnosis models, predicting real-time traffic conditions, or conducting experiments to validate research hypotheses. Consequently, open dataset search, which aims to ensure the efficient and accurate fulfillment of users' dataset requirements, has emerged as a critical research challenge and has attracted widespread interest. Recent studies have made notable progress in enhancing the flexibility and intelligence of open dataset search, and large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong potential in addressing long-standing challenges in this area. Therefore, a systematic and comprehensive review of the open dataset search problem is essential, detailing the current state of research and exploring future directions. In this survey, we focus on recent advances in open dataset search beyond traditional approaches that rely on metadata and keywords. From the perspective of dataset modalities, we place particular emphasis on example-based dataset search, advanced similarity measurement techniques based on dataset content, and efficient search acceleration techniques. In addition, we emphasize the mutually beneficial relationship between LLMs and open dataset search. On the one hand, LLMs help address complex challenges in query understanding, semantic modeling, and interactive guidance within open dataset search. In turn, advances in dataset search can support LLMs by enabling more effective integration into retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) frameworks and data selection processes, thereby enhancing downstream task performance. Finally, we summarize open research problems and outline promising directions for future work. This work aims to offer a structured reference for researchers and practitioners in the field of open dataset search.

Session-Bound Behavioral Watermarking for Autonomous LLM Agents 2026-10-28
📝 12 pages, 3 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

Large Language Model (LLM) agents are increasingly acting through Web interfaces and external tools. They produce trajectories. Those trajectories may pass through several intermediaries. They can store, modify, or dispute those trajectories. In Web-based agent deployments, trajectories may pass through APIs, marketplaces, and tool servers. As such, a copied or disputed log may not by itself reveal which agent owner produced it. Behavioral watermarking can embed ownership evidence in agent action choices. However, existing methods mainly address detection, removal, rewriting, or imitation. They do not directly test whether repeated observation of watermarked trajectories enables cross-session forgery by exploiting learned action preferences. We formulate this learned-forgery threat and propose AgentAnchor, a session-keyed behavioral watermark for owner attribution from trajectory logs. We derive each step key from a secret key, a fresh public session identifier, and semantic states of recent observations, while a guarded digest of the action-policy state diversifies linear coding rows. It embeds parity evidence through a KL-bounded exponential tilt, and a trusted verifier checks a disputed log without rerunning the agent, using wrong-key calibration and abstention.

In high-risk text interactions, a system may need to estimate whether the current speaker is likely to be a minor before applying age-conditioned safeguards. From Leniency to Suspicion: How an Open Post-Training Recipe Changes Whom LLM Fact-Checkers Believe 2026-10-28
📝 10 pages, 4 figures, 6 tables. Preprint, under review
Show abstract

LLMs increasingly fact-check online content with the poster's identity in view. However, it remains unclear which post-training changes how much a model's truth judgement depends on the poster's social class, and how such a change happens inside the model. This paper investigates job-based doubt, i.e., judging a claim false more often when the poster has a lower-status job, using fact-checked claims with randomised poster profiles in base and post-trained checkpoints of six model families. Our results reveal that: 1) post-training creates such doubt only with the open Tülu-3 recipe (OLMo-2 32B, Tülu-3 70B), not with Meta's, Alibaba's or Mistral's; the effect is under one percentage point but statistically reliable, exceeding training drift and replicating on independent claim sets; 2) in OLMo-2 32B, the model does not start noticing the poster's job more, but starts interpreting it differently: a lower-status job pushes the base model toward "true" and the post-trained model toward "false"; and 3) this flip is carried by a single residual-stream direction and happens at supervised fine-tuning. The mechanism was confirmed on 400 new claims with pre-specified predictions. None of seven deployed commercial models doubts lower-status posters more. Our work underscores the need to audit speaker bias per training recipe.

WebMaze: Defending Against LLM-Powered Web Scanners with Functionality-Coupled Deception 2026-10-28
📝 10 pages, 4 figures
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled more effective vulnerability scanning of web applications. However, these capabilities also make it significantly easier for attackers to automate cyberattacks on the web. In this work, we investigate how to divert malicious LLM-based web scanning, with preservation of genuine functionality as a deployment requirement. We systematically study six LLM-based scanners and summarize how they gather information and iteratively select actions based on observations. Guided by the study, we then present WebMaze, a defense that preserves genuine application functionality while diverting LLM-based scanners to a generated decoy world. WebMaze embeds functionality-coupled traps in genuine pages and constructs view-tailored interactive decoy worlds from the application's existing views. This demonstrates that WebMaze effectively diverts scanners away from genuine application pages and into the generated decoy worlds. Even when explicitly informed about the decoy mechanism and instructed to avoid decoy pages, scanners continue to explore predominantly decoy pages.

Temporal Evidence-Parameter Co-Alignment for LLM Reasoning over Temporal Knowledge Graphs 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with temporally grounded reasoning because their static parametric memory is misaligned with the dynamic evolution of real-world knowledge. Although RAG and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) provide practical ways to enhance LLMs, they remain insufficient for temporal reasoning. Existing retrieval methods typically prioritize semantic relevance while overlooking temporal validity, event order, and evolutionary dependencies, resulting in evidence that is relevant in isolation but temporally fragmented. Meanwhile, conventional PEFT methods usually employ fixed and input-agnostic low-rank updates, making it difficult to capture time-dependent entity semantics and relation drift. To address these limitations, we propose Temporal Evidence-Parameter Co-Alignment (TEPCA), a framework for LLM reasoning over TKGs. TEPCA aligns LLM reasoning with temporal knowledge at both the evidence and parameter levels. At the evidence level, it introduces time-constrained Dynamic Relevance-Guided Path (DRGP) pruning to preserve globally coherent temporal paths, together with Mutual-Information (MI)-based pruning to retain query-informative local facts under temporal constraints. At the parameter level, it integrates Dynamic-Gated LoRA (DG-LoRA), a timestamp-conditioned low-rank adaptation mechanism that dynamically modulates the effective adaptation subspace according to temporal context. This design enables LLMs to better capture fine-grained entity evolution and time-dependent relation semantics while preserving parameter efficiency. Experimental results on two TKG benchmarks demonstrate that TEPCA achieves state-of-the-art performance. Additional evaluations of DG-LoRA on eight commonsense QA datasets further verify its general input-adaptive capacity, scalability across LLM backbones, and interpretability.

Rethinking Bayesian Optimization for Co-Optimizing LLM Training Configurations 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Fine-tuning an LLM to maximize performance on a downstream task requires finding the optimal data and model configuration. This is a black-box optimization problem that Bayesian optimization (BO) addresses by sequentially evaluating training configurations and using observed performance feedback to adaptively guide the search towards better configurations. However, directly applying BO to the joint data-model space suffers from two fundamental challenges: the prohibitive cost of full LLM training at each iteration, and the large number of BO iterations required for effective exploration due to the high problem dimensionality. This paper introduces JoBS, a BO-based approach that co-optimizes data and model configurations without requiring a full training run at every iteration. JoBS allocates an initial portion of the optimization budget to learn a scaling-law-inspired performance predictor that estimates fully trained LLM performance from only a small number of training steps. The remaining budget is then used to run BO with this predictor, eliminating the need for full training runs and enabling JoBS to explore significantly more data and model configurations within the same budget. We analyze JoBS's average regret and derive the optimal budget allocation between predictor learning and BO iterations. Empirically, JoBS outperforms independent data and model optimization methods and existing multi-fidelity BO baselines across a diverse set of downstream tasks. Our code is available at https://github.com/chenzhiliang94/BO-for-LLMs.

Holistic Diversity Management in LLM-Guided Evolutionary Search 2026-10-28
Show abstract

LLM-guided evolutionary search iteratively selects existing programs, uses LLMs as mutation operators to generate a new program, and evaluates it to guide subsequent search. To preserve diversity and reduce the risk of premature convergence, it often adopts the island model, in which groups of programs, called islands, evolve separately. However, this structure alone does not guarantee distinct search directions, as islands may start from similar programs or converge toward similar solutions during evolution. This suggests that diversity must be considered throughout the search process, from initializing islands with distinct programs to developing island trajectories and sharing discoveries across islands. To this end, we propose HoliDivEvolve , a holistic approach to establishing and effectively utilizing island diversity throughout evolutionary search. Specifically, we introduce diverse initialization to establish different initial programs, trajectory adaptive island selection to allocate computation based on current score, recent growth, and the remaining search budget, and growth adaptive migration to share alternative ideas across islands. Experiments across diverse benchmarks show that HoliDivEvolve achieves competitive performance on most evaluated tasks. We further conduct controlled analyses to examine how our design choices affect search behavior and final performance.

LLMs as Judges and Examinees for Medical Item Difficulty Prediction 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Predicting medical item difficulty from item text alone remains challenging. Prior work has largely relied on linguistic features or text representations derived from general-purpose and domain-specific language models, but predictive performance has remained modest. This study takes a different approach by using difficulty-related signals that emerge when open-weight large language models (LLMs) interact with test items. Using USMLE and PANCE medical licensure examination items, we examined LLMs in two roles. As judges, the models provided absolute difficulty ratings and pairwise difficulty comparisons. As simulated examinees, they generated response-based signals including correctness, keyed-option probability, response entropy, and the probability margin between the top two options. We then combined the strongest judgment and response signals with baseline item features in downstream prediction models. Pairwise difficulty judgment was the most consistent individual LLM signal across both datasets, while simulated-examinee signals and absolute judgments provided complementary information in some feature combinations. The best configurations reached a Spearman correlation of $ρ$ = .447 on USMLE, exceeding the best system in the 2024 BEA shared task ($ρ$ = .25), and $ρ$ = .494 on PANCE. These results were comparable to direct absolute judgments from the proprietary frontier model Claude Opus 5 ($ρ$ = .443 and .488, respectively). Overall, judgment and response signals from open-weight LLMs captured difficulty-related information beyond text representations. Because these models can be deployed locally, the approach offers a practical option for predicting the difficulty of unreleased or high-security test items.

Uncovering Why LLMs Fail: From Internal Mechanisms to Adversarial Reasoning Trajectories 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are usually evaluated by final-output correctness or safety, yet an endpoint label does not reveal how a failure develops throughout the reasoning process. We therefore ask whether ordinary reasoning failures and adversarially induced failures can be characterized using the same internal coordinates. Towards this end, we in this paper study a white-box diagnostic framework CauTrace to disclose the root-cause of why LLM fails from three fundamental inspections: Functional States, Propagation of Representation, Temporal-State Coordination and Reasoning Trajectory. First, seven functional states are creatively defined to represent the support and constraint processing in LLMs. Second, the propagation of representation change measures how the changes among the seven states persist across subsequent Transformer blocks. Third, temporal-state coordination and trajectory topology characterize when the commitment and verification occur relative to the support, and how the dominant state sequence evolves over the full inference process. Finally, we evaluate CauTrace on both clean reasoning failures and paired adversarial perturbations. Across Qwen and LLaMA-family models, the failed traces show weaker Attention-MLP agreement on support-state updates and less verification activity relative to commitment, while the exact representation-level signatures and timing shifts vary by architecture. Held-out repair and steering tests demonstrate consistent operational signals that were in line with our claims for certain models and specific settings, with aligned steering producing the predicted directional changes in process measures. Overall, CauTrace provides a unified diagnostic framework for analyzing failure dynamics through changes in support, commitment, and verification, which would shed light on the in-depth research on LLM security.

Compute Has Shape: Self-Morphing the Internal Computation of Frozen LLMs 2026-10-28
Show abstract

Test-time scaling improves large language models through additional computation, but how to adapt the topology of internal computation to individual inputs remains underexplored. Existing approaches largely fix recurrent or parallel architectures during training, or reconfigure frozen models along a single structural axis. We introduce SelfMorph, which makes computational shape an input-dependent inference-time decision. It unifies the original forward path (BASE), serial reuse of a layer span (REPEAT), and local branching and merging of perturbed hidden states (BRANCH) in a unified space parameterized by type, location, and intensity. Since they offer complementary benefits rather than uniform dominance, we use a lightweight selector that reads the backbone's depth-wise hidden state trajectory on the prompt to choose a morphology before response generation.
