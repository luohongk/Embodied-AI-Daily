Re-Search: Dissecting LLM Ideation Through a Two-Player Questioning Game 2026-11-10
📝 Preprint. 15 figures, 10 tables
Show abstract

Research ideation is iterative: researchers refine their ideas step by step based on feedback. Yet existing evaluations obscure this search process, making it difficult to understand how LLMs explore the space of research ideas and what limits their progress. We introduce Re-Search, which adapts the format of Twenty Questions to research ideation: given only the research problem of a published paper, a model must rediscover the paper's core idea by asking yes-or-no questions. Built from 200 ICML 2026 papers, Re-Search tracks how a model's idea evolves as it asks questions and receives binary oracle feedback, isolating ideation from execution. Across six recent LLMs, nearly all progress happens within the first 30 of 100 questions, which largely decide how the game ends; after that, most models plateau. Through controlled interventions on the candidate questions and the hypothesis space, we find that the bottleneck is generation, not judgment: models recognize a good idea or a good question when one is offered, but often cannot produce it on their own. We further analyze the ideation traces and find that models fail by either over-exploiting one direction or over-exploring too many. These findings point to concrete design principles for autoresearch systems: since models can judge candidates they cannot produce, enriching the search with externally sourced ideas and questions may be more effective than improving any single model's generation ability.

Learning to Reason from Reference Samples Alone: Reinforced Adversarial Learning for Modern LLM Post-Training 2026-11-10
📝 31 pages, 6 figures, 10 tables, including references and appendices
Show abstract

In many settings where LLM post-training is desirable, high-quality answers are recognizable but difficult to score with explicit rewards. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is limited to tasks where an explicit correctness signal is available. Prior adversarial approaches to training reasoning LLMs either complement verifiable rewards with co-trained critics or require reference reasoning traces. We ask whether a co-trained LLM judge can turn reference answers alone into the sole reward for on-policy post-training of reasoning LLMs. We call this method Reinforced Adversarial Learning (RAL). We first validate RAL on three verifiable tasks (integer multiplication, code-based arithmetic-expression synthesis, and SMILES-to-formula translation), using the verifiers only for evaluation. With sufficiently capable judges and enough training, RAL matches direct RLVR. We then apply RAL to scientific abstract generation in two settings. In the first setting, RAL guides the generator to produce abstracts in the scientific domain of the reference abstracts, even though the prompt leaves that domain unspecified. In the second setting, we generate abstracts for U.S. National Institutes of Health grant proposals conditioned on the grant title. Human scientists and an ensemble of LLM judges find our generations harder to distinguish from real abstracts than those of frontier-LLM baselines. These results suggest a path toward post-training reasoning models in scientific and other high-value domains, where increasingly capable judges infer hard-to-specify reward functions from example answers.

Evaluating LLM-Generated World Models for Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Formal world models can support planning, verification, and safety reasoning, but constructing them from open-ended natural-language situations is difficult, particularly in multi-agent settings where several formalizations may be plausible. We study whether five LLMs can generate such world models as partially observable stochastic games and whether mechanical validity is sufficient to establish that the resulting model faithfully represents the intended decision problem. We introduce a benchmark of 44 scenarios spanning game, situational, and cultural settings and evaluate generated world models along three dimensions: mechanical validity, reference-relative structural agreement, and semantic criteria. Across three empirical tracks, we analyze 410 first-pass generations. In the reference-backed game track, 134/135 generated world models pass a strict mechanical audit, yet mean permutation-invariant structural F1 is only 0.366, with just 28/134 mechanically valid models exactly matching the authored reference. In the cultural track, explicitly naming the country changes the generated structural representation for most LLMs while producing only small changes in automated semantic scores. These results show that executability is a necessary but insufficient criterion for LLM-generated world models: models that are mechanically valid can still differ substantially in the structure used by downstream neuro-symbolic reasoning and planning.

Towards end-to-end LLM-based censoring-aware survival analysis 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Objective: Survival analysis is central to medical prediction, yet large language models (LLMs) are rarely used as end-to-end survival models because censoring prevents straightforward supervised fine-tuning. Here we present LLMSurvival, a framework that enables censoring-aware survival analysis with unmodified LLMs operating directly on tabular clinical data. Materials and Methods: LLMSurvival reformulates time-to-event prediction as pairwise ranking among comparable subjects, and derives test-time risk by aggregating comparisons against anchor individuals from the training cohort. Results: Across two clinical tasks (ICU mortality prediction in MIMIC-IV and fragility fracture prediction in a NewYork-Presbyterian/Weill Cornell Medicine cohort), LLMSurvival improves overall concordance over Cox proportional hazards modeling by 3.1% for ICU mortality and 0.5% for fracture risk, 2.1% on average for ICU mortality and 2.8% for fracture risk over three established deep learning survival models. Discussion: The results show that survival modeling with censoring can be made compatible with LLM fine-tuning through comparison-based reformulation. The framework demonstrates high portability and superior performance over expert curated scores like SAPS-II and FRAX scores across diverse clinical context. Furthermore, the framework supports local deployment, as compact, publicly available base models provide sufficient performance. Conclusion: The LLMSurvival framework serves as a proof of concept for an integrated, censoring-conscious approach to survival analysis via LLMs.

EngineAgent: Agentizing On-Device LLM Inference Engines with Inference Stack Hierarchy Awareness and Hardware Abilities 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Rapidly evolving LLM architectures and heterogeneous mobile hardware make supporting new models in on-device inference engines a recurring and costly system challenge. Each model may require coordinated changes across conversion, graph construction, operators, and hardware backends. Existing coding agents often miss these cross-layer dependencies and struggle to diagnose failures across host--device execution paths. We present EngineAgent, an agentic system for automating on-device LLM model enablement. Its key insight is to separate reusable engine and hardware knowledge from model-specific implementation. Before enablement, EngineAgent constructs a hierarchy-aware engine index that captures engine abstraction layers and cross-layer dependencies, and pre-validates backend-specific build, execution, and debugging workflows using already supported models. For each new model, a builder uses this knowledge to coordinate cross-layer changes, while an auditor checks implementation completeness and drives iterative debugging through device-side validation. Across 12 tasks spanning three mobile LLMs and four engine--backend pairs on CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs, EngineAgent achieves 75% end-to-end success with either of two underlying LLMs, versus 33.3% for the strongest coding-agent baseline. Success requires compilation, on-device execution, and output-logit agreement with reference implementations. EngineAgent also reduces average per-task time and model-API cost relative to baselines using the same underlying LLMs.

Highly Efficient and Effective LLMs with Multi-Boolean Architectures 2026-11-10
📝 ICLR 2026 (Main Conference)
Show abstract

Weight binarization has emerged as a promising strategy to reduce the complexity of large language models (LLMs). Existing approaches fall into post-training binarization, which is simple but causes severe performance loss, and training-aware methods, which depend on full-precision latent weights, adding complexity and limiting efficiency. We propose a novel framework that represents LLMs with multi-kernel Boolean parameters and, for the first time, enables direct finetuning LMMs in the Boolean domain, eliminating the need for latent weights. When Should Agents Remember? Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution for LLM Agents 2026-11-10
Show abstract

LLM agents increasingly operate in long-horizon, verifier-rich environments, but current self-improvement mechanisms often write episode-derived lessons directly into memory, making local success an unreliable signal for reusable and safe knowledge. This paper addresses the core question of when an experience-derived behavioral update should be admitted into persistent agent state. We propose Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution (FGSE), a verifier-grounded framework that represents each candidate lesson as a structured hypothesis with an explicit precondition, behavior change, expected effect, and verifier. FGSE applies the hypothesis only in a temporary state, tests it on target-transfer, falsification, and archived-regression probes, and then commits, refines, or rejects it according to measured gain and risk. Across web, app, tool-use, and long-term memory benchmarks, FGSE achieves strong task performance, including 39.8 WebArena success rate, 78.8 AppWorld average completion, and 0.724/0.487 Pass$^1$ on $τ$-Retail/$τ$-Airline, while reducing falsification failures to 5.0%, regression damage to 1.3%, and harmful committed updates to 3.6%. These results suggest that testing memory updates as hypotheses supports more reliable long-term adaptation.

Don't Just Trust Your Memory: Evidence-Linked Memory Reduces Memory-Induced Degradation in LLM Agents 2026-11-10
📝 Submitted to ACL Rolling Review (October 2026 cycle); under review
Show abstract

External memory enables LLM agents to improve from experience by distilling trajectories of past tasks into concise lessons for new tasks. However, memory can also lead to memory-induced degradation, where an agent fails on tasks it would solve without memory, often because it follows a lesson that is incorrect, or misapplies or misreads a valid one. This is because lessons are inferred from trajectories labeled only with task success or failure, yet are typically provided without the trajectories they came from, leaving the agent no way to check them. To address this, we introduce WikiTrace, an evidence-linked memory system designed to let agents check each lesson against its original trajectory. Specifically, motivated by the finding that a trajectory often still holds the evidence needed to catch an erroneous lesson, WikiTrace stores each lesson as a concise insight linked to a summary of why its trajectory succeeded or failed and to the raw trajectory itself. Then, on a new task, the agent starts from all insights and, whenever one lacks details needed to act, conflicts with another, or comes from a failed task, descends through its links with terminal tools to fill in missing details or correct the insight before acting. We validate WikiTrace on five agentic benchmarks, showing that it consistently outperforms existing memory approaches and a baseline given the same insights alone, while also reducing memory-induced degradation. Our code is available at https://github.com/yukyeongleee/wikitrace

Whose Guardrail? User Identity Shapes the Effectiveness of Model Disclaimers in Mitigating LLM Political Persuasion 2026-11-10
📝 Correction were made to the appendix, citations, and author list
Show abstract

Politically biased AI can shift users' opinions, yet it remains unclear whether model disclaimers protect against such persuasion. We report a preregistered experiment (N=1,586) examining how disclaimer design and user characteristics shape susceptibility to politically biased AI. We varied whether disclaimers identified political bias versus potential inaccuracies and whether they included an explanation. Participants completed two decision-making tasks with an LLM biased against their political views, with outcomes assessed immediately and one week later. Disclaimers identifying political bias and including an explanation reduced persuasion, but effects varied across contexts and did not persist. Individual differences were more consistent predictors: Republicans were more persuaded and reported higher trust than Democrats across tasks. Similarly, participants who used AI more frequently were also more persuaded, whereas those with greater self-reported AI knowledge were less persuaded. These findings suggest that generic disclaimers are not enough: effective safeguards must account for user heterogeneity and context.

Beyond Third-Person Audits: Situated Interaction Auditing for User-Centered LLM Bias Research 2026-11-10
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

Research on bias in large language models (LLMs) has predominantly focused on third-person audits, which study how models represent or evaluate demographic groups as external subjects. However, this paradigm overlooks a structural blind spot because the user is absent from the audit. In practice, LLMs are used in open-ended, personal interactions, during which the model implicitly represents the user and adjusts its responses accordingly. When identical requests yield different responses depending on who is asking, bias manifests not in how the model describes others but in how it treats its interlocutor. Empirical first-person bias studies are multiplying, but they use different cues, outcomes, and validation choices, which makes their results hard to compare. We propose Situated Interaction Auditing (SIA), a shared diagnostic framework for this literature. It introduces a typology of user profile signals---names and surface markers, writing style and dialect, and stated identity---together with design principles and outcome measures that let studies be compared and accumulated. We instantiate the framework in a case study of Chilean first and last names across nine task domains and three models, and we close with guidance for practitioners and open questions for researchers.

PARLEY: Evaluating LLM Collaboration Through Cooperative Asymmetric Puzzles 2026-11-10
📝 12 pages, 8 figures, 2 tables
Show abstract

As large language models power autonomous agents, understanding how they collaborate and where they fail becomes critical. We introduce PARLEY, an extensible platform for studying LLM agent collaboration through four asymmetric puzzle tasks grounded in cognitive psychology (referential communication, joint coordination, perspective-taking, and spatial reasoning). GPT-4o agent pairs succeeded in 60.5% of conversations (N=400, 100 per task), with task-level variation (34-80%) reflecting distinct collaborative demands. From these conversations, we derived the data-driven PAC taxonomy: six failure types organized into Perception (visual interpretation and spatial reasoning), Adaptation (progress tracking and feedback integration), and Coordination (verification and grounding). Failure to incorporate external feedback was a critical indicator of unsuccessful collaboration, with agents repeatedly applying unsuccessful strategies. We also show that a taxonomy-guided intervention (N=200) improved average task success to 70.5%. These results demonstrate how PARLEY can be used to derive meaningful, data-driven insights that can in turn lead to improved agentic collaboration.

TRACE: Business Rule-Grounded Reasoning Curriculum for Knowledge-Preserving Parametric Tool Retrieval in Enterprise LLMs 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Parametric retrieval enables LLMs to retrieve tools implicitly by assigning each API a unique virtual token and training the model to generate it via constrained beam search. Toolsense shows that this regime has two critical drawbacks: it destroys parametric tool knowledge during training, and its beam-search decoding is too slow for real-time deployment. We introduce TRACE (Tool Retrieval via Augmented Chain-of-thought and Enterprise rules), a two-stage curriculum that resolves this dissociation. Stage 1 reuses the multi-format memorization SFT from ToolSense to seed tool knowledge with LoRA. Stage 2 is our core contribution: the model is trained to emit a thinking trace before producing a JSON list of tool tokens, using two data sources -- RRB pairs from ToolSense and queries synthesized to target business rules curated by domain experts -- both augmented with reasoning traces. This training objective preserves Stage 1 MCQ and QA probing accuracy while enabling single-beam greedy decoding at production latency. Evaluated on a combined enterprise catalog of 8,300+ tools across two enterprise product lines, TRACE training for Stage 2 not only preserves but improves tool understanding: MCQ accuracy gains +3.2 pp and QA probing gains +9 pp over Stage 1. On retrieval, TRACE achieves ~86% recall on Domain A and ~60% on Domain B -- compared to embedding baseline performance of ~27% & ~52% -- both with single-beam greedy decoding, making it directly deployable at production latency.

Scoped Semantic Invariance as a Foundation for LLM Reliability 2026-11-10
📝 Accepted at EMNLP 2026
Show abstract

LLMs often answer semantically equivalent queries inconsistently, even when the underlying facts are known. We formalize consistency as semantic invariance over equivalence classes of queries and show that invariance under arbitrary meaning-preserving transformations is undecidable in general. This motivates scoped semantic invariance, where model behavior is evaluated relative to bounded semantic domains. We operationalize this framework using knowledge graphs (KGs), which act as explicit semantic control structures defining local semantic constraints and grounding contexts. Across the MITRE D3FEND ontology and eight Wikidata- derived domains, we show that inconsistencies are structured rather than random, concentrating in localized hotspot subgraphs organized around recurring TREE, STAR, and CYCLE patterns that remain stable across models and domains. We further show that selective graph grounding improves correctness and cross-model consistency, while indiscriminate grounding introduces regressions. A layered grounding strategy achieves the strongest monotonicity profile, preserving correct knowledge while reducing inconsistency. Finally, we show that hotspot structures discovered from simple consistency probes transfer to more realistic compositional reasoning tasks.

Known By Their Actions: Fingerprinting LLM Browser Agents via UI Traces 2026-11-10
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

As LLM-based agents increasingly browse the web on users' behalf, a natural question arises: can websites passively identify which underlying model powers an agent? Doing so would represent a significant security risk, enabling targeted attacks tailored to known model vulnerabilities. Across 14 frontier LLMs and four web environments spanning information retrieval and shopping tasks, we show that an agent's actions and interaction timings, captured via a passive JavaScript tracker, are sufficient to identify the underlying model with up to 96\% F1 within a fixed harness. Although fingerprints are harness-specific, with cross-harness transfer falling to near chance, pooling traces across harnesses recovers model identification at 0.74 to 0.81 Macro-F1. We further show that strong classifiers can be trained from roughly one third of the available traces, and that identification improves steadily as more events are observed. Injecting randomised timing delays between actions substantially degrades classifier performance, but does not provide robust protection: a classifier retrained on delayed traces largely recovers performance. We release our code and a labelled corpus of agent traces at https://github.com/KabakaWilliam/known_actions.

Hx2Dx: From Anamnesis to Diagnosis - A Multimodal Arena for Conversational Clinical Reasoning in LLMs 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) achieve expert-level diagnostic accuracy in static evaluations, where the model receives a curated vignette or case report that already condenses the diagnostically relevant findings. Such evaluations measure inference over already selected evidence; the evaluations do not account for the inquiry used to obtain it. In clinical practice, however, diagnosis is an iterative process of inquiry. Clinicians take the patient's history, or anamnesis (Hx), through targeted questioning, examine the patient, and request and interpret tests, progressively narrowing the differential until they reach a final diagnosis (Dx). We introduce Hx2Dx ARENA, an interactive benchmark of 1'000 clinical cases from the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM). Each case is a simulated encounter that proceeds from initial presentation through history taking and physical examination to diagnostic workup. We evaluate open- and closed-source LLMs under multiple configurations. Performance varies considerably across models in the interactive setting, and every model loses diagnostic accuracy relative to the static setting, where the full case is presented upfront. The ability to ask the right questions accounts for much of both the gap between models and the loss of accuracy: diagnosis improves as questions become more targeted and build a more complete clinical picture. LLMs thus reason well once the relevant data are available but remain limited in hypothesis-driven information gathering, in knowing what to ask in order to know. Closing this gap is a prerequisite for their safe integration into real-world diagnostic workflows.

GSQ: Highly-Accurate Low-Precision Scalar Quantization for LLMs via Gumbel-Softmax Sampling 2026-11-10
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 27 pages. Code: https://github.com/IST-DASLab/GSQ
Show abstract

Quantization has become a standard tool for efficient LLM deployment, especially for local inference, where models are now routinely served at 2-3 bits per parameter. The state of the art is currently split into simple scalar quantization techniques, such as GPTQ or AWQ, which are widely deployed but plateau in accuracy at 3-4 bits per parameter (bpp), and "second-generation" vector- or trellis-quantized methods, such as QTIP, GPTVQ and AQLM, which push the accuracy frontier but are notoriously hard to implement and to scale. In this paper, we ask whether this gap is fundamental, or whether a carefully optimized $\textit{scalar}$ quantizer can recover most of it. We answer in the affirmative, by introducing GSQ (Gumbel-Softmax Quantization), a post-training scalar quantization method which jointly learns the per-coordinate grid assignments and the per-group scales using a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation of the discrete grid. GSQ matches the cardinality of the relaxation to the small number of levels available in the target bit-width regime (e.g., 3-8 levels for ternary and 3 bpp, respectively), making optimization tractable. Practically, on the standard Llama-3.1-8B/70B-Instruct models, GSQ closes most of the gap between scalar quantization and the QTIP frontier at 2 and 3 bits, while using a symmetric scalar grid with group-wise quantization, and thus remains compatible with existing scalar inference kernels. We further show that the same discrete-assignment optimization can be applied to practical GGUF K-Quant checkpoints: starting from publicly released GGUF models, GSQ improves accuracy while projecting the result back into the same deployment format. Finally, GSQ scales to trillion-scale Mixture-of-Experts models such as Kimi-K2.5, where vector-quantized methods are difficult to apply. The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/IST-DASLab/GSQ.

Machine Hearing: Can Audio LLMs Generalize Beyond Human Auditory Perception? 2026-11-10
📝 22 pages, 7 figures, 5 tables
Show abstract

Multimodal LLMs have made remarkable progress in perceiving the physical world, while their perceptual scope remains largely human-centric, focusing on what humans can directly see or hear. This progress raises the prospect of perception beyond human limits, such as extending hearing to sounds that are inaudible to humans. More broadly, the physical world is full of waves, vibrations, and oscillations, only a small fraction of which are directly audible to humans. This invites the extension of multimodal LLMs toward a broader form of machine hearing, which remains largely underexplored. To investigate this direction, we introduce the Machine Hearing Benchmark (MHB), comprising 6,128 instances across 13 tasks and seven domains. MHB spans highly heterogeneous input regimes, with sampling rates from approximately $10^{-5}$ to $10^6$ Hz, sequence lengths from $10^1$ to $10^6$ samples, and both single- and multi-channel inputs. Evaluations of current open-source and proprietary Audio LLMs (ALLMs) reveal limited zero-shot transfer to machine hearing. Supervised adaptation on machine-hearing data alone does not close this gap, suggesting that the challenge extends beyond data exposure to a systematic mismatch between existing audio front ends and machine-hearing inputs. To address this mismatch, we introduce MachEar, a lightweight encoder extension that complements the pretrained Audio LLM for machine-hearing inputs. The results show that MachEar equips a pretrained Audio LLM with stronger machine-hearing capabilities while largely preserving its existing audio understanding performance.

Controlled Knowledge Engineering for Taxonomy Induction with Tool-Grounded LLM Agents and Runtime-Adaptive Policies 2026-11-10
📝 Submitted to Knowledge-Based Systems. Code: https://github.com/bschaarmann/TIGA
Show abstract

Taxonomies are structured knowledge representations that support semantic search, recommendation, product catalog organization, and retrieval-augmented generation. Their automated construction from a fixed set of candidate entities requires more than plausible text generation: the resulting hierarchy must be complete, structurally valid, and semantically coherent. Large Language Models (LLMs) are promising for this task, but iterative free-form generation remains vulnerable to omitted or hallucinated entities, invalid hierarchical relations, and inconsistent organizational principles. We introduce TIGA (Taxonomy Induction with Tool-Grounded Agents), a knowledge-engineering architecture that formulates taxonomy induction as an iterative decision process over an explicit state and constrained action space. TIGA combines executable taxonomy-editing tools, an memory that records tool calls, rationales, and outcomes, and a runtime-adaptive organizational policy that the agent revises during inference. An optional global reviewer evaluates the constructed taxonomy against the candidate set and the active policy. We evaluate TIGA on four benchmark taxonomies using two LLM backbones and compare it with single-shot and iterative prompting-based baselines. TIGA achieves the strongest structural-quality results in most evaluated configurations, but does not dominate on every precision measure. The gains are accompanied by substantially higher token consumption, API-call volume, and inference time. Ablation results show consistent benefits from the policy and interaction-memory components, whereas the effect of global review depends on the dataset and the available revision budget. These findings characterize tool grounding and runtime policy adaptation as promising but costly mechanisms for improving the controlled construction of structured knowledge representations.

Continual Instruction Tuning for LLM-ased Management of Continuum Resources 2026-11-10
📝 Accepted at the Workshop on Research and Innovation in Scalable and Efficient Deep Learning (RISE-DL 2026), co-located with the 16th International Conference on the Internet of Things (IoT 2026), Newcastle upon Tyne, UK, November 2026. The Version of Record will appear in the ACM proceedings of IoT 2026
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly explored for resource management across the Cloud-Edge continuum. However, embedding LLMs directly inside latency-sensitive control loops places inference latency, execution jitter, and hallucination risks on the critical path. This study argues for a different use of LLMs: a continually instruction-tuned intent compiler that runs once per management intent or declared context change, above a fast, deterministic tactical controller that handles all low-latency runtime actions. We formulate intent compilation as a continual instruction tuning problem, because management intents arrive sequentially and accumulate while infrastructure and workload contexts drift. Unlike recent LLM-based management frameworks, our design scores the model's outputs by execution, so that catastrophic forgetting can be measured in the executed objective of the underlying controller. On a continuum simulator with a 2B backbone and parameter-efficient adapters, preliminary results show that naive sequential tuning forgets earlier intents in the executed objective, that replay of stored examples nearly prevents this forgetting, and that the tuned compact compiler performs on par with simpler translators while being more robust than a grammar parser to rephrased intents.

TAPCO-G: LLM-Guided Design-Adaptive Timing and Power Co-Optimization with Graph-Based Physical Priors 2026-11-10
📝 27 pages

Post-placement timing and power co-optimization is critical to meeting setup timing targets and reducing power within placement constraints. A cell replacement can improve timing along one path while increasing power or reducing slack on others. Conventional optimizers follow fixed rules for scheduling and accepting repairs, limiting their ability to address design-specific bottlenecks. A large language model (LLM) can propose changes to these rules, but on its own it cannot reliably estimate how a cell replacement affects endpoint slack and power. TAPCO-G combines LLM-guided optimizer adaptation with graph-based physical priors to evaluate candidate cell replacements. The outer loop tunes tool settings using physical-design feedback and revises executable repair policies when tuning stalls. It combines these estimates with coarse-to-fine power screening to prioritize candidates for physical trials. TAPCO-G produced placement-legal, logic-equivalent implementations and outscored CERBERUS on all eight ASAP7 benchmarks from the MLCAD 2026 Contest, with a mean quality score gain of 5.516 points, and scored 21.648 points higher than TAPCO on average. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed co-optimization framework.

Market Regime Council for Dynamic Credit Assignment in Multi-Agent LLM Decision Systems 2026-11-10
📝 40 pages, 13 figures. Accepted author manuscript, NeurIPS 2026 (Main Track). Code and demo data available open source
Show abstract

Multi-agent LLM decision systems for portfolio management still lack a principled way to assign credit across specialist agents, remain vulnerable to cold-start dominance under regime shifts, and offer limited transparency into how final allocations are formed. We propose Market Regime Council (MRC), an online credit-assignment mechanism over coalitions of specialist agents, with portfolio management as the downstream task. MRC computes exact Shapley credits across all single, pairwise, and Grand-coalition outputs for online agent weighting. Instantiated with N=3 specialist agents, at each trading period, MRC recomputes coalition-based Shapley weights from exponentially weighted performance histories, uses a Bayesian adaptive mixture to stabilize early periods, applies regime-dependent multipliers to adjust agent authority, and records each rebalance through a five-layer causal trace. Over 1,037 trading days across 13 crypto assets and five seeds, MRC achieves a Sharpe ratio of 1.51 and a cumulative return of 440.1%, ranking first on CR, SR, and IR among active baselines and attaining the lowest MDD among active methods. Ablation results show that the gains come from Shapley-weighted integration across coalition outputs rather than from any single stage in isolation. Code and demo data are available open source.

Scaling Shapley Energy Attribution to Large LLM Batches 2026-11-10
📝 13 pages, 11 figures
Show abstract

LLM services have become essential infrastructure for applications such as chat assistants and coding agents. Service providers need to attribute resource consumption to individual requests for purposes such as billing. Among these resources, energy is a key metric for assessing the cost of serving each request. However, serving engines batch requests together, and GPUs report only aggregate energy consumption. Providers typically estimate each request's share in proportion to its token consumption, but this approach can produce substantially unfair estimates. The Shapley value provides a principled solution to fair energy allocation, but computing it directly requires $2^N-1$ GPU runs for $N$ requests, which is infeasible for large batches. To address this problem, we propose StepSplit. Across GPU generations, we observe that the energy consumed by each inference step, a forward pass over a batch, consists of a shared energy, largely due to reading model weights, and a request-specific energy. We derive a closed-form Shapley value: each request is charged its own energy plus an equal part of the shared energy of every step in which it participates. A microbenchmark of 2.5-17.8 minutes calibrates both parts of the step energy once per model and GPU configuration. Under static batching, StepSplit uses this closed form in a single pass over the scheduler log. Under continuous batching, removing a request can change how the remaining requests are batched, so StepSplit adds a correction to the closed form, computed by replaying the scheduler on a CPU. We evaluate StepSplit against Shapley values measured using 529,513 GPU runs.
