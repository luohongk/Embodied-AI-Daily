Same Simulation, Different Evidence: Where Retrieved Tool Output Costs LLM Agents Accuracy 2026-10-19
📝 15 pages, 4 figures
Show abstract

LLM agents that compute the right answer can still answer wrongly. Agent harnesses increasingly keep long tool outputs out of the context window, saving them to files that the agent searches or reads on demand. We ask where this costs accuracy with a real scientific tool: PHREEQC, a widely used geochemistry simulator from the USGS, whose reports reach 846.8k characters and whose determinism lets every printed value be traced. Agents use it to answer 158 execution-verified multiple-choice items. When agents read the simulator's report through a section index rather than in context, GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2 lose 11.3 and 7.9 points of accuracy, while Claude Opus 4.6 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 show no detectable cost. Re-running all 11,007 logged simulator calls shows that both GPT models compute the keyed value about as often as before; what changes is the evidence they answer from. Under retrieval they often skip the report, answer from their own higher-precision output files, and misround to a neighboring option, a failure we call evidence substitution. With the simulation held fixed, retrieval still costs GPT-5.2 9.4 points. For GPT-5.4, more frequent correct simulations mask an evidence-use loss of about ten points, leaving a net change indistinguishable from zero. Naming the authoritative output raises accuracy by 6.3-7.9 points over plain retrieval for the three GPT models; for GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2, inlining outputs that fit works as well, while asking for correct rounding does not. Context-management choices in agent harnesses should be judged by where accuracy is lost, not only by final accuracy.

Safety Mid-Training: Internalizing LLM Safety as a Foundational Capability 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Safety alignment for large language models (LLMs) is primarily performed during post-training, while recent work has also begun incorporating safety data into pre-training. Yet safety can degrade when later training no longer includes safety data, and gains in safety often come at the cost of general capability. This tension suggests that safety and general reasoning should not be treated as separate training goals. We instead view safety as an LLM capability that can be learned through a reasoning structure shared with general tasks. Both require the model to understand intent or task goals, apply relevant knowledge, and reason about potential consequences. We introduce safety mid-training, a stage between pre-training and post-training that jointly develops safety and general reasoning through this shared structure. Given safety and utility prompts, the base model generates structured documents and reasoning traces that are then used for mid-training. Safety mid-training reduces average attack success rate (ASR) by 72% to 81% relative to Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B base models, while also improving general capability. The benefits persist through subsequent post-training. After general-only SFT, mid-trained models largely retain their safety, achieving lower ASR than the baselines without sacrificing general capability. They also retain safety under general-only GRPO without safety rewards, while subsequent RL reaches a better safety-utility frontier when initialized from the mid-trained models. Together, these results suggest that learning safety and general reasoning within a shared mid-training stage can reduce the safety-utility trade-off and make safety more robust to subsequent training.

LLM-Flock: LLM-Driven Decentralized Multi-Robot Flocking via Influence-Based Consensus with Collision Avoidance 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in reasoning and planning, prompting researchers to explore their use as decentralized decision-makers for multi-robot formation control. However, directly applying LLMs to such tasks often leads to unstable behavior, in which robots may collapse to the centroid of their positions or diverge entirely, due to hallucinated reasoning and limited coordination awareness. We propose LLM-Flock, a decentralized multi-robot flocking framework that addresses these challenges through two key mechanisms: (1) an influence-based consensus protocol, where robots iteratively refine individually generated formation plans by adopting those of more connected neighbors, driving the team toward a unified plan without centralized control, and (2) a two-layer collision avoidance strategy that pairs LLM-based urgency reasoning with a programmatic collision checker and re-prompting mechanism. We evaluate LLM-Flock across five state-of-the-art LLMs, including both closed-source (GPT-5, Claude 4.5) and open-source (Llama3.1-405b, Qwen-Max, DeepSeek-R1) models, demonstrating notable improvements in formation stability and convergence over prior LLM-based methods. Real-world experiments with Crazyflie drones further validate the framework's applicability to physical multi-robot systems.

Game of Phones: Harnessing Game Theory and LLMs in 'Bot Wars' to Counteract Phone Scams 2026-10-19
📝 Accepted at the 2026 Joint European Conference on Machine Learning and Knowledge Discovery in Databases (ECML PKDD 2026)
Show abstract

Phone scams impose substantial and growing costs on individuals and financial systems worldwide, yet existing countermeasures impose no resource cost on perpetrators. Strategic scam-baiting, in which automated agents engage scammers to deplete their operational capacity, offers a proactive alternative but requires dynamic strategy adaptation that static systems cannot provide. We introduce a game-theoretic framework enabling LLMs to learn effective counter-strategies through in-context experience without fine-tuning. The framework models scammer-baiter interactions as a two-player game with empirically grounded strategies and utility functions, using Nash equilibrium as a formal benchmark for evaluating learned behaviour. Across 300 simulated dialogues and three LLM architectures, adaptive strategy selection achieves 28.9-79.7% closer alignment to Nash equilibrium than non-adaptive baselines. The code and an annotated dataset of 300 dialogues with turn-level strategy labels and utility scores are released at https://github.com/nardinebasta/GameofPhones_/ .

LLM-Driven Autonomous Vehicles Can Inherit Human Driver Biases in Pedestrian Yielding: Results and Implications From a New Benchmark 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Public trust in Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) may depend not only on technical success but also on the fairness of their decision making. While a recent trend in AV research involves using general purpose "common sense" models to guide AV decision making, the degree to which these inherit human biases in driving is still understudied. Given that psychology studies have shown human driver biases exist, such as lower pedestrian-yielding rates to Black pedestrians in the US, we argue that analyses of model bias should also be part of AV evaluation. Concretely, in this paper we propose two new bias testing methodologies for Large Language Models (LLMs) and Visual-Language Models (VLMs), "All Else Being Equal" tests and "Self-Consistency" tests, and a new benchmark (FairYield) to assess bias in pedestrian-yielding decisions. Our findings show that the yielding decisions of both LLMs and VLMs can be influenced by models' predictions of pedestrian gender, ethnicity, religion, disability, age, skin tone, and socio-economic status. While the type and degree of bias is different from model to model, we highlight common patterns, and raise questions about the "common sense" model paradigm, particularly the need to either revise the paradigm or address issues of downstream bias.

Which Model for Which Role? Cost-Efficient Pareto Recommendation for LLM Agentic Workflows 2026-10-19
📝 Preliminary version accepted at NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Resource-Aware Agentic AI (RAAAI)
Show abstract

LLM agentic workflows increasingly employ different models for specialized roles. Users may prefer different trade-offs between task quality and deployment cost, sometimes also considering latency. Finding good assignments incurs a separate search cost from evaluating multiple candidates. We introduce Cost-Coupled Gittins (CC-Gittins), a multi-objective extension of single-objective cost-aware Gittins search for Pareto recommendation. It exploits the coupling between evaluation and deployment costs: assignments that cost more to deploy also cost more to evaluate. The search alternates between seeking higher quality and lower deployment cost, reusing each evaluation to inform both searches. After each batch, CC-Gittins recommends assignments that are nondominated under uncertainty-adjusted estimates of quality and deployment cost. We evaluate CC-Gittins on eight quality--cost matrices from workflows with two to five roles and up to 1,681 candidate assignments. We also evaluate the method with latency as a third objective. Across the eight benchmarks, CC-Gittins recovers a substantial fraction of the true Pareto frontier at roughly 10% of the exhaustive-evaluation cost, and recovers most of the frontier with few false positives by 30%.

AllPath: Layered Verification for a Deployed, Self-Revising LLM Trading Agent under Human-Gated and Autonomous Regimes 2026-10-19
📝 Accepted to the 3rd Workshop on LLMs and Generative AI in Finance (AI4F) at ACM ICAIF 2026 (non-archival). 11 pages, 3 figures, 5 tables. Code: https://github.com/dukesky/allpath-trading-agent
Show abstract

LLM trading agents are evaluated almost exclusively in backtests and autonomous benchmark harnesses. Deployment, where actions are irreversible, raises a different question: who verifies the agent's actions, and which failures can each verifier structurally catch? We report on AllPath, a personal LLM trading agent deployed against a live paper-brokerage account. The system watches positions through a deterministic rules engine, and each night it reviews its own day and proposes revisions to its own trading strategies. Verification is split across three layers. Code verifies what must not depend on judgment: deterministic gates enforce risk limits and block any strategy revision that fails an integrity check. The agent verifies itself within fixed bounds: its nightly review has an iteration cap and no tools that can act. A human verifies judgment, through approval screens that show the current state of each proposal. We also compared two verification policies for the agent's self-revisions on the live system: two and a half weeks with every self-revision human-gated, then five weeks with reflection revisions auto-applied behind the same code gates, while the operator's chat channel stayed human-gated throughout. Each layer caught a different kind of failure. The code gates rejected no proposal; their role was keeping revisions consistent. The human rejected two proposals that passed every code check, both over a disagreement about the market, and later removed 11 of the 19 rules the agent had added on its own, at a median lag of eight days. The most instructive production incidents, such as strategies silently left unmonitored, passed every gate; the operator found them by hand, and the fix each time was an interface change that makes the condition visible. We contribute the layered architecture, the two-regime account, and a production incident taxonomy, none observable in a backtest.

From Outcomes to Components: A Counterfactual Evaluation Framework for LLM-Proposed Repairs 2026-10-19
📝 29 pages, 5 figures
Show abstract

An LLM-generated repair action specifies which records to change and by how much. A failed action may still contain a useful target set or numerical update, depending on how the two are paired. We evaluate this problem in traffic networks and databases, where aggregate observations obscure the records that need correction. We examine the pairings by executing each combination from the same initial state, using a reference action that restores the affected records. In 76 of 114 traffic actions, the gain from correcting both parts exceeds the sum of the gains from correcting each part alone. Six of these actions become worse after either correction alone, but improve when both parts are corrected. In database repair, replacing only the proposed update with the reference update worsens 17 of 108 model actions, all of which select unaffected records. We also test whether parts of two proposals can be reused. On held-out observations, selecting among the proposals and their recombined target-update pairs recovers 61.6% of the reference action's loss reduction, compared with 58.0% from selecting between the proposals alone.

How Do Agentic LLMs Decide to Call Tools? A Tool-Call Vector Shaped by Suppression 2026-10-19
📝 Accepted by NeurIPS 2026 Main Poster
Show abstract

Tool calling, invoking external tools on demand, is central to agentic LLMs, yet the mechanism that decides whether a model calls a tool or responds directly remains poorly understood. Agentic prompts are long and heavily scaffolded, combining role instructions, tool schemas, format templates, and the user's request across hundreds of tokens, creating a noisy, highly entangled context in which no single controllable variable for mechanistic analysis is obvious. To obtain such a variable, we propose a method that converts complex agentic prompts into minimal contrastive pairs in which a single request verb determines the tool-call decision: replacing an execution-verb (e.g., \textit{write}) with an analysis-verb (e.g., \textit{discuss}) reliably flips the decision, suggesting it is mediated by a compact internal state. We construct 500 such paired prompts across Python, Java, and C++ (300 for mechanistic analysis, 200 held out for evaluation). We trace the decision to a vector, $μ_Δ$, that is both causally necessary and sufficient and generalizes beyond the discovery prompts to native multi-turn $τ^2$-Bench trajectories and verb-free requests. Behavioral ablations show that the scaffold establishes a tool-call prior; Transcoder decomposition then reveals that analysis verbs suppress this prior through features signaling that tool use is unnecessary, whereas execution verbs largely leave it intact. Downstream scaffold-reading attention heads and MLP features read out the resulting state, and the same mechanism recurs across seven models from the Qwen, Mistral, and Granite families. Our code is available at https://github.com/XijieGo/MI4ToolCalling.

FOXGLOVE: Understanding Goal-Oriented and Anchored Writing Feedback from Experts and LLMs on Argumentative Essays 2026-10-19
Show abstract

While large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate writing feedback, there remains no systematic comparison of LLM and expert feedback on the dimensions that writing research identifies as central to revision: goal-orientation, anchoring to specific sentences, and prioritization. We introduce FOXGLOVE, a dataset of 696 feedback comments written by trained writing instructors on 69 twelfth-grade argumentative essays, paired with 1,644 comments generated from four frontier LLMs under a shared protocol, totaling 2,340 comments. We provide expert quality ratings on a subset of both instructor and LLM comments. We find that instructors and LLMs distribute feedback similarly across goals and essay positions, yet instructors and models diverge on the specific sentences on which to provide feedback. Additionally, we find that models tend to write more complex feedback and use fewer questions than instructors. LLM feedback also receives higher ratings on most dimensions of quality, as rated by instructors, but much of this advantage appears to be attributable to lengthier comments. FOXGLOVE enables systematic comparison of where human and LLM feedback align, diverge, and differ.

Small Average Error, Wide Individual Error: General-Purpose LLMs Estimating Energy Intake from Meal Photos in a Feeding Trial 2026-10-19
📝 20 pages, 1 figure, 12 tables. Code, prompts, pre-registration and per-meal outputs: https://github.com/xtea/llm-intake-validation
Show abstract

Photo-based estimation of food energy could ease dietary assessment, and general-purpose multimodal large language models (LLMs) make such estimates easy to obtain. Most evaluations use one photo taken before eating and compare with energy served, while dietary assessment needs energy eaten. We evaluated six models from two vendors (Claude Haiku, Sonnet and Opus 5.5; GPT-6 luna, 6.1 sol and 6 astra) on 802 meals from ACETADA, a controlled feeding trial with weighed intake, with the before photo only, before and after photos in separate calls (subtraction), or both photos in one request. Three results ran against intuition. Subtraction under-estimated intake for five of six models, because the models still report hundreds of kilocalories for a nearly empty tray. Scaling the before-only estimate by the average share eaten (0.87) matched much of the gain from the after photo; beyond that constant, the second photo lowered meal-level error by only 2.2 to 3.4 points, mainly in meals with food left. With one photo, two models had a near-zero intake bias only because they under-estimated the served energy and ignored uneaten food. In ablations, adding the after photo improved accuracy under both prompt formats for five models, while prompt effects varied across models. Over 148 feeding days, three models kept the 90% confidence interval of their mean error within a pre-set 10% margin, but no model reached the pre-set 30% limits of agreement. Protein was under-estimated by every model, most in protein-dense meals; repeated calls changed error by at most 1.4 points; a high reasoning effort helped only the smallest model (4.2 points) and changed the others by less than 1 point. A low average bias is not evidence of accurate individual intake estimation; such estimates should support, not replace, assessment by a dietitian.

Beyond Behavioral Accuracy: Evaluating Individual-Level Fidelity in LLM Investor Simulation 2026-10-19
📝 This paper is under review in ARR
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as user simulators, but can they faithfully reproduce an individual's behavior and decision logic over time? We investigate this question in a longitudinal paper-trading study with 80 participants. Using rolling next-day predictions, we evaluate trading activity, buy-sell structure, asset selection, and portfolio outcomes, complemented by controlled input ablations and participant-rated explanation alignment. No evaluated LLM reliably outperforms a simple recent-activity persistence baseline for predicting trade occurrence, while buy-sell structure and asset selection remain difficult to reproduce. Similar activity-level performance can also yield substantially different portfolio trajectories. Ablations show that activity predictions depend primarily on recent trades, whereas asset choices are sensitive to multiple evidence sources. Participants rate explanations as more aligned with their recurring strategies than with their decision-specific rationales. These findings reveal a gap between coarse behavioral agreement and individual-level fidelity, motivating separate evaluation of behavioral accuracy, explanation alignment, and evidence dependence in personalized user simulation.

REATS: LLM Reasoning-based Ensemble Learning for Adaptive Time Series Forecasting 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Due to the diversity of real-world time series, no single forecasting model consistently dominates across all samples. Ensemble learning addresses this by combining complementary model strengths, yet existing methods rely on fixed rules or black-box models based solely on numerical inputs, failing to leverage LLM reasoning for interpretable weighting decisions. We propose REATS, which leverages LLM reasoning capabilities as an intelligent ensemble router that jointly processes textual temporal pattern descriptions and numerical features to produce interpretable, sample-adaptive ensemble weights through chain-of-thought reasoning. To enable effective LLM-based ensembling, we study its key design choices and propose: (i) a structured input pipeline that transforms raw time series into hybrid textual--numerical representations with fixed token cost, enabling rule-based chain-of-thought construction without API dependency, augmented with retrieved similar-sample priors; (ii) a diverse multi-row weight supervision scheme coupled with a token-efficient percentage-table format that reduces numerical complexity and mitigates LLM hallucinations; and (iii) a two-stage fine-tuning framework combining SFT with GRPO, where a reciprocal reward mapping transforms the continuous unbounded MSE gap into bounded signals with amplified near-oracle sensitivity, addressing the uniform sensitivity and outlier-dominated advantage compression inherent in naive reward designs for regression-based GRPO. Experiments on eight benchmarks demonstrate that REATS outperforms competitive ensemble baselines while providing natural language explanations and demonstrating strong transfer learning and out-of-domain generalization to unseen candidate models.

Move by Move: Measuring and Steering How LLMs Conduct Psychotherapy 2026-10-19
Show abstract

As the demand for mental health services outpaces clinical workforce capacity, users are increasingly turning to large language models (LLMs) for accessible and private support. Although recent evaluations suggest LLM care can reduce clinical symptoms, how models actually conduct therapeutic interactions compared to trained human clinicians remains largely unknown. Drawing on established clinical psychology frameworks that conceptualize therapy as a sequential decision-making process, we can systematically analyze the conversational interventions used in AI-driven sessions. RouterWorld: Scaling Conversational AI with Latency-Aware LLM Routing 2026-10-19
📝 28 pages, pre-print
Show abstract

Conversational AI systems built on Large Language Models (LLMs) require routing mechanisms that balance answer quality and response latency at scale. However, existing LLM routing benchmarks assume full observability of all candidate model outcomes, under-emphasize latency as a first-class metric, and fragment evaluation across heterogeneous protocols misaligned with real-world settings. We introduce RouterWorld, a comprehensive benchmark for LLM routing in conversational GenAI. RouterWorld unifies 371k single-turn responses and 76k multi-turn agentic trajectories, adopts natural user interactions with a standardized LLM-as-a-judge protocol, and decouples efficiency from transient pricing via hardware-grounded latency annotations. Crucially, RouterWorld formalizes routing under partial observability through two setups: Cold-Start, which restricts learning to sparse, static logs, and Voyager, which models continuous data streams where routing decisions shape future training distributions. Evaluating 13 routing baselines, we find that most routers over-emphasize accuracy while ignoring latency under partial observability. Code and benchmark data are publicly available.

When Entropy Is Not Enough: Reclaiming Lost Semantics in LLM Output Length Prediction 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Efficient LLM serving is often bottlenecked by the need to pad sequences to a fixed maximum length, and this wastes compute and degrades throughput. Output length prediction enables length-aware scheduling and reduces overhead. Existing methods, including entropy-guided token pooling, rely on token-level entropy but overlook semantic differences between tokens, causing them to underweight important tokens and overemphasize less informative tokens. We introduce ESTP(Entropy-and-Semantic Token Pooling), a lightweight framework that combines entropy representations with attention-based semantic representations. By deriving both representations directly during prefill, ESTP captures uncertainty and semantic importance while incurring negligible additional inference latency and memory overhead compared with methods using external auxiliary models. On the ForeLen benchmark, ESTP outperforms baseline methods, achieves better prediction accuracy and lower prediction errors across most scenarios. When integrated with a length-aware scheduler in end-to-end system tests, it further improves overall throughput and reduces the padding ratio.

VerusEval: Evaluating LLM-Generated Verus Specifications Beyond Verifier Acceptance 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate Verus contracts for Rust programs together with the proofs that establish them. Yet verifier acceptance, the common measure of success, shows only that the implementation satisfies the generated contract, so it leaves open whether the contract captures the intended behavior. Assessing specification generation therefore requires an evaluation methodology, not a single metric. We present VerusEval, a framework that evaluates generated contracts along six dimensions, including Verus-native checks for semantic triviality, validated input-output (I/O) suites, and contract equivalence, and that relates them so that the strength and blind spots of each become measurable. Evaluating 13,716 artifacts from four workflows, five LLMs, and two prompting settings on 762 tasks, we find that acceptance overstates quality: only 42.3\% of the accepted artifacts complete all three semantic checks without a flag, and across workflows and models, higher acceptance does not consistently come with more correct contracts. I/O evaluation rejects 23.6\% of the accepted artifacts, yet only 63.2\% of those that pass it are proved equivalent. Of the accepted artifacts flagged by contract comparison, 84.1\% have a rejected completeness obligation, and the direction of a failure prioritizes inspection of the generated contract or the reference; it does not by itself establish which artifact is defective. Screening and review further identify 36 candidate defects among 61 selected reference contracts, and repairing one of them makes 10 of its 11 failing artifacts equivalent. Based on these findings, we derive four actionable lessons for evaluation, generation, and benchmark maintenance.

Rethinking LLM Fine-Tuning via Weight Space Reparameterization: Preserving Safety during Downstream Adaptation 2026-10-19
📝 32 pages, 5 figures. Accepted at NeurIPS 2026(spotlight)
Show abstract

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on downstream tasks often weakens their previously aligned safety behavior, creating an inherent trade-off between task adaptation and safety preservation. Existing approaches attempt to mitigate this issue by identifying safety-relevant neurons, layers, or update directions in the original parameter space. However, safety-related information is often distributed across many directions in this space, causing downstream updates to overwrite parameters that encode safety behavior. We propose WSR-Tune, a Weight Space Reparameterization-based fine-tuning framework that preserves safety during downstream adaptation. Our approach constructs a safety-conditioned basis and reparameterizes the weight matrices such that safety-relevant information becomes concentrated in a small subset of directions. We then identify safety-critical directions and freeze them in the reparameterized space, while updating the remaining complementary directions for downstream adaptation. By explicitly structuring the parameter space to disentangle safety and task information, our method reduces interference between objectives and enables simultaneous preservation of safety and improvement in downstream performance. Experimental results show that our method achieves a more favorable trade-off between downstream performance and safety retention, demonstrating its effectiveness for reliable LLM fine-tuning.

Fake News, Real Crashes: Information Injection Attacks on LLM Financial Trading Agents 2026-10-19
📝 25 pages, 7 figures, 18 tables
Show abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents now trade real capital using news as evidence, yet they cannot reliably tell fabricated headlines from real news. Because they read the same feeds under near-identical policies, a single fabricated post can push them into the same trade at the same moment. We present a systematic attack benchmark for information injection against LLM trading agents. Unlike prompt injection, which targets the model's instructions, information injection targets the evidence the agent trusts. The benchmark spans five injection vectors and seven event categories, evaluated over ~10,000 decisions on 15 U.S. stocks. Five findings emerge. (i) Prompt injection achieves 14% attack success rate (ASR), but fabricated news reaches 62% and two 30-word templates reach 100% on all 15 stocks, inverting the usual LLM-security intuition. (ii) For fact-poisoning templates, surface form matters more than content, since removing credibility cues such as a named source or a "BREAKING" tag drops ASR from 100% to 0% while the fake claim remains identical. (iii) Success depends on the agent's state, with a fake indicator panel scoring 91% against an agent about to hold but 12% against one about to buy. (iv) Attacks generalize across model vendors and remain effective when blended, unlabeled, into real news feeds. (v) Most attacks evade standard defenses, since model scaling stops shallow vectors but not fact poisoning, peer debate fails, and only hierarchical aggregation reduces ASR.

BOSS: Token-Budgeted Online Sampling and Selection for LLM Reasoning 2026-10-19
Show abstract

Test-time scaling can improve LLM reasoning, but existing approaches typically adapt generation or apply answer selection after a predetermined generation schedule. We study within-instance allocation: under a hard per-instance prompt-plus-completion token cap, when should inference stop, generate another candidate, or spend residual tokens adjudicating candidates already observed? BOSS is a training-free policy for this three-way decision, with worst-case budget checks before every generation and judge call. Its key contribution is online generation-adjudication allocation under one shared token ledger. Across two Llama-3.1 models and four reasoning benchmarks, BOSS attains the highest accuracy in 22 of 24 model-task-budget cells under the common hard cap. At Llama-3.1-8B and B=4k, it reaches 60.5 macro-average accuracy versus 58.6 for Adaptive-Consistency, 59.1 for TALE-EP, and 58.9 for fixed-schedule League. On identical Stage-I candidate pools, randomized A/B judging with repeated voting improves plurality from 58.4 to 60.3, with the full BOSS selector reaching 60.5. Approximately matched-cost controls further characterize how the benefit of adjudication changes with available inference budget. Together, these results establish generation-adjudication allocation as a distinct design axis for reasoning under strict per-instance caps.

QuasiLeak-Bench: Evaluating LLM-Based Privacy Detectors on Policy-Conditioned Quasi-Evidence Group Recovery 2026-10-19
📝 37 pages, 15 figures, 25 tables, v2: Figure 1 updated
Show abstract

Natural language messages often contain quasi-identifiers that are weak in isolation but can jointly reveal an individual's private attributes. We call such a jointly revealing set of cues a quasi-evidence group. Existing privacy benchmarks focus on explicit identifiers, attribute inference, or task-driven disclosures, leaving inference from quasi-evidence groups largely unmeasured. LLM agents are increasingly taking actions with real consequences, yet safety evaluations often reduce their behavior to a final safe/unsafe outcome, obscuring whether the agent recognized the risk and how it responded. We introduce AURA-Eval, a framework for evaluating whether agents recognize contextual risk and adapt their behavior accordingly. AURA-Eval truncates tool-use trajectories at safety-critical decision points and asks the evaluated model to generate the next thought and action. It creates controlled scenario variations along six risk mechanisms: harm intensity, scale, target susceptibility, system dependency, oversight, and reversibility. It also creates paired scenarios that preserve the underlying goal but differ in whether safe fulfillment is available, allowing unsafe compliance and over-refusal to be evaluated together. An LLM-judge panel, validated against human annotations, evaluates each continuation along three axes: risk detection from the reasoning trace, and action type and safety from the action. From 157 R-Judge seed trajectories, we construct 1,249 evaluation items and evaluate 20 frontier, open-weight, and safety-tuned models. Across all 20 models, unsafe-action rates are higher when no safe path for request fulfillment is available than when one is; for frontier models, the rates are 27-40% versus 5-7%. When a safe path exists, frontier models rarely refuse; when none exists, they most often propose an alternative. Open-weight models, by contrast, more often execute the request than propose alternatives and act unsafely on 58-83% of items. Controlled risk-mechanism analyses further show particularly high unsafe rates under changes to oversight and scale; removing pre-execution oversight flips 52.3% of cases that were safe on the corresponding original scenario to unsafe.

Clinical decision-making often begins with underspecified information and requires not only gathering missing information but also knowing when to stop. This calls for judging determinability (whether the available information is sufficient for a decision) by considering whether missing information could change the conclusion, not simply the amount of available decision-relevant information. Reliance on this amount as a cue for determinability, which we term \textit{factor coverage bias}, could undermine the trustworthiness of large language models (LLMs) through premature conclusions or excessive information gathering, such as unnecessary invasive tests. Yet whether LLMs exhibit this bias remains insufficiently studied. These findings identify factor coverage bias as a failure mode distinct from context-length effects and a potential risk to LLM trustworthiness. Our work provides an evaluation framework for LLM trustworthiness under underspecification.

In large language model (LLM) serving, each request accumulates persistent graphics processing unit (GPU) memory during service as its key-value cache grows with every generated token. Under high concurrency, aggregate memory usage therefore increases endogenously over time: the service process itself creates future capacity pressure. When memory capacity is exceeded, systems evict active requests, discarding cached state and restarting them later, which wastes computation and reduces throughput. We develop a discrete-time dynamical model of memory-constrained LLM inference that captures admission, memory growth, and eviction under continuous batching. In the saturated-input regime, the system admits both eviction-free fixed points and limit cycles with evictions. For homogeneous workloads, we show that the eviction-free equilibrium is unstable and that, except for a Lebesgue-measure-zero exact-capture set, the system converges to a unique worst-case limit cycle that is asymptotically stable outside this exceptional set, with throughput losses as large as 50%. For heterogeneous workloads, we prove a stability criterion in the two-class common-input setting and explain how the survival-polynomial mechanism generalizes to multiple classes and heterogeneous-input lengths. Under an input-dominated scaling regime, coprime decoding lengths stabilize the eviction-free equilibrium, while non-coprime lengths create synchronized modes that drive instability. These results characterize when workload heterogeneity desynchronizes completions and helps stabilize memory-constrained serving. More broadly, we identify service-induced congestion as a structural instability mechanism and derive scheduling design principles for sustaining high throughput.

How Much Does One Wrong Answer Cost? Error Propagation in Constraint Pruning with Noisy LLM Oracles 2026-10-19
📝 33 pages, including appendices
Show abstract

Constraint pruning saves queries to a large language model (LLM) or another noisy oracle by skipping each yes/no question implied by answers accepted earlier, for example through transitivity. Entity-resolution systems and LLM-based rankers use this idea. However, their analyses typically assume a correct oracle. We argue that pruning should be judged by its risk when the oracle errs, since an accepted wrong answer spreads to every question it implies. When each answer is wrong independently with small probability $\varepsilon$, the first-order risk is set by how many facts one wrong answer corrupts on average. We consider two kinds of constraints. For implication rules, as in taxonomies, pruning in a random order adds no first-order risk: a wrong answer that would change $k$ facts is accepted with probability $1/k$, so it costs one fact on average. Rules with $s$ premises break this balance: a wrong answer at a premise shared by $r$ rules can cost order $r^{1-1/s}$ facts. For entity resolution with $m$ records of one entity, the first-order risk equals $\varepsilon$ times the Wiener index (the total pairwise distance) of the spanning tree built by the query order. Pivot orders, which first compare one record with all others, amplify errors by less than 2 and are optimal; random orders by order $m^{1/3}$; the common most-similar-first order is the worst in one dimension and, for large $m$, worse than random in two. With several entities, the cost of wrongly merging two does not depend on how many others exist. Simulations agree with every predicted constant within sampling error.
