EMA Policy Gradient: Taming Reinforcement Learning for LLMs with EMA Anchor and Top-k KL 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has enabled Large Language Models (LLMs) to acquire increasingly complex reasoning and agentic behaviors. In this work, we propose two simple techniques to improve policy gradient algorithms for LLMs. First, we replace the fixed anchor policy during RL with an Exponential Moving Average (EMA), similar to a target network in deep Q-learning. Second, we introduce Top-$k$ KL estimator, which allows for flexible interpolation between exact KL and sampled KL. We derive the stability conditions for using EMA anchor; moreover, we show that our Top-$k$ KL estimator yields both unbiased KL values and unbiased gradients at any $k$, while bringing the benefits of exact KL. When combined with GRPO, the two techniques (EMA-PG) lead to a significant performance boost. On math reasoning, it allows R1-distilled Qwen-1.5B to reach 53.9% on OlympiadBench compared to 50.8% by GRPO. On agentic RL domains, with Qwen-3B base, EMA-PG improves GRPO by an average of 35.6% across 7 datasets of Q&A with search engines, including 29.7% $\rightarrow$ 45.8% on HotpotQA, 27.4% $\rightarrow$ 40.5% on 2WikiMultiHopQA. Overall, we show that EMA-PG is a simple, principled, and powerful approach to scaling RL for LLMs. Code: https://github.com/LunjunZhang/ema-pg

SoK: Assessing the Evolution of LLM Capability in Software Vulnerability Research 2026-11-19
Show abstract

LLM-based agents are becoming increasingly capable at discovering, validating and patching vulnerabilities in real software, and vendors and researchers report that this is already changing how vulnerabilities are found and fixed. These claims, however, are scattered across papers, vendor reports and disclosures and are rarely comparable. We introduce a framework that rates these capabilities on an autonomy scale modeled on the levels of driving automation, and qualifies each claim by its correctness, its availability and the strength of its evidence. We use this framework to systematize the published evidence on LLM capability in vulnerability research. We find that agents discovered, validated, and patched vulnerabilities in real-world software without human intervention before the restricted frontier models of 2026, and that open-weight models subsequently attained comparable autonomy for discovery and validation. We then measure the consequences of evolving LLM security capabilities in a decade of public vulnerability records, advisories and exploitation data. In 2026 the number of disclosed vulnerabilities more than doubled, with an increasing share rated critical or high, and severe bugs that had survived a decade or more in widely used code were reported several times more often than before. Yet the time between the stages of a vulnerability's life, from report to fix and from disclosure to attack, has not shifted noticeably so far. We grade the mitigations proposed in the literature with an explicit rubric and draw guidance for each group of stakeholders from the best-supported ones.

Held While Waiting: What LLM Agent Sessions Reserve on Serverless and Agent Runtimes 2026-11-19
📝 12 pages, 3 figures
Show abstract

LLM agents spend nearly all their time waiting for the model. On Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, our agents waited a median of 2.7 seconds for each model reply, then computed for an estimated 5 to 40 milliseconds. AgentCore does not charge for CPU during the wait. But it reserves a separate environment, a virtual machine with its own memory, for every conversation, and keeps it reserved until the conversation is closed. So 1,000 conversations reserve 1,000 environments to do the work of 2 to 15 processor cores. A service runs out of environments long before it runs out of processing power. The shortage has two causes, and each has a fix. An environment stays reserved after its conversation ends: in the customer-service benchmark we ran, a conversation lasts 33 seconds on average, but its environment is held for 15 minutes more unless the client closes it. And an environment serves only one conversation, which is mostly waiting: in our simulation, 64 conversations sharing one environment need 59 times fewer environments, and 99 of every 100 replies are delayed by at most 40 milliseconds. The milliseconds are an estimate because they are hard to measure: a stubbed model, a timer around the model call, and steps run back to back each gave a plausible number that was too low. We show how to avoid all three. Of the two fixes, closing needs only the client. Sharing needs the platform, and of the agent runtimes we surveyed only one allows it, in part. They keep conversations apart because an agent runs tools, and sometimes code, chosen by a model. The open problem is to keep them apart without reserving an environment for each while it waits.

Training Vision Models for LLM-Driven Weight-Space Programming 2026-11-19
📝 Project page: https://viscom.uni-ulm.de/wsp
Show abstract

It has been demonstrated that, in certain settings, large language models can perform weight-space programming to directly modify the weights of neural networks. However, it is unknown how dedicated training of these networks can contribute to the successes of weight-space programming. We present a benchmark of LLM-driven weight-space programming on vision models, in which five LLMs edit convolutional networks and vision transformers on relabeling and error-correction tasks on Waterbirds, CIFAR-100, PACS and Entity-30. We report more than 2,600 such edit processes, whereby each edit is scored on held-out images for success, side effects and accuracy loss. HiSA: Sparsifying the Hidden Dimension of RoPE Attention in LLMs 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Attention in large language models (LLMs) scores every query against every key with a full dot product. We investigate whether this dot product must be computed completely for attention to be effective. We propose Hidden Sparse Attention (HiSA), which keeps the query and key projections intact but only computes a partial dot product, following a static, parameter-free rule set by the token positions: attention becomes sparse in the hidden (feature) dimension, an axis much less explored than token sparsity, rather than over tokens as in almost all sparse attention methods. Based on the structure of rotary position embeddings (RoPE), we decompose keys and queries into parts which can be omitted when computing attention. By training LLMs from scratch with HiSA, we find that this partial dot product does not hurt but improves performance compared with the complete product while computing only about a third of the query-key products. During inference HiSA improves decoding speed and shrinks memory footprint. We also extend HiSA to FlashAttention-3 kernels for training and inference. Finally, we motivate our approach by showing that dense attention learns to suppress the attention score parts that HiSA drops. Code is available at https://github.com/ambroise-b/hisa.

How Much Should an Agent Remember? Deployment Lessons from Self-Distilled Memory for LLM Agents 2026-11-19
📝 Accepted to IAAI 2027 (Oral)
Show abstract

LLM agents on long-horizon tasks fail in recurring, procedural ways. They mis-paginate an API, resolve the wrong entity, or answer when no answer was asked. We built a pipeline that lets an agent learn from its own history. It first mines short \emph{lessons} from the agent's own trajectories, one to three per episode. It then consolidates those lessons into a smaller set of \emph{guidelines}, where each guideline records how many lessons it merged. At inference the agent receives either all guidelines or a selected subset. The pipeline ships in the open-source altk-evolve library and is deployed in IBM's CUGA agent and watsonx Orchestrate platform. We report what we learned building, measuring, and productizing it. Our primary instrument is the AppWorld benchmark, across models spanning 40--92\% baseline task completion. The central finding is that the right amount of memory is a property of the model, not the memory. A strong model keeps improving as guidelines are added. DeepSeek-V3.2 gains +9.5 points of task completion and +16.1 points of scenario completion with its full 191-guideline set. A weak model gains less from the same full set than from a small selected subset. gpt-oss-120b starts at 39.9\% task completion. It reaches 51.2\% with all 199 of its guidelines but 55.4\% (mean of two runs) with a selected subset of about 29. Around that result we report what went wrong: a high-support guideline that backfired, provenance silently dropped during consolidation, a swallowed API error that failed every task while producing a plausible log, and a baseline confound that nearly produced a misleading comparison with a prior method. We distill these into recommendations for teams deploying agent memory.

Training LLMs to Follow Reasoning Instructions with Minimal Edits 2026-11-19
📝 Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Transitioning from Pre-training to Post-training
Show abstract

Large language models increasingly rely on extended reasoning traces, yet the training signals used to shape this behavior remain coarse. Supervised fine-tuning on full trajectories forces the model to imitate an entire demonstration, overconstraining its output distribution, while trajectory-level reinforcement learning provides only weak credit assignment, rewarding or penalizing whole completions without identifying where they succeed or fail. We propose edit-based training, a general framework for learning from a model's own trajectories via localized textual corrections. Given a sampled completion, an editor identifies the first point of failure under a specified criterion and proposes a minimal replacement span. The model is then supervised only on these edited spans. By design, this concentrates supervision where the model's reasoning first breaks down and aims to preserve its existing reasoning patterns elsewhere. The method applies both offline and online, with the latter continually adapting supervision to the model's evolving failure modes. We instantiate this approach on reasoning instruction following with Qwen3-32B on the ReasonIF benchmark. Compared to full-trajectory imitation and trajectory-level reinforcement learning baselines, edit-based training substantially improves compliance with the reasoning instruction while largely preserving the model's skills on untargeted tasks. These results suggest that localized correction is an effective complement to these approaches for shaping how LLMs reason.

Robust LLM Evaluation with Boundedly Bad Judges: A Constrained Maximum Likelihood Approach 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Evaluating language models at scale often involves reconciling costly, but reliable, labels (e.g., from human raters) with cheap, but noisy, proxy labels (e.g., from LLM-as-a-judge autoraters). This paper proposes constrained maximum likelihood estimation (CMLE), a likelihood-based approach to large language model (LLM) evaluation that explicitly models the error in the proxy labels via their true-positive and false-positive rates, subject to data-driven constraints. Functionally, these constraints act as an adaptive regularization that restricts the feasible judge error rates, particularly when the available number of reliable labels is limited. In contrast to asymptotically unbiased estimators (such as those based on prediction-powered inference), this formulation accepts a controlled bias under misspecified constraints in exchange for variance reduction relative to various baselines including unconstrained maximum likelihood estimation (UMLE); we characterize this bias--variance tradeoff analytically under point constraints. We identify the regimes in which CMLE outperforms UMLE and prediction-powered baselines in mean squared error. We further extend the framework to heterogeneous settings using a stratified variant involving stratum-specific constraints based on judge confidence. Empirically, on synthetic and real-world LLM evaluation tasks, CMLE achieves up to roughly 9\% lower mean squared error (MSE) than non-stratified baselines, and its stratified variant (SCMLE) achieves up to roughly 30\% lower MSE than the strongest baseline considered, with the largest gains in low-label regimes.

Static Analysis Guidance for Secure LLM Code Generation 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Static analyzers are cheap and widely deployed, which makes their reports a candidate feedback signal for secure LLM code generation. Existing approaches place the analyzer inside an iterative repair loop with intermediate steps, which is costly and makes it hard to isolate the report's effect on the model. We introduce two strategies that give the finding to the model during generation. When the analyzer raises an alert, generation is rolled back, and the finding is inserted either as a code comment (Comment) or in the user message (Steering). We compare them with two iterative approaches and a control without the analyzer, across three models and three Python benchmarks, and we measure security using each benchmark's oracle. Guidance during generation is cheap and can be effective, at 0.9 to 1.9 times the baseline's output tokens against up to 24.1 times for iterative approaches. With DeepSeek-V4.1-flash on RiskyPy, Steering raises the share of functional and secure programs from 52.5% to 64.4%, close to the best iterative approach (66.2%) while using less than a fifth of the tokens. A per-rule analysis shows that rules with a local fix benefit most, and replacing the XML parser is net-positive on two of the three models. The study also identifies when guidance should not be applied. Analyzers flag at most 5.6% of the functional but insecure programs on SecEvalPlus, leaving little guidance to act on. When a static analysis alert (warning or error) contradicts the task, models tend to follow the analyzer and break the program. The two Qwen2.5 models often fail when the fix requires restructuring the code. These results indicate that static-analysis guidance is worth applying with a capable model, on rules with a local fix, and after filtering alerts that conflict with the task specification.

Client Exits - A Way to Increase LLM Transparency on the Client Side 2026-11-19

Textual recommendations generated by large language models (LLMs) can be difficult to inspect. This paper introduces \emph{Client Exits}, a lightweight mechanism for enriching LLM responses while separating semantic intent from client-specific presentation. The model inserts predefined, machine-interpretable markers into its output, and the receiving client decides whether and how to realize the corresponding enrichments. Agent^2 RL-Bench: Can LLM Agents Engineer Agentic RL Post-Training? 2026-11-19
📝 NeurIPS 2026, 37 pages, 7 figures, 20 tables
Show abstract

We introduce Agent^2 RL-Bench, a compact diagnostic benchmark for evaluating agentic RL post-training, which tests whether LLM agents can autonomously design, implement, debug, and execute post-training pipelines that improve foundation models. RL post-training increasingly drives model alignment and specialization, yet existing benchmarks are largely static, rewarding supervised fine-tuning or script generation without assessing an agent's ability to close an interactive RL loop. Agent^2 RL-Bench provides a unified agent-facing interface: each run starts from an isolated workspace containing a base model, task data, instructions, and a grading API, and agents must iterate within a fixed budget by training models and submitting artifacts for evaluation. The benchmark spans six tasks across three levels, from static rule-based training to judge-based optimization and closed-loop online RL with trajectory collection. Two diagnostic skills, namely runtime recording and post-hoc summarization, enable structured analysis of agent behavior, facilitating smooth and effective iteration of the benchmark's evaluation framework. Overall, Agent2 RL-Bench shows that current agents can sometimes engineer online RL, but stable agent-driven RL post-training remains rare under fixed budgets. The Real Cost of Context: Predicting Compute Time and Energy Consumption in LLM Prefill and Decode Phases 2026-11-19
Show abstract

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into agentic architectures has substantially increased token consumption, raising concerns about their energy footprint. Yet, estimating the energy consumption of proprietary API-based models remains challenging, and existing approaches focus primarily on generated output tokens while overlooking the impact of the large input contexts characteristic of agentic workflows. To address this gap, we systematically profile compute time and energy consumption across a wide range of prefill (input) and decode (output) token lengths. Sightline: Calibrated Data-Flow Summaries for LLM-Integrated Applications 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Programs increasingly call large language models (LLMs), filling prompt placeholders with program values and consuming the output. Such a call crosses the boundary between natural language and programming language (NL/PL). To follow data across it, an analysis needs a data-flow summary of which placeholders the output uses, but no code describes what the model does. Analyses of these LLM-integrated applications therefore taint every output with every input, creating spurious flows, or ask a second model to label each call, which is costly. We prove, and mechanize in Lean 4, that a placeholder flows to the output of a transformer, to first order, only through the attention that reads it (no reading, no flow), and that a faint reading or sensitivity cannot certify that a placeholder does not flow. Unlike reading, the log-probability gradients and Fisher signals of existing methods vanish on confident outputs, and single-span removal fails on duplicated content. Our tool, Sightline, reads a recorded call in one forward pass, learns from labels which reads are uses, and calibrates the pruning to miss at most a chosen fraction $α$ of true flows, on average over call sites, when deployment is exchangeable with calibration. On 2,103 unseen repository families and four models, Sightline is the most accurate summary against the seven closest methods and a hidden-state probe. Calibrated at $α$ = 1%, it misses about 1% of true flows and prunes 43.9-65.6% of spurious flows, 1.6 to 2.6 times as many as the best baseline, and still 43.8-60.1% when calibrated on another collection. Through an open surrogate, it reads models treated as closed about as accurately as they read themselves, without their labels. In the information-flow control of an LLM agent, it removes about half of the labels from inputs that do not flow.

Context--Answer Metamodels: Forecasting LLM Activations Before the First Answer Token 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Under stochastic decoding, a single context can elicit many different answers from a language model. Characterizing this variation typically requires many expensive autoregressive rollouts, each revealing only one realization. We ask whether the activations produced while reading the context can directly forecast the model's rollout activations. We train context--answer metamodels that map a context activation sequence to point or distribution forecasts of mean-pooled answer activations. We find that (i) the four metamodel families we study forecast mean-pooled answer activations with high fidelity, and the distribution metamodels also capture rollout variability; (ii) performance improves as the training set grows to 500,000 prompts, and the metamodels generalize zero-shot to out-of-domain datasets; (iii) their forecasts retain behavior-predictive signal; and (iv) context activations from Qwen2.5 predict mean-pooled answer activations from Qwen3.5 and Gemma 4, suggesting that activation forecasts can transfer across models. Together, these results suggest that context activation traces can support scalable forecasts of behaviors in LLM answers before the first answer token is generated.

Same Judgement, Different Scores: Numerical Scoring Topology in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation 2026-11-19
📝 Accepted to Findings of EACL 2027

LLMs are increasingly used as automated evaluators to reduce the cost and time required for human annotation. AltRA-Test: An Alternative Annotator Test for LLM Judges Without Shared Annotators 2026-11-19
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judges and graders to replace human annotators, raising the question of when such replacement is statistically justified. To support such decisions, the Alternative Annotator Test (Alt-Test) was introduced for dense settings where all items are annotated by the same annotators. However, in many large-scale annotation settings, items are annotated by different subsets of annotators. We therefore propose the Alternative Annotator Test with Resampled Annotations (AltRA-Test), a statistical test that does not require that all items are annotated by the same annotators. AltRA-Test uses a bootstrap that resamples items, draws k human annotations for each item, and substitutes one of them with an LLM annotation. This produces paired all-human and mixed human-LLM panels. A replacement is supported when the inter-annotator agreement (IAA) of the mixed panel is statistically non-inferior to the IAA of the all-human panel, i.e., when the LLM preserves the signal that the human annotators share. We establish that AltRA-Test is a consistent test of asymptotic level alpha. We then evaluate it through simulations and real-world experiments in which we test 18 LLMs and 5 prompting techniques on 20 datasets across text, image, audio, and video. Across these evaluations, about 100 items often suffice for the test. In simulations, where we know whether the LLM is a better or a worse annotator than the humans, AltRA-Test makes more correct replacement decisions than existing baselines. In real-world data experiments, LLMs pass the test in about half of the setups, depending mainly on the task. Since replacement is not always justified, we encourage researchers to test it for their task.

Periodic and Harmonic Periodic RoPE for Length Extrapolation in LLMs 2026-11-19
📝 5 pages
Show abstract

The ability to process long contexts is crucial for large language models (LLMs) to perform long-horizon tasks. Rotary position embedding (RoPE) gives every token a new rotation angle, so a model eventually meets angles it never saw in training, i.e., position exhaustion. We propose Periodic RoPE (P-RoPE), a positional encoding mechanism that operates in conjunction with sliding window attention. It repeats the first sliding-window-size \(W\) angles of the RoPE table, so the model needs only \(W\) distinct angles. P-RoPE works but introduces a wrap error at the window boundary. We further propose Harmonic Periodic RoPE (HP-RoPE), which removes the error by choosing frequencies that complete whole turns in \(W\) tokens. We compare models that share the same architecture, data, and schedule and differ only in the window-layer position encoding: standard RoPE, P-RoPE, and HP-RoPE.
