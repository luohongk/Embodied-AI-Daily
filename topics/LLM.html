Right Reason, Wrong Advice: Evaluating Recourse in LLM Explanations of Denied Requests 2026-11-16
📝 21 pages
Show abstract

Language models increasingly write the explanations people receive when a rule system denies their request. If several rules deny the request independently, an explanation can name a true reason and still leave the person denied after they fix it. We call the missing property recourse validity: changing what the explanation cites, without changing what was asked for, can flip the decision. We compute it exactly, and a hitting-set argument predicts from the policy alone when a correct reason fails. In six of seven rule systems ported from access control and US benefit eligibility, a single correct reason almost never gives recourse when several reasons apply. Across 20 LLMs, checks of whether the cited reasons are correct, computed or asked of an LLM judge, are close to chance at spotting this failure, as the argument predicts. A strong judge asked whether acting on the explanation would work does spot it. Stronger and reasoning models do better but still fail on many denials with four or more independent reasons. Asking for every rule helps every open model with room to improve. Explanations of eligibility decisions should be built from the decision function and judged by whether acting on them works.

Evaluating Cultural Reasoning and Robustness in LLMs for Spanish Political Discourse 2026-11-16
📝 Accepted for publication in Latin American Conference on Informatics (CLEI 2026)
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to interpret, generate, and mediate public-facing language, which makes their reliability especially consequential in politically sensitive settings. Although sentiment analysis is a common task in this area, affective meaning in social media discourse is often conveyed through culturally situated and pragmatically indirect cues that are not well captured by label-centric evaluation alone. In this paper, we evaluate the robustness of LLMs for sentiment analysis of Spanish political discourse from social media, with emphasis on culturally situated affective interpretation. We introduce a diagnostic protocol that combines three independent human annotation views into Gold and Ambiguous subsets, and extends the corpus with a Synthetic subset built from sentiment-flipped counterfactuals selected through model-conditioned perplexity and LLM-as-a-judge criteria. Using this framework, we compare proprietary and open-weight models under zero-shot and few-shot prompting, focusing on predictive robustness across human agreement conditions and counterfactual shift. Our results show that annotation ambiguity and counterfactual perturbation reveal failure modes that conventional evaluation on the original corpus tends to obscure. GPT-4.1-mini remains the most stable among the evaluated models, whereas the two smaller open-weight models degrade more markedly under synthetic perturbations, and few-shot prompting provides only limited gains. Overall, the paper contributes a reproducible protocol for robustness-oriented evaluation in political sentiment analysis, an empirical characterization of model behavior under ambiguity and sentiment-flipped counterfactuals, and a publicly available Spanish social media dataset with three-way human sentiment annotations.

Self-EvolveRec: Self-Evolving Recommender Systems with LLM-based Directional Feedback 2026-11-16
📝 NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Traditional methods for automating recommender system design, such as Neural Architecture Search (NAS), are often constrained by a fixed search space defined by human priors, limiting innovation to pre-defined operators. While recent LLM-driven code evolution frameworks shift fixed search space target to open-ended program spaces, they primarily rely on scalar metrics (e.g., NDCG, Hit Ratio) that fail to provide qualitative insights into model failures or directional guidance for improvement. To address this, we propose Self-EvolveRec, a novel framework that establishes a directional feedback loop by integrating a User Simulator for qualitative critiques and a Model Diagnosis Tool for quantitative internal verification. Furthermore, we introduce a Diagnosis Tool - Model Co-Evolution strategy to ensure that evaluation criteria dynamically adapt as the recommendation architecture evolves. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Self-EvolveRec significantly outperforms state-of-the-art NAS and LLM-driven code evolution baselines in both recommendation performance and user satisfaction. Our code is available at https://github.com/Sein-Kim/self_evolverec.

Replicating and Extending Weight-Space Programming: Frontier LLMs Compile Programs into Weights and Read Input Layers 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Researchers at MIT recently reported that GPT-6 Astra, a frontier large language model (LLM), can write the weights of small neural networks directly in a single reply, and can read the raw weights of small networks to say what they were trained to do. In a three-day sprint, we replicated and extended these results. Our four findings: (1) Writing weights reproduces. Astra's networks for the game Breakout scored 514, 550 and 307 (versus MIT's 517), and scored just as well on two modified versions of Breakout. Claude Opus 5.5 can also write working networks, but often runs out of output tokens. (2) What is new is translating programs into weights. Earlier models could write a good policy as code a generation before they could write network weights, and the networks the models write behave like compiled programs: two models independently decompiled Astra's Breakout network into Python matching its actions in all tested states. On classifying handwritten digits, Astra's networks scored 50% on average, below a simple classifier (82%). (3) Reading weights is mostly reading the input layer. Astra and Opus 5.5 identify which game a network plays almost perfectly, but on three new games we designed and trained ourselves, Astra recovered where things are on the board, while mostly missing how the game works. (4) Auditing from weights alone is possible but shallow. Without executing code, Astra picked out a small language model poisoned during training in 10 of 10 runs, one poisoned by extra training afterwards in 7 of 10, and a malicious adapter in 23 of 32 cases, but never identified the trigger or malicious behaviour. Open-weight models lag behind. From this early study, we conclude that LLMs can read and write small networks, but that this currently amounts to compiling hand-designed programs and reading the layout of the input layer. It is a promising but early tool for interpretability.

Rubric-Based AES with LLM Ensembles: Scoring of Student Responses in Critical Online Reasoning Using Ensembles of LLMs 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Grading student responses is costly and time-consuming. Moreover, human annotators are prone to bias, fatigue, and rater drift, which further limits grading reliability. Various methods have been employed to develop automated essay scoring (AES) systems to reduce these costs and address bias-related issues. These methods range from early text-feature-based systems to current machine-learning and transformer-based models. Specifically, large language models (LLMs) offer the potential to streamline the development of AES systems. However, they are subject to well-known limitations, such as hallucinations and prompt sensitivity. This creates a need to explore the potential of a mixed-methods approach that leverages the synergies among individual LLMs through ensemble formation. We evaluate the potential of ensembles of LLMs to match human-rater agreement when grading student responses in economics and medical education across 103 criteria. We use 631 German-language responses and corresponding human ratings by 2 to 5 annotators from a previously conducted assessment. We experiment with 523 rubric-based prompt configurations across 15 open-weight LLMs and assess their consistency with human raters using Krippendorff's alpha for inter-annotator agreement (IAA) and the Alternative Annotator Test (alt-test). Our results show that replacing one human rater with the best-performing LLM--prompt configuration maintains or improves IAA for 91 of the 103 criteria and that LLMs pass the alt-test for 90 criteria. Our findings suggest that current state-of-the-art LLMs can support AES of German responses in economics and medical education, reducing human workload and enabling faster processing, while validation against human ratings remains necessary.

Do Embedding-Map Distances Guide LLM Ideation? Four Tests Against What Came Next 2026-11-16
📝 48 pages, 5 figures. Code, data pointers and pre-registrations: https://github.com/JaviMaligno/latent-gap-ideas
Show abstract

A popular ideation recipe treats an embedding map of existing work as a guide: pick points far from everything and decode them into ideas. I test four ways such distances could guide ideation against what appeared later. (1) Far random directions are not text: no later item reaches a raw cosine above about 0.10 with one (after centring or whitening, 22 of 24 cells stay out of reach), and an LLM decoder shown a target's neighbours, with score-only feedback, writes those neighbours in all six target sets. A neighbour-free search writes coherent text at median cosine 0.57 and 0.47 to interior gaps, beating the nearest existing item on 15 and 14 of 20, but only 0.07 and 0.06 to far points. (2) On k-means cells of a past-only map, the registered hole score mostly tracks cell size and position; three density-based gap scores forecast which cells grow fastest at or below chance (AUC 0.30 to 0.66) and add nothing to momentum in Steam and arXiv, with patents inconclusive; more isolated new Steam games are slightly better reviewed. (3) LLM continuations of arXiv papers stay closer to the source than papers that cite it, under three user prompts and against proposal rewrites (gpt-4o) and April-December 2023 papers (OLMo). Critique lengthens this step beyond content-free revision, replicated on 150 new papers. Revealing a real follow-up's title turns the ideas by 0.032 along a follow-up-specific direction beyond revision; reviewer, oracle and distance feedback add 0.13, 0.08 and 0.34 of that turn (upper bounds 0.46, 0.46, 0.70), and their turn is not separable from revision or their longer step. (4) For inspiration material graded by distance, no inverted U was detected, on an anticipation margin that tracks format more than content. Original tests were registered before running, follow-ups (thirteen requested in two AI-agent reviews) after the main results; all are dated only by local git commits.

A Crowd-Audited Atlas of Implicit Bias in LLM-Powered Agents 2026-11-16
📝 Preprint. 32 pages, 5 figures
Show abstract

Prior work has shown that large language models (LLMs) express implicit biases in high-publicity, one-off decision scenarios such as hiring and intelligence assessment. However, as people increasingly employ autonomous, LLM-powered AI agents that make decisions on their behalf in doing day-to-day tasks, it is of critical importance that we understand how implicit biases, based on sociodemographic assumptions, emerge across a broad milieu of workaday decision scenarios. We used the 17 domains of daily activity in the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) to generate an atlas of 506 decision scenarios in which LLMs --- endowed with personas representing different races, gender identities, and political ideologies --- expressed significant implicit bias. Next, in a crowdsourced audit, 500 participants made 8,010 binary choices about which implicitly biased decision scenarios most deserved further review. We found that participants prioritized decision scenarios that had broad relevance (e.g., about food choices), were high stakes (e.g., about health), and where all members of a simulated sociodemographic group made unanimous decisions (because it was a clear indication of stereotyping). They were less swayed by the magnitude of an observed disparity or the specific ATUS domain the decision scenario represented. When examining the rationale behind their prioritization choices, we found that participants often interpreted disparities as traits of demographic groups and judged whether those traits seemed plausible, often based on their own internalized stereotypes. We discuss implications for building personalized AI agents, digital twins, social simulations, and for using crowdsourced bias audits.

Cancellation Is Not Enough: What Retries Leave Behind in an Overloaded LLM Engine 2026-11-16
📝 Withdrawn pending internal content review and approval by the authors' institution
Show abstract

Clients and proxies in front of an LLM engine retry calls that time out. After a transient overload, these retries can keep the engine from recovering even when calls arrive below its capacity. Since the engine may keep computing for clients that have left, propagating cancellation looks like the natural remedy. In this paper, we test it on vLLM with overload experiments on three open models, and with scheduler traces of one that follow each client attempt to the execution it creates in the engine. We find that with retries on, cancellation alone did not restore service in any run, and only 12-38% of the calls that arrived after the overload were delivered. The traces suggest why: retries that vLLM ran typically started prefill less than half a second before they timed out. Executions that vLLM later aborted took 79-85% of the prompt tokens it computed, and vLLM's prompt-token counter missed a third to half of all computed prompt tokens. A retry budget restored service by shedding retries but left many of the overload's calls undelivered. Request coalescing delivered every call from the overload onward, some late, unless cancellation had already aborted the running execution. Overload controls should thus be judged by the executions that retries add and the calls that clients receive.

Style over Substance? Linguistic Bias in LLM-based Peer Review 2026-11-16
Show abstract

The number of paper submissions to major AI conferences has increased dramatically in recent years, substantially increasing the peer-review workload of researchers. At the same time, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to support peer review, both by individual reviewers and by conference organizations. While LLM capabilities have improved considerably, their evaluations may be influenced by linguistic characteristics that are unrelated to scientific content. In this work, we introduce a controlled experimental framework for studying such effects by systematically perturbing the linguistic style of research papers. Externalized CPDAG Summaries Improve LLM Causal Deduction 2026-11-16
📝 28 pages, 2 figures. Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Observational data rarely identify a single causal structure. Such structures are typically modeled as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) whose arrows point from direct causes to their effects, and observed correlations and conditional independencies usually leave a whole family of DAGs that fit the data equally well. A causal claim follows from the data only if it holds in every one of them. Classical causal discovery methods such as the Peter--Clark (PC) algorithm summarize this family as a completed partially directed acyclic graph (CPDAG): an edge is directed when every compatible graph orients it the same way, and undirected when the data leave its direction open. On the Corr2Cause benchmark, large language models (LLMs) that reason in free text often lose track of this family and decide from a few local edges. We propose \emph{Structured Thinking}, a two-turn method in which the model first summarizes the CPDAG in a constrained schema and then checks the claim against that summary. Without fine-tuning or an external solver, it raises Qwen3.5-27B from $77.5$ to $86.5$ F$_1$ (mean of three seeds) over a baseline that receives explicit PC-algorithm instructions, whereas a second turn of free-text analysis does worse than that baseline. The gain holds on test splits with renamed variables or paraphrased hypotheses and on other models. Given only the graph and the claim, the model's answer flips when the graph is replaced by a valid CPDAG under which the claim has the opposite truth value. Moreover, replacing the model's graphs with reference CPDAGs on a fixed subset shows that most remaining errors arise in building the graph rather than in reading it. Making causal structure explicit can help LLMs reason more reliably, provided the intermediate representation is itself trustworthy.

Mining Workflow Graphs for Black-Box Boundary Testing of Conversational LLM Agents 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Failures of conversational LLM agents can have real-world consequences, for example when an agent completes a transaction without the user's confirmation. An important class of such failures occurs at workflow boundaries, such as identity checks and confirmation steps, which a test can reach only after several turns of conversation. We present AgentEval, a black-box testing framework that discovers and tests these boundaries. AgentEval interacts with an agent to mine a conversational workflow graph, a model of its behavior whose nodes are the agent's activities and whose edges are the user actions observed between them. It generates tests at each node of this graph, replaying the conversational path to that node before applying a perturbation. AgentEval then runs each test, and a separate LLM judge decides whether it passes or fails using only the conversation turns. We benchmark AgentEval against a privileged, white-box auditor with access to the agent's underlying source code, which AgentEval never sees. On four $τ^3$-bench agents, the boundary tests of AgentEval cover 38.8-46.0 distinct boundaries per agent on average over five repetitions. In ablation studies, generating tests without targeting the graph's nodes lowers the distinct-boundary count by 2.0-7.0 in every agent and raises the duplicate rate in most agents.

Hidden Measurement Error in LLM Pipelines Distorts Annotation, Evaluation, and Benchmarking 2026-11-16
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 14 pages, 7 figures. SI Appendix: https://solomonmg.github.io/pdf/totalevalerror_si.pdf
Show abstract

LLM evaluations drive model deployment, safety standards, research conclusions, and future projections, for example, about AI's impact on the labor market. Yet the way we often estimate noise from autoraters (LLM-as-judge) ignores variability from factors like judge model choice and prompt phrasing, which means we're underestimating error in a way that worsens with more data. This unmeasured variance can affect results enough to reverse conclusions. And judge-pipelines that fail to average this variation are more vulnerable to "benchmark hacking". This paper introduces a framework we call "total evaluation error (TEE)," which decomposes LLM pipeline uncertainty and uses design-study projections to reduce total error. In our safety and ideology applications, naive standard errors are 53-70% smaller than the corrected SE under our TEE framework. Using Chatbot Arena data, we show naive 95% CI coverage drops as n grows, while TEE-corrected coverage holds at 95%, and TEE-guided pipelines restrict the potential range of benchmark gaming by roughly 43%. We show further that a small pilot allows for the estimation of proper CIs, with the added benefit of projecting which design changes most improve precision. Using those projections to allocate API calls cut MMLU estimation error roughly in half against the answer key at the same budget, and increased per-match agreement with human voters on Chatbot Arena by 5.5 percentage points.

The SIMPLE Law for Energy Cost of LLM Inference 2026-11-16
📝 Under review. 19 pages, 8 figures, 9 tables
Show abstract

Generative AI is billed per token, yet tokens are not equally expensive. Each token enters the KV-cache and raises the cost of those that follow, so energy grows non-linearly along a sequence whose tokens look identical. No analytical model states what a token costs without per-LLM fitting, and no law names the factors that set it. From closed-form expressions of LLM inference's computational and memory-access complexity, we derive the SIMPLE Inference Model to Predict LLMs Energy (SIMPLE), a four-parameter law that separates prefill from decode, and decode into KV-cache and next-token computation cost. Over traces of 13 dense LLMs from several families on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, it attains R2 = 0.969 and 11.97% MAPE, 3.1-3.5x below the SOTA, without per-LLM calibration. SIMPLE explains as well as predicts: prefill energy grows quadratically with hidden dimension, so larger LLMs suffer most from long inputs; and higher GQA KV grouping reduces the per-token energy linearly relative to standard MHA. Increasing the reasoning token budget pushes output length beyond the analytically derived energy minimum, driving super-linear energy growth; accuracy rises but energy rises faster, so Intelligence per Joule peaks at an intermediate budget and falls by up to 3.14x across a 4.0x range. Moving to agentic serving raises energy per request by 13.0x, as both prefill and KV-cache terms grow with input length; prefix caching removes most of the prefill cost (-93%), but not KV-cache cost, so even a cached agentic request costs 4.8x more.

OmniToM: Benchmarking Theory of Mind in LLMs via Explicit Belief Modeling 2026-11-16
📝 50 pages, 9 figures, 42 tables; includes appendix NeurIPS 2026 acceptance and camera-ready update
Show abstract

Theory of Mind (ToM), the ability to infer others' knowledge, intentions, and emotions, is commonly evaluated in large language models (LLMs) using end-point question answering, where performance is judged solely by the final answer to a social reasoning query. This paradigm obscures whether the model actually constructs the underlying mental-state representations required for robust reasoning, particularly in scenarios involving divergent, evolving, or mistaken beliefs. In order to address this research gap, we introduce OmniToM, a benchmark that directly evaluates these representations by requiring explicit modeling of belief structures for all relevant actors within a narrative. These structures are composed of belief propositions: minimal statements of what an actor takes to be true about the world or another actor's mental state, allowing knowledge, intentions, emotions, and false beliefs to be analyzed in a common format. Models are evaluated in two stages: Stage 1: Belief Extraction, which extracts from the story the beliefs relevant to its social dynamics, and Stage 2: Belief Labeling, which assigns each belief a seven-dimensional schema label covering recursive order, truth status, knowledge access, explicitness, content type, mental source, and context. Built from 895 stories from the existing ToMBench story corpus and augmented with 22,343 labeled belief propositions, OmniToM uses a human-calibrated LLM-assisted annotation pipeline. Across diverse models in zero-shot evaluation, OmniToM reveals an actor-specific belief-tracking bottleneck: current LLMs struggle with the knowledge-access and representational decisions required to transform narrative facts into actors' beliefs and shared mental states.

SIRI: Self-Internalizing Reinforcement Learning with Intrinsic Skills for LLM Agent Training 2026-11-16
📝 EMNLP 2026 Main Conference
Show abstract

Long-horizon LLM agents can benefit from reusable skills, yet existing skill-based methods often rely on external skill generators during training or persistent skill retrieval at inference, increasing engineering complexity, context length, and deployment latency. We propose Self-Internalizing Reinforcement learning with Intrinsic skills (SIRI), a three-phase framework that enables agents to discover, validate, and internalize skills without external skill generators or inference-time skill banks. SIRI first warms up the policy with GiGPO to acquire basic interaction ability and collect successful skill-free trajectories. Further analysis shows that our self-mining strategy can achieve performance comparable to distillation with closed-source large model.

Multi-LLM Agentic AI at the Edge: Joint Agent Scheduling and Resource Allocation 2026-11-16
Show abstract

In this paper, an edge-enabled multi-large language model (LLM) agentic AI architecture is proposed, where one supervisor AI agent and multiple worker AI agents cooperatively execute users' requests at the edge. The supervisor AI agent decouples the user request into a series of sub-tasks and dynamically dispatches them to LLM-equipped worker AI agents via two agentic workflows, i.e., parallel and serial workflows, with a two-level retry mechanism to enhance execution reliability. LLM-as-a-Tutor: Policy-Aware Prompt Adaptation for Non-Verifiable RL 2026-11-16
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) for non-verifiable instruction following increasingly relies on LLM judges with prompt-specific rubrics as reward signals. While recent methods adapt these rubrics to the evolving policy during training, the training prompts themselves remain static, drawn from fixed corpora. This static approach often results in a critical misalignment between prompt difficulty and policy capability, leaving the judge unable to recover a discriminative reward signal when prompts fail to elicit quality variance among rollouts. To address this misalignment, we introduce LLM-as-a-Tutor, a framework that extends the LLM's role from judge to tutor: a single model serves as an examiner that pairwise-compares policy rollouts to detect non-challenging prompts, and as a generator that appends atomic constraints to them. This append-only design preserves the original prompt while adding constraints to increase difficulty in step with the policy's capability, producing a self-calibrating training signal without external difficulty schedules. On three complex instruction-following benchmarks, our method outperforms both policy-unaware baselines and prior policy-adaptive methods that adapt rubrics or rewrite prompts, on average and on four of five metrics, suggesting prompt adaptation as an underexplored axis of policy-awareness in non-verifiable RL for constraint-heavy instruction following.
