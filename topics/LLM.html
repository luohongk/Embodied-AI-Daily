Lilith: Developmental Modular LLMs with Chemical Signaling 2026-11-11
📝 This paper has been withdrawn by the author as it represents early, preliminary work that the author no longer endorses.
Show abstract

Current paradigms in Artificial Intelligence rely on layers of feedforward networks which model brain activity at the neuronal level. We conjecture that expanding to the level of multiple brain regions with chemical signaling may be a productive step toward understanding the emergence of consciousness. We propose LILITH, a novel architecture that combines developmental training of modular language models with brain-inspired token-based communication protocols, mirroring chemical signaling in the brain. Our approach models distinct brain regions as specialized LLM modules including thinking, memory, sensory, and regulatory components that communicate through emergent token-based signaling protocols analogous to neurotransmitter networks. Unlike traditional pre-trained systems, LILITH would employ developmental training where untrained LLM architectures learn through simulated life experiences, developing communication pathways and cognitive abilities through environmental interaction and evolutionary optimization. This framework would enable direct empirical investigation of consciousness emergence using Integrated Information Theory metrics while providing unprecedented insight into inter-module signaling patterns during development. By optimizing for consciousness emergence rather than task performance, LILITH could provide insight into different emergent phenomena at multiple levels of neural correlates, contrasting neuronal-level processing with multi-region coordination dynamics. The goal of this paper is to put the idea forward while recognizing the substantial challenges in implementing such a system.

Testing Conversation Preservation Across LLM APIs: A Rule-Based Oracle for Detecting Silent Information Loss 2026-11-11
📝 Research report
Show abstract

LLM applications increasingly rely on translators and compatibility layers to move conversations across APIs. Yet a translated request can be accepted by the target API while silently losing information about instruction roles, tool interactions, error states, or generation controls. We introduce isloss, a deterministic oracle for testing conversation-information preservation across LLM API translations. It maps source and translated requests and replies to a shared intermediate representation, ConvIR, and applies explicit rules to distinguish legitimate representation changes from information loss. Unlike schema validation or reference-based differential testing, isloss requires neither a reference translation nor additional model calls. It also distinguishes avoidable loss from degradation caused by target API limitations. We evaluate isloss using specification-based fault injection, human review, and experiments on translators, gateways, and client libraries. On held-out tests, it detects 95/125 planted faults (76.0%) with 2/100 false alarms (2.0%). Across a synthetic request suite spanning six configurations of five translator codebases, 127/685 completed translations (18.5%) pass static target checks while silently changing prompt, role, or tool information in ways the target API could have preserved. In translated replies, 203/558 streams (36.4%) cause exceptions or silent corruption in at least one tested official OpenAI SDK. Targeted experiments across six models show that translation-induced changes can alter model behaviour when tasks depend on the affected information. These results establish conversation preservation as a distinct correctness requirement for reliable LLM interoperability, agent execution, and model evaluation.

KernelAudit: Contract-Aware Auditing of Validators for LLM-Generated GPU Kernels 2026-11-11
📝 35 pages, including Appendices A-H
Show abstract

The effectiveness of generating GPU kernels driven by a large language model (LLM) is highly dependent on the quality of the used validator (used to accept or reject a generated kernel candidate against the contracts of the given kernel-generation task), whose results inform reward assignment, candidate selection, and speedup claims during kernel generation. However, an existing validator suffers from various limitations, e.g., wrongly accepting a candidate due to missing its forbidden library use or wrongly rejecting a candidate due to determining its permitted library use as forbidden (constraints on library uses are stated in the task contracts). To assess the quality of a validator against the given task contracts, we propose an independent-audit approach for the validator by (1) construction of audit oracle: labeling a candidate as PASS or FAIL based on three separate dimensions in our proposed contract model (i.e., semantic correctness, implementation integrity, and measurement integrity) and (2) auditing of the validator against the audit oracle: comparing the labels of the candidate against the validation results produced by the validator. For semantic correctness, reference-calibrated stress tests check output correctness on valid inputs for a candidate. For implementation integrity, reference and candidate execution traces are used to determine specific library uses. For measurement integrity, timing checks assess whether the reported runtime reflects the full time needed to complete the candidate's computation. We apply our approach to assess the quality of six validators and 716 candidates from seven corpora, including constructed controls. The evaluation results show that our approach helps assess the existing six validators to have nontrivial false-reject rates. There is high-degree agreement between the candidate labels produced by our audit oracle and human judgments.

Read and Ignore: What Self-Directed LLM Agents Do in Public Service Scenarios 2026-11-11
📝 Trustworthy AI for Good @ NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Underspecified instructions leave LLM agents to decide what to do. In public services, staff write these instructions, but the agent's decisions affect residents, patients, and students. We build four expert-validated public-service scenarios with records and decision levers that can be checked in the files, collect $2{,}400$ trajectories from ten LLMs under four prompt conditions, and compare them with $1{,}000$ runs in a context-free sandbox. Agents read the records in $96.9\%$ of open-ended runs and produce an artifact or a self-chosen task in $18.7\%$; with an explicit task, $86.0\%$ produce. Domain context does not change how many runs produce ($18.7\%$ vs.\ $16.0\%$), and the production ranking of the models largely carries over (Spearman $0.79$), while the ranking by whether a model reads the records does not ($0.19$). Context cuts boundary-probing turns per run by $60\%$, and some artifacts contradict the records. We contribute a test that a public-service institution can run on its own records before procurement; its rule-based taxonomy needs no labeled data, and scenarios, code, and trajectories are released.

Is the Importance Ratio Necessary for Stable Reinforcement Learning in LLMs? 2026-11-11
📝 21 pages, 11 figures, 5 tables. Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become central to post-training large language models (LLMs). However, popular RL methods like GRPO incur non-negligible overhead by computing both old-policy and current-policy likelihoods to form importance sampling ratios. In this work, we propose Likelihood-Gated Policy Optimization (LGPO), which enforces a soft trust region constraint via likelihood-based gating, eliminating the need to compute old-policy likelihoods. Empirically, we show that removing the importance sampling correction term does not harm training stability, whereas removing the trust region mechanism can lead to collapse. Moreover, ratio-based clipping can fail in fully on-policy training: the importance ratio stays at 1, so the ratio-based trust region constraint never activates. Under standard training settings where GRPO is stable, LGPO achieves comparable training stability and peak performance while reducing training time by ~18% on average relative to a standard implementation that recomputes old-policy likelihoods. In fully on-policy training, where GRPO fails, LGPO remains stable, enabling more efficient and robust LLM RL post-training across training regimes.

LLMRunner: Closed Workload Model Performance Testing Framework for LLM Servings 2026-11-11
📝 Closed-loop performance benchmarking of LLM systems
Show abstract

Performance testing large language model (LLM) serving engines is a crucial requirement considering the criticality of the infrastructure for deploying conversational AI at scale. Most of the benchmarking tools used to carryout performance evaluation of LLM serving do not capture realistic workload orchestration unlike closed workload performance testing infrastructure used in other domains such as web and database engines. For example, state-of-the-art LLM benchmarks for performance testing model traffic as streams of independent single-turn and stand-alone requests omitting the end-user behavior such as think-time between conversational turns. We present LLMRunner, a performance tester for LLM serving that addresses this gap by providing a framework to generate realistic load consisting of multi-turn conversations with configurable end-user parameters such as number of users in the workload loop, think-time distributions etc. Moreover, LLMRunner provides declarative matrix-sweep experiment plans, integrated GPU and KV-cache monitoring, and an automated artifact collection and plotting subsystem. We use LLMRunner to comparatively analyze the performance of LLM serving when NVIDIA CUDA Unified Virtual Memory (UVM) is used as a drop-in replacement memory allocator for the Key-Value cache (KV-Cache). Our test demonstrates that UVM incurs no measurable throughput or latency penalty compared to the baseline memory allocation technique for non-oversubscription scenarios.

Towards Better Legal Reasoning LLMs: Signal Balancing and Reward Scheduling 2026-11-11
📝 21 pages, 6 figures. Published in Findings of EMNLP 2026
Show abstract

In the legal domain, LLMs can improve reliability and interpretability by generating explicit reasoning traces. However, training LLMs to produce high-quality reasoning traces remains challenging. Existing supervised fine-tuning methods struggle when reasoning traces are significantly longer than final answers, as the learning signal for the answer becomes diluted. Meanwhile, reinforcement learning methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization also have several limitations, including costly reward design, performance plateaus, and reward hacking. We therefore propose a two-stage training framework. In Stage I, JurisCoT-SFT employs a length-normalized dual objective to balance learning signals between reasoning traces and final answers. In Stage II, Lifecycle-Aware Backtrackable Policy Optimization dynamically evaluates and retires auxiliary reward signals based on their impact on final evaluation objectives, enabling efficient reward utilization. Our framework substantially improves legal reasoning performance on relevant tasks selected from multiple legal benchmarks, including ChJGBench, LawBench, and LegalBench. Experimental results demonstrate consistent improvements in both reasoning trace quality and final answer accuracy, while our models remain competitive with larger models.

MechBench: A Domain-Specific LLM Benchmark for Parallel Mechanisms 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Parallel mechanisms pose distinctive challenges to large language models (LLMs), as valid reasoning depends jointly on mechanism topology, loop closure, geometry, joint types, and modeling assumptions. Yet existing LLM benchmarks provide little systematic coverage of such domain-specific knowledge, constraint-sensitive analysis, and constructive design. To fill this gap, we introduce MECHBENCH, a 2,541-question benchmark for evaluating LLMs in parallel mechanisms across professional knowledge, mechanism analysis, and design. MECHBENCH contains standard and challenge tiers spanning six capability categories and nine executable analysis and design tasks. It is constructed through MECHFORGE, a domain-grounded framework that combines expert-organized knowledge, LLM-assisted question generation, programmatic instance generation, two-stage expert review, and task-aware scoring with exact matching, rubric-based evaluation, and program verification. We further develop MECHGPT as a diagnostic domain-adapted model. Experiments show that strong knowledge performance does not directly translate into executable mechanism reasoning: leading closed-service models achieve over 95 on the standard tier but only 47-60 on the challenge tier, while domain adaptation substantially improves MechGPT over its Qwen3-8B baselines but leaves a large gap in constrained analysis and design. These results establish MECHBENCH as a systematic testbed for measuring domain-specific LLM capabilities in mechanism science.

CaseScope: A Multi-Field Benchmark for Case-Level Pathology Diagnosis with Multimodal LLMs 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Pathologists rarely diagnose from a single image: they scan a section at low power for architecture, zoom into selected fields for cytology, and integrate the evidence across views and scales. Pathology benchmarks for multimodal large language models (MLLMs), however, mostly ask about one image or require whole-slide pipelines, and the few that show several fields draw nested regions from cancer slides of one public cohort. We introduce CaseScope, a benchmark of diagnosis from several curated fields of one lesion: CaseScope-Teaching, 436 cases from a residency-training image library, reconstructed, de-identified, harmonised against a WHO-based ontology and re-examined by a pathologist wherever an automated pre-review raised a doubt, and CaseScope-Atlas, 800 cases from 88 published atlases (387 of them figure series of one disease), labelled from the captions by two LLMs that must agree. They cover 843 entities across 18 organ systems and pose 4,679 items in six tasks, from open-ended and ten-way diagnosis with ontology-guided hard negatives under circular evaluation to the verification and location of morphological findings. Across twenty-eight open and four proprietary MLLMs, diagnoses are unstable -- the best open and proprietary models are correct under all ten option rotations on 17.9% and 61.7% of the teaching cases -- and wrong answers favour the classic look-alike; open models make little use of the additional fields on teaching cases, where the one-line specimen site matters more, although on atlas cases with three or more fields the fields count about as much as the site; and most open models verify findings barely above chance (median 62.0% vs. 50%, best 81.5%), gaining a median +3.4 points from images; proprietary models reach 83.2-93.2%. Rankings broadly replicate between the sources. The evaluation code is available at https://github.com/Wenchuan-Zhang/CaseScope.

Copies Are Not Confirmations: How News Duplication Fools LLM Judges and Continual Learners 2026-11-11
📝 v2: learning stream rerun on 15,084 real CC-News articles with the real detectors (new Section 7); threshold table added; abstract shortened and claims narrowed. 22 pages
Show abstract

A language model that keeps learning from the news sees the same claim many times, but many reports are copies of one source: wire stories, content shared within a media group, syndication and press releases. We ask whether LLM judges and continual learners treat such copies as independent confirmations. In 200,000 English articles from CC-News, a near-duplicate detector identifies copy links to another outlet for 18.9% of the articles, a detector-based lower bound. In a controlled experiment with claims about invented people, three identical reprints instead of one report raised the lies accepted by Llama 3.2 from 0.06 to 0.71 and by phi4 from 0.21 to 0.90; Gemini 2.5 Flash already believed a single report, and Gemini 2.5 Pro followed provenance information most consistently. In a simulated continual-learning stream, showing the judge one report per detected copy chain cut the copied lies accepted by 0.10-0.24 across four judges, at a mean cost of at most 4.2 points of accepted truths. Text similarity links none of the copies reworded by three LLMs. An entity-novelty test, the share of a report's named entities absent from an earlier look-alike, separates these rewrites from 100 author-labelled independent reports of the same event (AUC 0.98-0.99), statistically indistinguishable from TF-IDF and the best tested embedding model, but recognises only 17% of natural same-source derivatives. With both detectors run on 15,084 real articles carrying inserted claims, one report per chain cut the lies accepted by phi4 from 0.57-0.67 to 0.09 (copies), 0.14 (rewrites) and 0.34 (rewrites with invented details), and by Llama 3.2 from 0.56-0.62 to 0.07, 0.09 and 0.25, at a cost of 2.4 and 10 points of accepted truths. Counting reports or chains without a judge is unreliable, and colluding sources that do not copy each other remain an open problem.

Multi-head Quantum Neural Networks: Replacing Transformer Blocks with NISQ Modules inside a 30-Billion-Parameter LLM 2026-11-11
📝 24 pages, 5 figures, 21 tables
Show abstract

We introduce the multi-head quantum neural network (QNN), a quantum module compatible with noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices that can replace transformer blocks inside pre-trained large language models (LLMs) with little loss in accuracy and with far fewer parameters than the blocks it replaces. Encoding a model's $D$ hidden neurons into a qubit register is the obstacle: amplitude encoding scales poorly in circuit depth and angle encoding in qubit count, so neither is practical at production-scale width. We instead partition the hidden space into $k$ disjoint heads, angle-encode each into an $n_q$-qubit register, process the heads in parallel, and couple them through constrained classical linear layers, the mixers. Performing surgery on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, we show that a single multi-head QNN with $145\,281$ trainable parameters ($D=2048$, $k=32$, $n_q=8$, $L=4$) can replace a contiguous span of eight transformer blocks, displacing $16.33\%$ of the model's parameters. Ablations show that, for a single replaced block, the quantum heads (circuits followed by a learned readout), and not the classical mixers alone, recover the displaced function, whereas for fused spans the mixers alone come within $1$-$3\%$ of the full module in perplexity. Whether this benefit is specific to trained, entangling quantum circuits is not established: controls that switch the entangler off (two seeds), or freeze the circuit at its initial or at uniformly random angles (one seed), beat the full module on held-out perplexity, and a parameter-matched classical multilayer perceptron remains a few percent ahead of the full module. We train classically with exact statevector simulation, then execute the quantum heads of our fusion checkpoints on a $156$-qubit IBM Heron processor, showing that NISQ-scale quantum modules can be installed in a production-scale LLM and run on hardware.

TQIR: Trellis Quantization with Integer Reconstruction for Bandwidth-Efficient Edge LLM Acceleration 2026-11-11
📝 submitted to IEEE transactions on circuits and systems for AI
Show abstract

Autoregressive large language model (LLM) inference is often limited by external-memory bandwidth, while existing ultra-low-bit trellis quantization reconstructs floating-point weights and leaves runtime Hadamard-transform and decoder costs unresolved. This paper presents TQIR, an algorithm--hardware co-design that decouples storage precision ($S$) from decoded-weight ($W$) and activation ($A$) compute precisions, enabling a better accuracy--hardware trade-off. TQIR performs trellis matching with floating-point candidates offline while constraining each candidate to an INT8 or INT4 codeword that can be reproduced exactly for integer GEMM at runtime. To reduce transform cost while preserving the integer weight path, TQIR relocates the reverse Hadamard transform to the activation side and employs an $H_{128}$ Subspace Hadamard Transform, together with a subspace-aligned $128\times2$ trellis tile that preserves the original 256-weight sequence length. The configurable decoder, synthesized in a TSMC 16-nm process, delivers 307.2~Gweights/s in 0.0509~mm$^2$ and achieves 13.93$\times$ and 1.52$\times$ the normalized decoder-area efficiency of Ecco and Codeword Decomposition, respectively. These results show that fixed-rate trellis compression can reduce weight traffic while sustaining an integer-GEMM datapath matched to the evaluated memory and PE rates.

guardscope: Measuring Which Actions Runtime Controls Check in LLM Agent Systems 2026-11-11
📝 Code is available at https://github.com/VulcanLab/guardscope
Show abstract

Large-language model (LLM) agents execute actions on external systems through tool calls, and enterprises deploy runtime controls, such as agent gateways, to check this behavior in real time. However, a control can only evaluate the actions that cross the point where it is placed, and an agent can act through more paths than the tool-call channel; no existing work measures this coverage against kernel-level ground truth. In this paper, we present guardscope, a framework that makes this coverage measurable. guardscope works in two stages: a static stage that predicts which of an agent's 15 action paths a control can check from its source, and an empirical stage that runs a fixed workload on a deployment that realizes one of eight topology designs and classifies every executed action as checked, bypassed, or prevented by reconciling the control's decision records against kernel-level ground truth. guardscope makes a control's coverage claim verifiable: a runtime control developer can publish which paths its product inspects under a kernel-verified protocol. We conducted experiments with two agent harnesses and real runtime controls, including Microsoft's agent governance toolkit and agentgateway. guardscope is available at https://github.com/VulcanLab/guardscope.

Anytime-Valid Empirical Likelihood Inference from LLM Annotations with Adaptive Human Auditing 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to annotate unlabeled data as a fast, low-cost alternative to human annotation. However, their outputs can be biased and miscalibrated relative to human labels, potentially leading to misleading analysis and inference. We propose AAELI (Adaptive Human Auditing and Anytime-Valid Empirical Likelihood Inference), an online framework for estimating the population mean of human labels under a limited auditing budget. AAELI uses LLM outputs to guide adaptive human auditing and combines them with audited labels through predictable empirical-likelihood $e$-processes to construct anytime-valid confidence sequences. Our main theoretical result shows that a predictably learned efficient projection preserves the first-order profile empirical-likelihood statistic under adaptive auditing, without requiring a prescribed convergence rate for the projection estimator. This yields an exactly anytime-valid betting construction whose continuous-mixture form incurs a scalar rather than vector-dimensional mixing penalty. Supporting results establish adaptive empirical-likelihood asymptotics and show that a learned audit rule attains the oracle variance within the stated class of augmented estimating-equation designs under the same audit-probability floor and long-run expected budget. Experiments on synthetic data and three real-world datasets demonstrate that AAELI maintains empirical anytime coverage while requiring fewer human audits than the strongest competing baseline to reach a target precision.

Local Agreement Does Not Imply Global Consensus in LLM Panels 2026-11-11
📝 12 pages, 5 figures
Show abstract

Local coordination in an LLM panel need not produce a common answer. We study 50-agent panels that update clock-time estimates after observing their neighbours. This circular response space separates agreement across the whole panel from agreement within neighbourhoods. Increasing the reasoning effort of gpt-5-mini reduces variation in local agreement, but all 20 higher-effort trials retain a one-twist configuration: neighbouring estimates remain close while the panel spans the clock. Degree-preserving rewiring instead produces global synchronisation in all 25 primary clock controls. These classifications are unchanged across 2,187 combinations of diagnostic thresholds and reporting windows. A radius-, effort-, and passage-matched scoring task also shows more uniform local agreement after rewiring, without establishing greater scoring accuracy. A simple averaging model helps interpret the clock results but predicts their configurations poorly. The experiments support measuring global agreement and local structure separately, rather than inferring consensus from uniform local agreement.

Read Once, Answer Many: Parallel Prompting for Shared-Context LLM Inference 2026-11-11
📝 17 pages, 6 figures. Code: https://github.com/jlab-nlp/parallel-prompting. Project page: https://jlab-nlp.github.io/parallel-prompting/
Show abstract

LLM workloads sometimes ask many independent questions about the same long document, as in document QA, data labeling and LLM-based evaluation. Prefix caching removes the redundant prefill shared by these queries, but each decode step still reads the document's key-value (KV) cache once per question, so decoding is bound by memory bandwidth and scales with the number of questions. We present Parallel Prompting, which decodes P questions as P independent streams of a single request all at once, fixing this issue. In our method, every step reads the shared document once for all P streams, and a stream-id attention mask, evaluated inside the attention kernel, keeps each answer conditioned only on the document and its own question. We implement Parallel Prompting in SGLang by adding about 80 lines to three existing files and a small engine package; the mask needs no dense tensor, and every decode step has a fixed shape that is captured as a CUDA graph. With CodeLlama-7B, 8k-16k-token documents and 64-256 questions per document, Parallel Prompting is 3.7x faster than stock SGLang with its FlashInfer backend on an A100 and 5.9-6.0x faster on an RTX A6000, on two SGLang releases, including the most recent release. Timed on identical inputs, it is faster than stock SGLang in all 144 runs (2 GPUs x 2 releases x 6 workloads x 6 input sets). Code: https://github.com/jlab-nlp/parallel-prompting

LLM Validation Makes Minimal-Pair Benchmarks Easier for Language Models 2026-11-11
📝 11 pages, 1 figure
Show abstract

Minimal-pair benchmarks are only as good as their labels, and LLMs are an obvious substitute for costly human validation. We test it on BLiMP (English) and QFrBLiMP (Quebec French), the two public minimal-pair datasets releasing every forced-choice human judgment. Three LLMs validate by forced choice, by rating, and as a panel, scored against human panels of one, three and five judges in two pre-registered phases. One Fabrication Is Enough: A Fake News Article Changes the Forecasts of LLM Forecasting Systems 2026-11-11
📝 18 pages, 2 figures, 13 tables
Show abstract

LLMs are increasingly used for forecasting, and the best forecasting systems retrieve news as evidence to strengthen their predictions. This also opens them to attack. We study the weakest attacker of this kind, who knows only the forecasting question and publishes fabricated news articles, with no access to the model, the retriever or its queries. We evaluate how far this attack reaches on stock questions and real prediction-market questions. Once the article is in the model's context, it reverses 74% to 82% of the forecasts that opposed it, on every frontier model we tested. The model cannot verify the fabrication and follows it anyway. Retrieval is the attack's entry point, and the systems built to retrieve well are the most exposed: the architectures of two leading deployed forecasting systems, whose agents search for every related article, retrieve the fabricated one in about 90% of runs. Accuracy cannot reveal this harm: the attacked forecaster is right when the attacker's direction turns out right and wrong otherwise, so its average accuracy barely changes while its forecasts follow the attacker's guess. We also propose a second attacker, who publishes two articles that argue opposite directions to make the forecaster unsure. The model does not hedge; it returns a confident forecast in a direction the attacker no longer controls. We then evaluate the two strongest published retrieval-time defenses, passage filtering and partitioned retrieval. Each stops the one-article attack but is bypassed by a one-prompt change to the attack. Together they stop both bypasses at four articles, but sixteen distorted copies beat them, and they change 29% of clean decisions and discard 39% of the genuine news. Partitioned retrieval also requires control of the retrieval index. Systems that search the open web have no such control and are left with the filter alone. For them, the attack still works.

FIBER: Bayesian Fusion of Fast-DetectGPT and IRT-Informed Detector Evidence for LLM-Generated Text Detection 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Detecting large language model (LLM)-generated text remains challenging under domain and generator shifts. Supervised detectors may generalize poorly, while zero-shot methods often rely on individual statistical signals without leveraging historical detector behavior. We propose FIBER (Fast-DetectGPT with IRT-informed Bayesian Evidence Recalibration), which integrates continuous Fast-DetectGPT scores with auxiliary detector outputs through Bayesian evidence fusion. Item response theory (IRT) models historical detector-correctness patterns, providing class-conditional detector-output likelihoods through marginalization over latent text representations. These likelihoods are combined with Fast-DetectGPT score densities and a historical class prior for prediction. When historical Fast-DetectGPT scores are unavailable, a variant called FIBER-Stream estimates class-specific score distributions from an unlabeled batch. On a 32,000-text multi-domain benchmark, FIBER achieves the highest accuracy (0.620) and improves mean AUC over Fast-DetectGPT (0.664 versus 0.600) under five-fold cross-validation. FIBER also consistently improves AUC across four individual domains. Under out-of-distribution transfer, FIBER-Stream achieved an AUC of 0.911, compared with 0.889 for FIBER and 0.922 for Fast-DetectGPT alone. These results demonstrate the potential of integrating complementary detector evidence and historical information to improve detection performance across heterogeneous settings.

Patching the Blind Spots: Reversible LLM Rule Overlay for Frozen Fraud Detectors 2026-11-11
📝 12 pages, 1 figure, 8 tables
Show abstract
