Towards Continual Learning: Mitigating Forgetting With Subspace Tuning 2026-10-15
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) acquire new skills through fine-tuning, but it can destroy capabilities they already have: catastrophic forgetting remains a central obstacle to continual learning in LLMs. Recent works connect forgetting to how far, and along which directions, fine-tuning moves the weights. On-policy methods such as reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation avoid updating the principal singular directions of weight matrices and thus forget less. In contrast, SFT concentrates updates along these directions and is susceptible to catastrophic forgetting. In this work, we introduce Subspace Tuning (SST), which restricts each weight update to the bottom singular subspace, eliminating changes along the principal directions. SST is optimizer-agnostic and composes well with fine-tuning recipes including SFT and self-distillation. SST combines tight subspace protection, dense gradient estimation, and a simple refresh mechanism, enabling stronger learning than prior orthogonal fine-tuning methods. With these mechanisms, protecting as much as 99\% of the weight subspace substantially improves retention while preserving strong target-task performance. This behavior strengthens with model and data size and nearly eliminates forgetting in the large-model regime. SST also keeps the model close to its base distribution, inducing $2$--$4\times$ less KL drift than the baseline recipes. Overall, SST advances the learning--forgetting Pareto frontier, making it a practical default for fine-tuning pre-trained language models.

Learning Changes the World and Then What Was Learned has Changed: Training and Deploying Neural Networks in the Control Loop 2026-10-15
Show abstract

Neural networks offer critical adaptability for controlling complex systems where nominal modeling proves intractable. However, when offline-trained neural networks are deployed within closed-loop control systems, their actions fundamentally alter the state distribution upon which they were trained. This paper introduces a unified taxonomy to categorize these sequential failure modes: the initial Closed-Loop Identification Error, the deployment-induced Action Error, and the continuous Tracking Error. Through discrete-time sensitivity analysis, this work formalizes how offline-learned parameters locally guarantee a trajectory divergence upon deployment, degrading performance due to local loss geometry. To mitigate this distribution shift, we propose actionable design policies centered on time-scale separated online adaptation. These mechanisms are validated through ablation studies on a nonlinear Duffing oscillator, demonstrating that bounded continuous learning successfully mitigates trajectory divergence whereas static deployments fail, offering concrete guidelines for practitioners deploying machine learning components in safety-critical feedback systems.

Time to Fake It: Continual Machine Learning Intensifies Unfairness in Deepfake Detection 2026-10-15
Show abstract

Deepfake detectors safeguard against misinformation, rendering their failures as much a societal as a technical concern. As generators constantly evolve, detectors trained on static datasets miss newly arising threats, while progressive training interferes catastrophically. Yet, mitigating forgetfulness in terms of accuracy, the prevalent focus of continual learning (CL), is insufficient to reflect real-world impact. We therefore scrutinize the effects of CL methods with respect to evolving fairness. To this end, we analyze four dimensions of fairness across diverse demographics for detectors trained progressively on a chronological sequence of 11 real-world deepfake generation methods. Tracking fairness metrics over time, we expose non-trivial interplays between performance and fairness that depend on both the choice of CL algorithm and demographic intersections. We find accuracy degradation to typically exacerbate unfairness and, conversely, fairness improvements to remain scarce over time. Dissecting the CL method families (rehearsal, prompts, regularization), we observe that inequitable forgetting is not explained by underrepresentation alone, but likely entangled with representation learning. We thus issue a call to action for CL methods to take social outcomes into account when balancing the stability-plasticity tradeoff.

RSIgen: Reciprocal Self-Improvement for Agentic Visual Generation 2026-10-15
Show abstract

Agentic visual generation produces experience that can improve the native understanding (agent) and generation roles of a unified multimodal backbone, but task success alone does not determine which role should learn. Generator updates can also make the agent's action supervision stale. We introduce RSIgen, a task-centric reciprocal self-improvement framework linking learning allocation, supervision refresh, and recursive curriculum construction. Dynamic Responsibility Routing (DRR) uses matched interventions on evidence, inputs, and supported actions to select learning recipients and construct compatible training targets. After generator learning, RSIgen re-evaluates the relative utility of tested actions with branch inputs held fixed to refresh agent supervision; the retained roles then construct the next curriculum. Across BAGEL, Qwen-Image, and Wan-Image-7B, RSIgen improves AgentGen-Bench Full Overall9 by 3.2--3.3 points over a three-cycle SearchGen adaptation with action costs. Wan controls support allocation gains at matched role-level training exposure, refresh gains at a matched adaptation budget, and benefits from updated curriculum sources while recipients continue learning. In blind evaluation on 200 real-user requests, pooled tie-adjusted preference scores against the same baseline reach 70.0% for task completion and 64.9% for visual quality.

Reliable Knowledge Acquisition for Continual Learning Agents 2026-10-15
Show abstract

As agents powered by foundation models move into open-ended environments, continual learning from experience becomes essential. For agents built on models with fixed parameters, this learning requires reusing prior task experience in context to improve performance and efficiency on future tasks. Existing memory and experience-learning systems typically focus on either retained facts or procedures, and lack a mechanism for establishing the reliability of claims extracted from the ongoing task stream. Indeed, we find that approximately 12--33\% of entries produced by representative systems harm future task performance when reused. To address these limitations, we propose \textbf{RIKA} (Reliable Knowledge Acquisition), which combines hierarchical extraction of factual, conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive knowledge with Evidence-based Knowledge Admission, which uses source evidence and subsequent experience to govern which claims enter the task agent's context, without additional validation tasks. Across six task streams from Continual Learning Bench and four model backbones, RIKA achieves the highest aggregate score on every backbone.

Functional Subnetwork Localization for Efficient Continual Learning in Large Language Models 2026-10-15
Show abstract

Biological intelligence continually adapts to new information while retaining prior skills through localized plasticity. In contrast, continual learning in large language models (LLMs) remains vulnerable to catastrophic forgetting. A promising direction is to identify functionally specialized subnetworks within LLMs that enable targeted and resource-efficient adaptation. Using gradient-attribution profiling, we localize cognition-associated functional subnetworks exhibiting extremely low mutual overlap. Building on this finding, we propose Function-isolated Memory-Efficient Optimization (CiMEO), a continual learning framework that routes each training example to its corresponding functional subnetwork, restricting parameter updates and optimizer states to selected regions. Across 1.4 million sequential instructions, CiMEO achieves the highest average performance among the compared methods on five generative benchmarks spanning programming, examination-style tasks, and mathematical reasoning, while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Subspace-overlap and principal-angle analyses further indicate that localized updates help preserve pre-trained representations, highlighting functional subnetwork localization as a practical approach to efficient and stable continual learning in LLMs.

Memorilla: Latent Semantic Memory for LLMs 2026-10-15
📝 Website: https://memory.stanford.edu/
Show abstract

Memory enables large language models (LLMs) to use information beyond their parameters, such as past user conversations, long documents, or an agent's earlier actions. For a frozen LLM, access to this accumulated knowledge is limited to what a memory system places in its context window. However, gathering and combining relevant information needed for each query while keeping the model's context short remains challenging for existing systems. Here we introduce Memorilla, a memory system that gathers information from an entire document collection into a fixed number of query-dependent 'memory tokens' for a frozen LLM. Memorilla encodes documents (such as user chats, book chapters) once into reusable embeddings, then learns to retrieve and place query-specific information in the LLM's input embedding space. Using only 16 memory tokens, Memorilla achieves the best performance against baselines including Mem0 and MemoRAG on five of eight benchmarks spanning personalization, long-document and knowledge-intensive question answering. Notably, on FactKG, it reaches 84.33% accuracy versus 80.43% with full-context inference, while using 108x fewer tokens to represent the source documents. Furthermore, Memorilla can facilitate continual learning in agentic systems where the document count (the agent's past turns) increases throughout the trajectory. In a TextWorld environment, we show that Memorilla reduces the agent's tendency to wander between rooms it has already explored, and improves its average success rate from 18.6% with full-history in-context learning to 24.2%. Overall, Memorilla aids frozen LLMs in accumulating and using knowledge effectively across long-context interactions and takes a step towards building continually learning AI systems.

Ranking and Partitioning of Attention Heads for Sparse and Continual Reinforcement Learning Fine-tuning 2026-10-15
📝 26 pages, comments are welcome
Show abstract

We study sparse and continual reinforcement learning fine-tuning of language models through attention-head ranking and partitioning. For sparse learning, we rank attention heads by gradient norm to identify a small subset of heads that can be trained effectively. For continual learning, we partition heads across tasks to reduce cross-task interference and preserve previously learned capabilities. Theoretically, we relate gradient norm to training effectiveness, and further characterize why sparse, separated head updates can remain stable. Empirically, across math, instruction-following, and coding tasks, tuning higher-ranked heads consistently outperforms tuning lower-ranked ones while often matching denser baselines, and head partitioning improves retention while preserving adaptation to new tasks.

Compatibility Matters: A Framework for Token-Level Teacher Selection in On-Policy Distillation 2026-10-15
Show abstract

On-policy distillation (OPD) has recently been introduced as a promising paradigm for continual learning, where a student policy can be supervised at the token-level. Typically, OPD is performed using a single teacher per prompt rollout (or even per training run). However, this ignores a whole other axis of flexibility that is available due to the on-policy nature - token-level teacher selection during rollouts. Building on this insight, we theoretically explore what makes a teacher and a student compatible for OPD, and leverage our findings to derive several empirical heuristics for teacher selection. In particular, our framework captures several desiderata for OPD: compatible teacher-student reasoning patterns, teacher competence, and induced student diversity. Theoretically, we categorize several types of teacher routing schemes, and we prove that under different scenarios, different routing schemes are likely to be beneficial for optimization and student diversity. We back up our findings with extensive experimental results in domains such as math, coding, kernel generation, and visual reasoning, where we find upwards of 10 percentage points of improvement on pass@$k$ when compared to vanilla OPD and the teacher experts on certain tasks, indicating both capability improvement and diversity over high-quality responses.

Optimal Explicit and Implicit Regularization Strengths in High-Dimensional Continual Linear Regression 2026-10-14
Show abstract

We study continual linear regression in a high-dimensional regime, where a learner observes a sequence of tasks and updates its parameters sequentially. In this setting, we derive an explicit closed-form expression for the generalization loss of a model trained with gradient descent for a fixed number of steps per task, which holds for arbitrary teachers. We prove that the optimal fixed step size decays nearly inversely with the number of tasks, specifically as $\ln T / T$, for a single teacher and, more generally, i.i.d. teachers. Under this scaling, we show that the resulting generalization loss is asymptotically equivalent to that achieved by optimally tuned L2 (isotropic) regularization. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to generalized ridge regression with diagonal regularization matrices, obtaining a closed-form characterization of the generalization loss and identifying necessary and sufficient conditions under which the optimal regularizer is isotropic. Finally, we identify a regime where the optimal step size remains finite even as the number of tasks tends to infinity, thus revealing a non-trivial tradeoff between task variability and implicit regularization, in contrast to the i.i.d. teacher scaling. Our theoretical predictions are corroborated by experiments on both linear models and neural networks, providing practical guidance for the design of continual learning systems.

HERO: Preserving Structure and Semantics in Heterogeneous Continual Graph Learning 2026-10-14
📝 under review
Show abstract

Machine learning on heterogeneous graphs has advanced rapidly, driven by the diverse entities, relations, and semantics inherent in real-world data. However, most existing studies assume static graphs, whereas real-world graph data are often continuously updated. Continual learning in this setting is particularly challenging because historical knowledge is encoded not only in target-node representations, but also in their heterogeneous structural contexts and relation-dependent semantic responses. To this end, we introduce HERO, a heterogeneous continual graph learning framework that preserves historical knowledge at both structural and semantic levels while adapting to incoming tasks. We analyze experience replay as an approximation to the unavailable historical gradient and decompose its error into target-selection and context-reconstruction terms. This analysis shows that replaying target nodes alone is generally insufficient for relation-sensitive HGNNs, as removing typed neighborhood context can alter both representations and historical gradients. Motivated by this, HERO employs DiSCo to select representative target nodes and reconstruct compact heterogeneous contexts through relation-aware multi-type neighbor expansion. To further preserve knowledge that cannot be captured by replayed subgraphs alone, HERO aligns task-conditioned predictions and heterogeneous semantic responses through multi-level knowledge distillation. A lightweight look-ahead adaptation step is additionally used to improve plasticity on incoming tasks. Experiments on four datasets with three HGNN backbones show that HERO achieves the best or tied-best average performance among state-of-the-art baselines, while maintaining competitive forgetting. Our code is available at https://github.com/gqBond/HCGL.

Data-Free Continual Learning for World Action Model with Rectified Generative Replay 2026-10-14
Show abstract

World action models (WAMs) built on pretrained video generation models adapt quickly to new robotic tasks, but they forget previously learned tasks under sequential fine-tuning. Experience replay (ER) mitigates such forgetting by rehearsing data of previous tasks, yet this data is often unavailable in robotics, where a policy is deployed as weights only and its training data is not shared along with it. In this work, we ask whether a WAM can achieve data-free continual learning by regenerating its own past. Our key observation is that a WAM can indeed autoregressively regenerate complete rollouts of previously learned tasks and replay them to mitigate forgetting. However, replaying such raw rollouts still lags far behind replaying real data, which we attribute to two issues. First, the WAM is unable to recognize task completion and keeps generating purposeless motion after the task is done. Second, under open-loop generation, the generated actions gradually drift away from the generated observations. To address both issues, we present rectified generative replay (R$^2$-WAM), which truncates generated rollouts at task completion with an agentic evaluator and filters out rollouts whose actions are physically inconsistent with the observations. On the four LIBERO suites and five real-world tasks, R$^2$-WAM reduces forgetting by over 90% relative to sequential fine-tuning and nearly matches ER without accessing any data from previous tasks. Our analysis further reveals that forgetting in WAMs concentrates in inverse dynamics modeling: a forgotten WAM still produces visually plausible observations of previous tasks while its generated actions fail. Safe-FCL: Continual Risk Awareness and Fine-Grained Safety Aggregation for Federated Continual Learning of LLMs 2026-10-14
📝 16 pages, 4 figures, 11 tables
Show abstract

Federated fine-tuning enables large language models (LLMs) to learn collaboratively from distributed client data, but malicious client updates can compromise global model safety. As federated tasks evolve, changing malicious update patterns challenge the ability of existing defenses to maintain reliable risk detection over time. To address this challenge, we propose Safe-FCL, a framework integrating continual risk awareness with fine-grained safety aggregation for federated continual learning of LLMs. Specifically, the Continual Risk Awareness (CRA) mechanism learns emerging risk patterns across sequential tasks and constrains detector updates using a protective subspace derived from historical features, mitigating forgetting of previously learned malicious patterns while adapting to new risks. To translate risk awareness into aggregation-level safety control, the Fine-Grained Safety Aggregation (FGSA) mechanism combines client-level risk assessments from CRA with module-level deviations from historical update behavior to adaptively regulate aggregation contributions, suppressing potentially malicious updates while minimizing unnecessary restrictions on benign updates. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Safe-FCL outperforms current SOTA methods, improving global-model Rule and MD-Judge scores in federated continual learning by 0.41% and 0.64%, respectively, while increasing average risk recognition accuracy by 2.01%.

Constitutive parameterized deep energy method for solid mechanics problems with random material parameters 2026-10-14
Show abstract

In practical structural design and solid mechanics simulations, material properties inherently exhibit random variations within bounded intervals. However, evaluating mechanical responses under continuous material uncertainty remains a persistent challenge. Traditional numerical approaches, such as the Finite Element Method (FEM), incur prohibitive computational costs as they require repeated mesh discretization and equation solving for every parametric realization. Similarly, data-driven surrogate models depend heavily on massive, high-fidelity datasets, while standard physics-informed frameworks (e.g., the Deep Energy Method) strictly demand complete retraining from scratch whenever material parameters change. To bridge this critical gap, we propose the Constitutive Parameterized Deep Energy Method (CPDEM). In this purely physics-driven framework, the strain energy density functional is reformulated by encoding a latent representation of stochastic constitutive parameters. By embedding material parameters directly into the neural network alongside spatial coordinates, CPDEM transforms conventional spatial collocation points into parameter-aware material points. Trained in an unsupervised manner via expected energy minimization over the parameter domain, the pre-trained model continuously learns the solution manifold. Consequently, it enables zero-shot, real-time inference of displacement fields for unknown material parameters without requiring any dataset generation or model retraining. The proposed method is rigorously validated across diverse benchmarks, including linear elasticity, finite-strain hyperelasticity, and complex highly nonlinear contact mechanics. To the best of our knowledge, CPDEM represents the first purely physics-driven deep learning paradigm capable of simultaneously and efficiently handling continuous multi-parameter variations in solid mechanics.

Instruction-driven image editing has reached native 4K, yet how such editors are trained remains undisclosed, and pretraining one from scratch is beyond most teams. SPARS: A Stability-Plasticity-Aware Region Selection Framework for Continual Learning 2026-10-14
Show abstract

Continual learning aims to acquire new knowledge while retaining previously learned knowledge. However, updating shared parameters when learning new tasks often overwrites existing knowledge, causing catastrophic forgetting. Memory replay methods mitigate catastrophic forgetting but rely on previous samples, raising privacy, memory, and scalability concerns. On the other hand, parameter isolation-based methods preserve learned knowledge but may limit knowledge transfer, whereas regularization promotes adaptability but remains susceptible to interference between old and new tasks. To maintain the stability-plasticity trade-off, this paper proposes Stability-Plasticity-Aware Region Selection (SPARS), a replay-free hybrid framework combining global parameter isolation with localized parameter regularization. SPARS estimates parameter importance using either the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) or a novel Confidence Score (CS) estimator that leverages logit sensitivity and achieves comparable performance to FIM without requiring labelled data. Based on parameter importance, SPARS partitions parameters into three importance-aware regions namely, winning, neighboring, and non-important parameter regions. Each region receives a different adaptation strategy: selective freezing, soft regularization, or unrestricted learning based on its role in the stability-plasticity trade-off. To mitigate classifier bias in class-incremental learning, we employ Quadratic Discriminant Analysis to improve performance on previously learned classes .Experiments on CIFAR-10 and TinyImageNet demonstrate consistent improvement in average incremental accuracy over existing isolation- and regularization-based methods across task- and class-incremental learning. On TinyImageNet, SPARS achieves the best performance with 6% improvement for class-incremental learning while retaining over 60% of parameters for future tasks.

Learning, Fast and Slow: Towards LLMs That Adapt Continually 2026-10-14
📝 39 pages, 23 figures, including appendix; Blog post: https://gepa-ai.github.io/gepa/blog/2026/05/11/learning-fast-and-slow/
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are trained for downstream tasks by updating their parameters (e.g., via RL). However, updating model parameters forces them to absorb task-specific information, which can result in catastrophic forgetting and loss of plasticity. In contrast, in-context learning with fixed LLM parameters can cheaply and rapidly adapt to task-specific requirements (e.g., prompt optimization), but cannot by itself typically match the performance gains available through updating LLM parameters. Inspired by how human learning works at multiple time scales (e.g., System 1 vs 2), we propose a fast-slow learning framework for LLMs, instantiated with model parameters as "slow" weights and optimized context as "fast" weights. These fast "weights" can learn from textual feedback to absorb the task-specific information, while allowing slow weights to stay closer to the base model and persist general reasoning behaviors. Fast-Slow Training (FST) is up to 3x more sample-efficient than only slow learning (RL) across reasoning tasks, while consistently reaching a higher performance asymptote. Moreover, FST-trained models remain closer to the base LLM (up to 70% less KL divergence), resulting in less catastrophic forgetting than RL-training. This reduced drift also preserves plasticity: after training on one task, FST trained models adapt more effectively to a subsequent task. In continual learning scenarios, where task domains change on the fly, FST continues to acquire each new task while RL stalls.

CRAFT: Forgetting-Aware Intervention-Based Adaptation for Continual Learning 2026-10-14
📝 21 pages
Show abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can learn new tasks through fine-tuning, but learning tasks sequentially often leads to catastrophic forgetting of earlier ones. This paper proposes CRAFT, a continual learning framework that keeps model weights frozen and instead learns low-rank interventions on hidden representations. Similar tasks share an intervention to help knowledge transfer, with similarity judged by how each task alters the model's output distribution rather than by a learned gating network. Within a shared intervention, a Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty keeps each new task close to the intervention's existing behavior, which limits forgetting of earlier tasks, while a small, task-private intervention in the deepest layers preserves dedicated capacity that later tasks cannot overwrite. The learned interventions also make it possible to identify the task at test time, so task identity labels are not needed. Across multiple benchmarks and model scales, CRAFT improves overall performance and reduces forgetting compared with strong LoRA-based methods, while remaining robust to task ordering.

Task-Driven Subspace Decomposition for Knowledge Sharing and Isolation in LoRA-based Continual Learning 2026-10-14
📝 Accepted by ICML 2026
Show abstract

Continual Learning (CL) requires models to sequentially adapt to new tasks without forgetting old knowledge. Recently, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), a representative Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) method, has gained increasing attention in CL. Several LoRA-based CL methods reduce interference across tasks by separating their update spaces, typically building the new space from the estimated null space of past tasks. However, they (i) overlook task-shared directions, which suppresses knowledge transfer, and (ii) fail to capture truly effective task-specific directions since these ``null bases" of old tasks can remain nearly inactive for new task under correlated tasks. To address this, we study LoRA learning capability from a projection energy perspective, and propose Low-rank Decomposition and Adaptation (LoDA). It performs a task-driven decomposition to build general and truly task-specific LoRA subspaces by solving two energy-based objectives, decoupling directions for knowledge sharing and isolation. LoDA fixes LoRA down-projections on two subspaces and learns robust up-projections via a Gradient-Aligned Optimization (GAO) approach. After each task, before integrating the LoRA updates into the backbone, LoDA derives a closed-form recalibration for the general update, approximating a feature-level joint optimum along this task-shared direction. Experiments indicate that LoDA outperforms existing CL methods. Our code is available at https://github.com/HHHLF/LoDA_ICML2026.

Have we made mitigating catastrophic forgetting more complicated than it needs to be? Lifelong In-Context Learning with Transformers Requires Parametric Forms of Attention 2026-10-13
Show abstract

Lifelong continual learning remains an obstacle on the path to human-like intelligence. Modern transformers show sparks of intelligence with in-context learning. The quadratic nature of attention, however, prohibits transformers from performing this process on arbitrarily long sequences. In this work, we argue that extending in-context learning to lifelong settings is a practical solution for continual learning in AI agents. In particular, we argue that parametric forms of attention are needed to understand a lifetime of context with transformers on a fixed hardware budget. These attention mechanisms learn the relationship between keys and their associated values at test-time with parametric regression. Our generalization of parametric approaches (linear attention, state-space models, fast weight programmers, and test-time training layers) contrasts with nonparametric counterparts like softmax attention. Overcoming Catastrophic Forgetting: Continual Learning for Radar Waveform Classification 2026-10-13
📝 15 pages, 5 figures, 16 tables. Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Aerospace and Electronic Systems
Show abstract

Automated radar waveform recognition classifies the waveform and modulation type of a received signal. As new waveform variants appear over time, a deployed classifier must learn them without retraining from scratch. Catastrophic Forgetting occurs when learning new waveforms overwrites prior knowledge. Continual Learning (CL) methods tackle this challenge, but prior radar studies only evaluate a few methods on narrow benchmarks. Practitioners lack evidence on which method to deploy or which mechanisms are effective. This work presents the first large-scale benchmark of continual learning for radar waveform recognition. Eighteen CL methods are evaluated on a sequential ten-task problem, drawn from four public datasets spanning 54 waveforms, under task- and class-incremental (TIL, CIL) settings and single- and multi-epoch regimes. Methods that retain data from old tasks lead both TIL and CIL settings. Plain replay alone closes 77-88% of the gap between the lower and upper practical limits under TIL, but only 46-55% under CIL. The internal mechanisms of the leading methods are ablated and statistically tested to identify effective mechanisms. Each ablation is paired against its full method over n=6 training seeds, topped up to n=12 when inconclusive, and p-values are Holm-Bonferroni corrected within each method's family. Ablation results show that several mechanisms confer smaller but statistically significant gains. Combining these mechanisms improves the leading F1 score by 3.4-5.5% and 2.7-4.9% under TIL and CIL, respectively, and the combined models exceed every benchmarked method in both settings for all SNRs. These results give researchers of radar waveform classification systems a mechanism-level basis for design.

Stabilizing the Modality Gap: Null-Space Projected Continual Learning for Vision-Language Models 2026-10-13
Show abstract

The modality gap is intrinsic to multimodal alignment, where embeddings from different modalities remain partially separated in a shared representation space. In continual learning, CLIP-based vision-language models (VLMs) not only suffer catastrophic forgetting but also experience modality gap drift that disrupts the shared embedding geometry. Such drift degrades cross-modal alignment and generalization. We propose FPCD, an efficient framework combining Fast Null-space Projection (FNP) and Contrastive Distillation (CD). FNP accumulates compact Gram matrices of previous tasks' activations and projects gradients onto layer-adaptive approximate null spaces to reduce interference with learned representations. By replacing singular value decomposition with symmetric eigendecomposition, FNP accelerates projection-basis construction by a factor of 5-9.
