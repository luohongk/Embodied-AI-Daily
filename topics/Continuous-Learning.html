Continual-ARC: Measuring Skill Acquisition over a Working Lifetime 2026-11-10
📝 51 pages, 13 figures. Code and run records: https://github.com/unifyai/continual-arc
Show abstract

Agent harnesses have emerged as a promising mechanism for continual learning, alongside test-time parametric learning. However, the current benchmarks have several limitations: they generally focus on a single applied domain, serve each instance only once, and assume a single family of learner, so memory-based and weight-based systems are rarely compared on the same scale. We propose Continual-ARC, a lifelong stream built from the three ARC-AGI editions, whose tasks need no domain knowledge. The same tasks return after dormant gaps with fresh instances, while new tasks keep arriving. On each instance, the learner may pay for demonstrations, submit answers, or act inside the harness, with retries permitted on incorrect submissions, which are paid for. Each instance is scored based on the share of this feedback budget consumed. Since a learner may keep anything between instances, memory-based and weight-based systems are scored on the same scale. After the stream, the learner is frozen and tested on fresh instances of the tasks it met, which measures memorization, and on unseen tasks, which measures generalization. We run an agent harness with frozen weights, with and without persistence, on Qwen3.5-9B and GLM-5.3 Flash, comparing them with two prompting floors, a program-induction method and Qwen3.5-9B's weights trained to imitate GLM-5.3 Flash. Persistence in the harness generally does not improve memorization unless provided with explicit expert guidance. Most harness-based methods still learn less than a floor that simply caches each task's demonstrations, across all models we tested. No harness-based method gains on unseen tasks, whereas weight-based learning shows substantial gains. This opens the door to future weight-based continual learning that could teach models to drive harnesses in new domains.

Personalized Affective Perception via Continual Learning in Human-Agent Negotiation 2026-11-10
📝 17 pages, 11 figures. Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing
Show abstract

Self-interested intelligent agents that negotiate with humans should interpret nonverbal expressions and adapt their behavior accordingly. However, generic facial-affect models degrade across individuals and contexts, creating a generalization gap. We introduce an affect-adaptive negotiation strategy that incorporates Continual Learning with Imagination for Facial Expression Recognition (CLIFER) to personalize valence-arousal perception in real time. Differential feedback converts changes in valence-arousal direction and intensity into adjustments of the agent's target utility. An asynchronous architecture updates CLIFER in the background during live interaction. In a counterbalanced within-subject study in which 66 participants negotiated with a humanoid robot, the CL-enabled agent achieved significantly higher individual utility and elicited higher participant concession rates than the FaceChannel baseline. The CL condition yielded significantly higher mean arousal estimates, alongside a broader output range. Participant utility and Nash distance did not change significantly between conditions. These outcomes support the practical value of coupling continual affective personalization with differential feedback for real-time negotiation.

Unipolar Neuron Networks Trained by Recirculation for Reward-Based Continual Learning 2026-11-10
📝 37 pages, 11 figures, 8 tables
Show abstract

Unipolar neurons are the predominant type of nerve cell in the octopus and in most other invertebrates. A single neurite (a fiber extending from the cell body) branches into a tree that carries both input and output synapses. The branches send graded local outputs to nearby cells, and one site on the main neurite sends a global output to distant targets. This paper proposes a network of such neurons and a learning rule for it. Each neuron is modeled as a tree of passive segments, whose activations are computed exactly as the steady state of a linear system. The network learns from reward alone, with a rule called recirculation. After each forward pass, a second pass sends requested changes of the outputs back through the same connections. Each synapse then adjusts its weight using only quantities available at its own location. Because the system of each neuron is symmetric, this update closely follows the gradient of the error in the predicted reward. Two mechanisms limit forgetting when tasks are learned in sequence: a task code inhibits a different subset of branches in each task, while consolidation lowers the learning rate of synapses that changed strongly in the recent past. The model is evaluated on a sequence of MNIST and Iris tasks. The network maintains high accuracy with low forgetting, whereas gradient-based training of the same network forgets earlier tasks almost completely.

In open-world recognition, machine learning models not only make predictions on unknown structures ("generalized open-set"), but also continually learn and improve from those predictions over time. STABLE: A Continual Learning Optimizer with Adaptive Drift Control 2026-11-10
📝 Accepted at the 40th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2026)
Show abstract

Continual learning suffers from catastrophic forgetting, leading to the loss of past knowledge and unstable adaptation across task streams. Existing approaches typically address this issue through replay buffers, architectural changes, or additional regularization terms. We argue that forgetting is a consequence of unstable learning dynamics, which may benefit from an optimizer-level solution. The main challenge is that learning dynamics are time-dependent, as demonstrated by empirical phenomena such as the stability gap. First, we formally prove that, for distance-based classifiers, bounded parameter drift guarantees bounded margin erosion under a local score-stability condition. Based on this insight, we propose STABLE, a primal-dual optimizer with adaptive drift control. STABLE enforces a drift budget by increasing the dual variable when the budget is violated and decreasing it otherwise. This results in high stability without penalizing plasticity. Experiments on long-horizon unimodal streams and CLIP-based multimodal streams show that vanilla PEFT baselines trained with STABLE match or outperform specialized continual learning methods. In the multimodal setting, STABLE further preserves zero-shot transfer, matching or exceeding dedicated multimodal continual learning methods. Code is available at https://github.com/atlas-luiss/STABLE.

Anchor Behavior, Not Parameters: Rehearsal-Free Continual Learning with a Single Shared Prompt 2026-11-09
📝 Accepted at WACV 2027
Show abstract

Prompt-based continual learning adapts a frozen pre-trained vision transformer by tuning a small set of prompt tokens. Recently, one-prompt methods which share one single prompt across all tasks make this paradigm maximally parameter efficient. The open question is how a shared prompt can keep the knowledge of old classes while being updated for new ones. Existing methods protect old knowledge in parameter space, where they freeze task-specific prompts, expand new components, or sparsely gate which tokens receive gradients. However, this method sacrifices plasticity, yet the drift it still permits in shared tokens can corrupt the prompt's function on past classes. What must be preserved is the prompt's behavior. We propose TEA-Prompt, which couples (i) a token evaluation module that assigns each prompt token a continuous, input-conditional relevance score injected into the attention logits, softly specializing tokens across tasks without discarding any of them, and (ii) an attention-behavior anchoring loss that constrains each token's attention output around class-specific query regions using only compact Gaussian query statistics without stored old images, weighted by the token's past relevance. Anchoring the attention value allows the parameters to continue updating freely, as long as their interaction remains unchanged. TEA-Prompt achieves state-of-the-art accuracy with markedly lower forgetting on CIFAR-100, ImageNet-R, and CUB-200 with only 0.38M parameters. Code is publicy available at https://github.com/cuonga1cvp/TEA-Prompt.git

MLoRA:Continual Low-Rank Adaptation via Relative-Response Subspace Selection and Momentum Orthogonalization 2026-11-09
Show abstract

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) provides a parameter-efficient approach to continual learning, but its nominal rank can conceal a loss of effective adaptation capacity. We identify \emph{spectral plasticity collapse}: during sequential adaptation, update energy becomes concentrated in a small subset of singular modes, leaving much of the available low-rank space underutilized. This exposes a limitation of interference avoidance alone: protecting historical representations does not ensure that the remaining adaptation capacity is responsive to new tasks or effectively utilized. To address this problem, we propose \texttt{MLoRA}, which jointly controls capacity allocation and utilization. First, historical whitening identifies input directions with strong current-task response relative to accumulated historical response, yielding a task-adaptive basis that remains fixed during training. Second, approximate polar orthogonalization of momentum updates reduces spectral concentration within theselected space. An orthonormal basis connects these mechanisms by transferring the factor-update spectrum exactly tothe induced weight update. We establish a max--min characterization of exact subspace selection and derive cumulative spectral bounds under controlled cross-step anisotropy. Across five class-incremental benchmarks and eight incremental settings, \texttt{MuLoRA} achieves the highest mean accuracy in 15 of 16 reported metrics.

Autonomous Continual Learning and Knowledge Transfer via Cortex-Inspired Neural Assemblies 2026-11-09
📝 15 pages, 11 figures
Show abstract

Unlike the brain, artificial neural networks (ANNs) suffer from "catastrophic forgetting": complete overwriting of solutions when re-training on new functions ("concept shift") or on a disjoint domain of the current function ("domain shift"). ANNs require each batch of data to be roughly representative of the complete distribution, a typically expensive and impractical requirement. In this paper, we introduce Functional Task Networks (FTNs), a method of continual learning and knowledge transfer modeled after several properties of the mammalian neocortex. FTN is formulated as a distributed, stochastic mixture of experts in which a sparse, binary routing mask is computed at inference time using a small support sample of input-output pairs. Per-batch reconfiguration of the router during training, together with mask-overlap-filtered replay, shapes a basis of overlapping, partially trained subnetworks. Unrelated tasks route through mostly separate assemblies, related tasks overlap on shared components. On block-sequentially trained continual learning and knowledge transfer tasks FTN retains solutions nearly as well as disjoint-mask isolation and attains the lowest pre-update head-start loss among matched-architecture comparators. The learned mask-overlap geometry tracks the known concept-sharing structure where available. We show experimentally that under correlated concept shift, support-set mask reconfiguration is necessary even for retention. Under domain shift, the distributed bank with replay is sufficient to handle retention without the configurer. Forward transfer, or head-start, and backward transfer, are also contributed by support-set mask configuration.

NCCL-BOT: Closing the Loop on NCCL's Cost Model with Online Bandits 2026-11-08
📝 46 pages, 13 figures, 38 tables
Show abstract

NCCL picks an algorithm-protocol pair for every collective from an analytic cost model (AUTO) that never sees a measured latency. The model is a strong prior but a brittle final decision: priced on forced-arm runs, its choice is exact on most contexts and badly wrong in a few. We built NCCL-BOT, a tuner plugin that closes the loop: a per-context bandit, warm-started from the model, learns from measured latencies. We replay a forced-arm H100 sweep, tuned at 4-8 nodes and scored at 16-32, and price the model's choice on the same forced arms; that sweep has no stock-NCCL run. The bandit cuts the contexts where the model's choice loses more than 10% from 19 to 1 (p99 103% to 9.9%, mean 6.1% to 1.6%; p=0.047 over nine scenarios after Holm correction). The prior and continued learning both matter: cold-started bandits, and committing after one probe of each arm, lose more on the mean in every scenario. Supervised selectors fit once on the same data and tuned with the same budget match the mean, and their heavier tail is not significant (7 against 1 contexts above 10%, p=0.06); at seen scales they match or beat online selection. With hyperparameters frozen from H100, UCB1 cuts AUTO's p99 from 60% to 23% and from 56% to 15% on two GB200 fabrics, but its contexts above 10% go from 16 to 8 on one and from 10 to 13 on the other, and scenario-level tests do not separate the two. Live, on two latency-emulated node sets of one H100 system with one run per policy, the learners repair most of a gross AUTO error on one (selection loss 31% to 6-8% for deterministic Greedy and UCB1; average all-reduce bandwidth 1.18-1.24x stock) but average 0.94-0.99x stock on the other, and on both lose to stock below 1 MiB (on the first through plugin overhead); on H100 the cost model is exact there offline, so small sizes can be left to it. We do not show a net end-to-end win.

The Surprising Effectiveness of Deleting Weights in LLM Post-Training 2026-11-07
📝 NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

How little of a pretrained large language model (LLM) has to change for it to acquire new capabilities? We find that zeroing fewer than 0.05% of its weights, with no other modification, matches full fine-tuning (FFT) at scale across three LLM post-training regimes: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, supervised fine-tuning, and on-policy distillation. Our method, Deletion Tuning, learns a binary mask over the gate projection of transformer blocks. Two practical advantages follow: the trained mask is hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the corresponding FFT checkpoint, and Deletion Tuning forgets far less, which addresses a central challenge in continual learning. Under reinforcement learning, its prior-task accuracy stays near the base level while FFT, LoRA, and random sparse fine-tuning lose up to 5.4 points. To understand why masking alone suffices, we analyze the geometry of the learned weight changes under reinforcement learning using stable rank, spectrum drift, and principal-weight overlap. Compared with LoRA and random sparse fine-tuning, Deletion Tuning is closest to FFT across the three measures.

Audit Before You Merge: Provenance, Probing, and Continual LoRA Composition 2026-11-07

Show abstract

Post-hoc merging of public LoRA adapters is an attractive gradient-free path to continual adaptation, but reliable measurement of catastrophic forgetting in this setting depends on infrastructure that prior work has not addressed. We treat each adapter merge as a sequential task in a continual-learning setting, with the instruction-tuned base model's capabilities as Task 0, and study forgetting on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Public adapter hubs contain modules that fail basic provenance checks: silent base-version mismatches and near-zero delta norms whose inclusion inflates measured interference by ~10pp. We propose a two-check audit (base-model match, non-trivial delta norm) and a leakage-free probing protocol as preconditions for any forgetting study in this setting. Ergodic Risk Measures: Towards a Risk-Aware Foundation for Continual Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-06
📝 In Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) 2026
Show abstract

Continual reinforcement learning (continual RL) seeks to formalize the notions of lifelong learning and endless adaptation in RL. In particular, the aim of continual RL is to develop RL agents that can maintain a careful balance between retaining useful information and adapting to new situations. To date, continual RL has been explored almost exclusively through the lens of risk-neutral decision-making, in which the agent aims to optimize the expected long-run performance. In this work, we present the first formal theoretical treatment of continual RL through the lens of risk-aware decision-making, in which the behaviour of the agent is directed towards optimizing a measure of long-run performance beyond the mean. In particular, we show that the classical theory of risk measures, widely used as a theoretical foundation in non-continual risk-aware RL, is, in its current form, incompatible with continual learning. Then, building on this insight, we extend risk measure theory into the continual setting by introducing a new class of ergodic risk measures, and showing that it is compatible with continual learning. Finally, we provide a case study of continual risk-aware learning, along with empirical results, which show the intuitive appeal of ergodic risk measures in continual settings.

Against All Likelihood 2026-11-06
Show abstract

Maximum likelihood is the dominant training principle for large language models, yet likelihood is neither necessary nor sufficient for truth. Unlikely events happen every day, and frequently repeated statements can be false. Drawing on a philosophical tradition that ties objectivity to invariance rather than probability (from Nozick's account of objective facts and Wigner's principles of invariance to the structural realism of Poincaré and Cassirer), we argue that the truth of a statement should be judged not by its probability under a single distribution, but by its invariance across relevant transformations of the environment. Semantic Concept Steering Breaks the Explanation Drift Loop in Continual Learning 2026-11-06
📝 NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) manifests not only as accuracy degradation but also as \emph{explanation drift}: models maintain predictive accuracy while silently shifting attention from diagnostic features to spurious correlations. This work identifies and resolves a previously overlooked structural flaw in explanation-aware CL: pixel-level saliency regularization derives supervision targets from the model's own frozen saliency maps, creating a \emph{self-referential drift loop} that actively reinforces spurious attention across tasks. We introduce \textbf{C$^3$L} (Concept-Consistent Continual Learning), which breaks this loop by anchoring regularization to class-consistent semantic concepts (e.g., ``curved beak'', ``striped pattern'') discovered offline, independent of model state, providing (i) target independence from model drift, and (ii) cross-instance semantic consistency across every instance of a class. We identify that our concept-based objective, combined with approximate LRP relevance conservation, induces a \emph{crowding-out effect}: since concept and background regions compete for the relevance of the same prediction, enforcing high relevance within concept regions implicitly suppresses spurious background correlations without explicit penalization. \method does not require human-annotated concept sets, as it employs a fully automated concept discovery and spatial grounding. Extensive evaluation across five benchmarks and over 20 baselines demonstrates state-of-the-art performance in accuracy, forgetting, explanation quality, spurious correlation mitigation, and out-of-distribution robustness.

Copies Are Not Confirmations: How News Duplication Fools LLM Judges and Continual Learners 2026-11-06

Many news reports of a claim are copies of one source: wire stories, content shared within a media group, syndicated features and press releases. We ask whether LLM judges and continual learners treat such copies as independent confirmations. From Trajectories to Prefixes: Reusing Teacher Trajectories via Replayed Prefixes and Online Continuation 2026-11-06

Show abstract

Small language models are attractive backbones for interactive agents, but direct distillation from strong teacher trajectories often turns rich multi-turn behavior into one-shot imitation targets. This is inefficient in long-horizon environments, where early decisions shape later states and rewards. We propose Prefix-GRPO, a reinforcement learning framework that decomposes teacher trajectories into replay-aligned prefix queries and online continuations. Each prefix is replayed in the environment to recover a valid intermediate state, after which the student continues online interaction and receives task reward. Unlike response-only GRPO, Prefix-GRPO also applies clipped policy updates to historical assistant tokens inside the replayed prefix, using a policy-distilled SFT checkpoint to estimate their old log-probabilities. This unifies prefix learning and continuation learning within the same policy-optimization form. Experiments on TextCraft, BabyAI, and ALFWorld show that Prefix-GRPO improves small-model agents over distillation and standard RL baselines, while ablations show that replay alone is insufficient without explicit prefix-token optimization. A Theoretical Bridge Between Long-Tailed Recognition and Continual Learning 2026-11-06
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

We theoretically and empirically establish a previously unexplored connection between Long-Tailed Recognition (LTR) and Continual Learning (CL). Specifically, we show that training on a long-tailed dataset with imbalance factor $\mathrm{IF}$ drives model parameters into an $O(1/\sqrt{\mathrm{IF}})$ neighborhood of the dominant-class solution, under both uniform and exponentially decaying cardinality distributions. Building on this result, we establish an objective-level reformulation of LTR as a sequential learning problem and prove that the CL objective upper-bounds the balanced LTR loss up to a constant determined by the residual Head loss. Motivated by this connection, we introduce Continual Learning for Long-Tailed Recognition (CLTR), a principled framework that leverages standard off-the-shelf CL methods to sequentially learn Head and Tail classes while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Extensive experiments on CIFAR100-LT, CIFAR10-LT, ImageNet-LT, and Caltech256 validate our theoretical predictions and demonstrate strong performance across diverse LTR benchmarks. In the foundation-model regime, CLTR with DualPrompt on a CLIP backbone outperforms standard adaptation baselines and is competitive with specialized methods using external semantic supervision. Our work bridges LTR and CL both theoretically and empirically, providing a principled approach for addressing long-tailed learning using standard CL strategies.

What to Preserve, Where to Adapt: A Depth-Wise Analysis of Forgetting in Continual Gynecological Image Segmentation 2026-11-05
Show abstract

The clinical management of gynecological diseases often relies on medical imaging for diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up. Segmentation in this setting is challenging because successive tasks may differ in imaging modality, target anatomy, pathology, and annotation structure. Continual learning allows models to adapt to new tasks without simultaneous access to previous datasets. However, when successive tasks differ substantially, learning a new task can degrade performance on earlier ones, a problem known as catastrophic forgetting. Understanding where adaptation disrupts previous knowledge can help guide the design of more targeted continual-learning strategies. We investigate how forgetting changes as different parts of an encoder--decoder network are allowed to adapt. We progressively expand the trainable region of a 3D nnU-Net backbone from the bottleneck toward input- and output-proximal blocks. Under a shared learning rate, adaptation near the bottleneck largely preserves previous-task performance but provides limited current-task learning, whereas broader adaptation improves current-task performance but sharply increases forgetting. This trade-off persists even when the average change in trainable backbone parameters is approximately comparable. Assigning different learning rates to different blocks substantially reduces forgetting when part of the backbone is trainable, although this changes both the size and location of the updates. Forgetting still increases as more blocks are trained and remains severe when the full backbone is updated. These results show that forgetting depends not only on how much the model changes, but also on which parts of the model are allowed to change.

RL Forgets! Towards Continual Policy Optimization 2026-11-05
Show abstract

Continual post-training is becoming a central paradigm for adapting vision-language models to evolving tasks. Recent studies report that reinforcement learning is less prone to forgetting than supervised fine-tuning, motivating the view that RL is inherently resistant to forgetting. However, this view remains insufficiently validated, as existing evidence is largely drawn from outdated or homogeneous benchmarks. We revisit this assumption by introducing MRCL, a Multimodal Reasoning Continual Learning benchmark built from recent and diverse multimodal reasoning tasks. Experiments on MRCL show that standard reinforcement learning still suffers from catastrophic forgetting during continual post-training. We trace the failure to an objective mismatch. The KL regularization used in common policy optimization methods is evaluated on current-task data, whereas forgetting is caused by behavioral drift on prior-task distributions. To address this problem, we propose Continual Policy Optimization (CPO), a replay-free method grounded in a prior-task behavioral KL objective. CPO derives a local Fisher surrogate from the historical KL objective and uses parameter movement as a gradient-free proxy for Fisher sensitivity, enabling sparse regularization with negligible additional overhead. Experiments on three model scales and comparisons with multiple RL baselines show that CPO consistently reduces forgetting while maintaining effective adaptation and preserving broader pretrained capabilities. The implementation code is available at https://github.com/MaolinLuo/CPO.

LatentGym: A Testbed For Cross-Task Experiential Learning With Controllable Latent Structure 2026-11-04
📝 61 pages
Show abstract

We envision continually learning agentic systems that become more useful over time: as they encounter sequences of related tasks, they should infer the hidden structure shared across those tasks and use it to improve future decisions. This cross-task experiential learning capability is pivotal in domains such as personalization and interactive assistance, but existing training/evaluation frameworks do not provide shared, controllable latent structures and cannot measure whether or why agents improve. We introduce LatentGym: a controllable suite in which each environment is organized around a ground-truth latent variable governing the structure across tasks. Together, these results establish a controlled foundation for studying how LLM agents learn from experience across tasks, and for designing agents that adapt more reliably in sequential, personalized, and interactive settings.

Federated continual learning trains a model on a sequence of tasks across several clients without sharing their data while retaining knowledge of earlier tasks. Sparse mixture-of-experts models support this setting by adapting selected parameters on a frozen backbone. However, clients learn different expert content as tasks evolve, making it difficult to share knowledge across clients without disrupting task-specific and client-specific specialization. Matching tile coordinates alone does not establish whether their learned content is compatible. We present FedSEAM, a federated continual learning framework that organizes task knowledge into sparse experts and selectively shares their content across tasks and clients. On each client, task-aware tile selection allocates capacity to a new task, while a subspace-based compatibility test determines which existing expert tiles to reuse and where to introduce fresh capacity. On the server, Subspace Expert Agreement Merging (SEAM) compares client updates as subspaces and moves each client towards consensus only along agreed-upon directions, leaving the rest of its update unchanged. We identify two remaining sources of forgetting, routing drift and merge erasure, and address them with three protections, one of which exactly preserves tile components in a selected prior-overlap subspace at every merge. We evaluate FedSEAM on TRACE using Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.2-3B, with component-wise ablations and studies of data size to assess its balance between learning new tasks and retaining prior knowledge.
