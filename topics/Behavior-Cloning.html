Inner Product Regularization for Imitation Learning 2026-11-12
Show abstract

Robots that learn from demonstrations should extrapolate their policy despite state perturbations such as sensor noise, scene variation, and compounding errors. One approach to this robustness is regularization. Prior regularizers consider each state on its own and limit how sensitive the policy is to any perturbation at that state. By contrast, we explore how the policy's output shifts when given the same perturbation at different states. Our insight is that, locally, the robot should respond to state perturbations in a consistent manner: e.g., if changing an irrelevant object position does not impact the policy at one state, that same change should not shift the policy output at a nearby state. Applying this insight, we develop inner product regularization (InneR). We show that the inner product on action space induces an inner product on state space through the policy Jacobian (the Fisher information for stochastic policies), and that the null space of this inner product governs which state perturbations have a first-order effect on the policy. By clustering states whose inner products are aligned, averaging the inner products within each cluster, and minimizing the smallest eigenvalues of that average, InneR shapes the learned policy so that nearby states share a common null space. InneR enforces consistent responses to perturbations without limiting policy expressiveness, and can be appended to existing imitation learning pipelines for deterministic or stochastic policies. In simulated experiments, InneR makes learned policies more robust to irrelevant state dimensions and shifts between training and test distributions, whereas norm penalties often perform worse than behavior cloning. On a real robot arm performing three manipulation tasks, InneR raises the average success rate of a diffusion policy from 18% to 57% without additional data.

CADET: Construction of PCB Routing Instances with Executable Demonstrations 2026-11-12
📝 28 pages, 10 figures, 15 tables. Code and data: https://github.com/LGAI-Research/CADET
Show abstract

Printed circuit board (PCB) routing is the stage of physical layout that finds paths connecting the placed pads of each net. Public datasets in this domain are few, and most contain only the final states of routed boards, not demonstrations of the electronic design automation (EDA) engine actions that produce them. To close this gap, we propose CADET (Constructing executAble Demonstrations for Engine-grounded Training), which jointly generates synthetic boards and demonstrations built from EDA engine actions. The resulting RouteDemos provides 100,000 synthetic boards routed with zero design rule violations (DRVs), together with their demonstrations. Their distributions over 17 statistics resemble those of 142 real reference boards. CADET trains a routing policy on these demonstrations with behavior cloning and fine-tunes it with reinforcement learning (RL). On real test boards, the policy raised the rate of complete DRV-free routing from 38.0% to 67.2% over an RL-only baseline. This rate was comparable to that of the open-source autorouter FreeRouting and exceeded those of the other baselines, including GPT-6 Astra. We release the dataset and its generation code as open source at https://github.com/LGAI-Research/CADET.

Diffusion Guidance Is a Controllable Policy Improvement Operator 2026-11-11
Show abstract

At the core of reinforcement learning is the idea of learning beyond the performance in the data. However, scaling such systems has proven notoriously tricky. In contrast, techniques from generative modeling have proven remarkably scalable and are simple to train. In this work, we combine these strengths, by deriving a direct relation between policy improvement and guidance of diffusion models. The resulting framework, CFGRL, is trained with the simplicity of supervised learning, yet can further improve on the policies in the data. On offline RL tasks, we observe a reliable trend -- increased guidance weighting leads to increased performance. Of particular importance, CFGRL can operate without explicitly learning a value function, allowing us to generalize simple supervised methods (e.g., goal-conditioned behavioral cloning) to further prioritize optimality, gaining performance for "free" across the board.

Distill to Think, Foresee to Act: Cognitive-Physical Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Driving 2026-11-10

Show abstract

Current end-to-end autonomous driving models are fundamentally constrained by the behavioral cloning ceiling of imitation learning. While reinforcement learning offers a path to smarter autonomy, it demands two missing pieces of infrastructure: (1) a cognitive foundation that understands traffic semantics and driving intent, and (2) a foresighted physical environment that can anticipate the consequences of candidate actions. To this end, we propose CoPhy, a CognitivePhysical reinforcement learning framework for autonomous driving. To distill to think, we distill VLM knowledge into the BEV encoder and then discard the VLM entirely, retaining cognitive ability at zero inference cost while releasing the cognitive channel as a pluggable interface for optional human language commands. To foresee to act, we build an auto-regressive BEV world model that explicitly predicts future semantic maps conditioned on candidate actions, serving as an interpretable physical sandbox from which safety metrics are directly derived. Built upon this dual infrastructure, we optimize the driving policy via GRPO with a novel dual-reward mechanism: a physical reward derived from BEV rollouts enforces hard safety constraints, while a cognitive reward from a language-aligned scorer ensures intent compliance. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CoPhy not only achieves state-of-the-art results on NAVSIM v1 and v2 benchmarks, but also enables safer driving via cognitively informed scene compliance and flexible intent control through user-defined language instructions. VLA Adaptation with Real and Simulated Data: A Case Study on Robotic Chess 2026-11-09
📝 CoRL 2026 Workshop "Pretrain to Adapt: What Makes a Pretrained Policy Adaptable?". 8 pages main text, 15 pages total, 15 figures, 4 tables
Show abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are trained as general-purpose foundation models for robot learning, and require fine-tuning for each downstream task and robot. This raises practical questions such as how much data and training updates are needed, how checkpoints should be selected, and whether simulation can be leveraged to alleviate the cost of real data collection. We study these questions on a task that requires both precise manipulation and spatial understanding of the scene. In this task, an SO-101 arm moves a chess king between two squares that are given only by their names, such as e4 and a5. We fine-tune $π_{0.5}$ with various dataset sizes and update budgets, using teleoperated demonstrations as well as simulated demonstrations from a randomized scripted expert that imitates the variability of human demonstrations. In simulation, the success rate scales with both data and updates. Importantly, we find that a short learning-rate cool-down adds up to 31 points and is necessary for comparing checkpoints, and that validation loss and action error are unreliable for model selection. On the real arm, we fine-tune on up to 256 teleoperated demonstrations and evaluate ten policies in 25 trials each. Partial results indicate that pretraining on the simulated demonstrations helps substantially, most clearly when real data is scarce. We also present a negative result, in which training on the policy's own rollouts with filtered behavior cloning, RECAP-style advantage conditioning, corrections from an automatic coach, or residual reinforcement learning did not yield a measurable improvement over plain behavior cloning in our setting. We release the simulator, the expert and the real demonstrations.

The Relative Value of Demonstration Data: Execution Time Limits and Operator Mixtures in Behavior Cloning 2026-11-09
Show abstract

Robot demonstration datasets combine demonstrations from teleoperators of uneven experience, so collection teams need to know how many demonstrations from one group match those from another. We measure this with a data multiple: the ratio of the numbers of demonstrations two groups need for their policies to reach the same success rate. On robomimic's simulated Square and Can tasks, we train behavior-cloning policies on demonstrations from the two teleoperators robomimic labels Better and the two it labels Worse, varying dataset size and the share of Worse demonstrations. On Square, within robomimic's 25-second time limit, an all-Worse training set needs 1.55 times as many demonstrations as an all-Better set. Most of this difference is late completion: policies trained on Worse demonstrations finish later, as the Worse teleoperators do, and with a 100-second limit the multiple falls to 1.11. On Can, most of the difference remains at 100 seconds. If each Worse demonstration were worth 1/1.55 of a Better one, as in an all-Worse set, a half-Better, half-Worse set on Square would need 1.22 times as many demonstrations as an all-Better set. In fact, it needs 13% more than that, and this excess persists at every time limit from 20 to 200 seconds. The excess remains when training draws demonstrations uniformly instead of in proportion to their length. On Square, within the 25-second limit, switching to these Worse teleoperators is cheaper at equal success only if they earn less than a third of the Better teleoperators' hourly pay, because Worse demonstrations also take longer to record.

PAIR: Proprioception-Aligned Image-Ray Representations for Viewpoint-Robust Manipulation 2026-11-09
Show abstract

While imitation learning has made substantial progress toward generalizable robot manipulation through scaling, viewpoint robustness remains a major bottleneck. Visuomotor policies rely heavily on visual inputs, yet images provide an inherently ambiguous signal under viewpoint change: similar scenes may appear markedly different across viewpoints, while distinct states may share certain visual overlaps. A policy must therefore infer not only the task-relevant state, but also how it is visually projected under a particular camera configuration. This entanglement of viewpoint-dependent appearance and task-relevant state can make learning inefficient and limit generalization to unseen viewpoints. Recent work has begun to address this challenge through camera-ray conditioning and representation learning using geometric cues to induce invariance. However, providing camera information as additional context does not by itself ground visual representations in how the task scene is visually projected, while geometric alignment without camera context can introduce spurious correspondences by ignoring viewpoint-dependent projection. We propose PAIR, a contrastive learning framework that aligns joint image-ray representations using robot proprioception. PAIR encourages observations with similar robot states to remain close in representation space across viewpoints, providing a structured grounding signal for learning viewpoint-robust features. PAIR is a highly adaptable framework: it can be used to train Diffusion Policy (DP) or post-train vision-language-action (VLA) models. In simulated and real-world manipulation benchmarks with camera variation, PAIR consistently outperforms the behavior cloning baseline without ray conditioning by 12.7% and 26.7% points, respectively, demonstrating stronger generalization to unseen viewpoints. Project website: PAIRrobot.github.io.

Explicitly capturing demonstrator multi-modality for distillation into diffusion policies 2026-11-07
📝 Presented at Pretrain to Adapt Workshop at the 10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026). Project website: https://shaoyuan.cc/research/vine/
Show abstract

The diversity of a distilled diffusion policy's outputs matters beyond how it impacts the policy's success rate. In particular, downstream applications like test-time search and policy improvement require exploration. Thus, we want to distill multi-modal demonstrators into diffusion policies which reproduce their conditional action distributions. Standard data collection for behavior cloning applies a sliding window to demonstration episodes, which pairs unique observation histories with actions. These datasets can be fit by a policy that is either multi-modal, or unimodal with high Lipschitz constant to capture a diversity of actions seen at nearby observation samples. Prior work finds that diffusion policies trained on sparse data learn the latter, memorizing one action per observation. We adapt vine sampling to behavior cloning data collection to resolve this ambiguity: at some states the demonstrator visits, query it $k$ times from exactly the same observation. On a synthetic task, we study how the number of actions per observation, $k$, affects the learned policy, and find that larger $k$ yields a policy that better matches the demonstrator's conditional distribution, including its multi-modality and diversity. On a 2D truck unloading task, vine sampling likewise leads to a policy closer to the demonstrator and substantially improves both best-of-$N$ and expert iteration performance.

Online Imitation Learning for Stabilizing Vlasov--Poisson Plasmas 2026-11-07
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 43 pages, 9 figures, 2 tables
Show abstract

We study the problem of controlling instability in the Vlasov--Poisson plasma systems, a fundamental challenge for nuclear fusion control. Motivated by the partial observability of plasma states in practice, we investigate imitation learning algorithms that learn from a fully-observable expert controller, but operate under macroscopic measurements. We theoretically establish a separation between online and offline imitation learning --- even a small behavior cloning error leads to error compounding that exponentially amplifies instability over time. By way of contrast, the online imitation learning loss can polynomially control the stability of rollout trajectories. We then propose a DAgger-style algorithm that learns the stabilizing controller under partial observability. Simulation results on a 1D Vlasov--Poisson system demonstrate that our algorithm can effectively stabilize plasma instabilities over longer time horizons than behavior cloning.

WARP-JEPA: World-Model-in-the-Loop Amortized Rollout Planning for Frozen Hierarchical Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures 2026-11-07
Show abstract

Hierarchical Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (H-JEPA) enable long-horizon visual control but rely on costly test-time gradient descent for planning. Our analysis reveals that iterative optimization consumes up to 97.7% of episode runtime and that low predicted rollout costs do not necessarily translate into successful control. We further identify a planning-horizon asymmetry between long-horizon top-level composition and short two-step subordinate planning. To address these challenges, we propose WARP-JEPA, a hierarchical amortized planning framework that replaces online gradient optimization with learned multi-candidate trajectory proposers while keeping the world models frozen. WARP-JEPA combines a lightweight world-model-in-the-loop causal Transformer at the top level with parallel residual MLPs at subordinate levels. The proposers are trained using model-based rollout costs and best-of-K behavior cloning, enabling efficient candidate selection without test-time gradient optimization. Across 30 frozen H-JEPA models on four benchmarks, WARP-JEPA achieves 9.6-18.8x faster episode inference on long-horizon tasks (2.9-3.0x on Cube) and improves task success by up to 33.3 percentage points. These results demonstrate that amortized planning can substantially improve both inference efficiency and control performance without retraining the world models. LAFP: Preserving Latent Action Structure in Latent Policy Learning via Flow Matching 2026-11-07
📝 NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Learning high quality latent actions from large-scale unlabeled videos, coupled with limited real-world interaction data for training an action decoder, has emerged as a promising paradigm for scalable latent policy learning. However, existing approaches typically rely on behavior cloning, which tends to collapse inherently multimodal action distributions into unimodal ones, thereby degrading the pretrained latent action structure. While flow matching provides a potential alternative, directly applying it leads to a misalignment between latent actions and physical actions during action decoder training, due to the stochastic nature of the learned policy. To address these, we propose Latent Action Flow Policy (LAFP), which leverages flow matching for latent policy learning and introduces an inference-time interpolation mechanism to mitigate stochasticity-induced misalignment. Experimental results demonstrate that LAFP consistently outperforms prior methods on most downstream imitation learning tasks, achieving an 8.2\% improvement in average success rate while incurring less than 1x additional inference overhead.

Self-Supervised Keyframe Discovery for Horizon-Invariant Behavior Cloning 2026-11-07
📝 Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Behavior cloning (BC) in non-Markovian environments is a challenging problem because policies have to reason over contextual information over long horizons. Existing policy architectures rely on recurrent or attention-based mechanisms to capture long-term dependencies. However, recurrent models suffer from hidden-state collapse and gradient instability under backpropagation through time while attention-based models are fundamentally limited by context length. To address these issues, we propose Keyframe Mnemonics, a novel self-supervised method that $\textit{discovers}$ a set of information-critical observations ($\textit{mnemonics}$) by learning an objective from randomly sampled past observations and using it as a reward for keyframe selection. We then train a BC policy that conditions on the discovered keyframes to model the action distribution. Under the simplicity bias assumption, our formulation provides context retention guarantees over an infinite horizon, while maintaining a small set of decision-relevant keyframes in the policy's working memory. We evaluate our method on synthetic memory domains, where mnemonic-conditioned BC policies achieve $100$% success rates (SR) and generalize to horizons orders of magnitude beyond training without performance degradation. Moreover, we evaluate on a memory-intensive robot manipulation benchmark, achieving a $13.9$% average absolute SR improvement over the strongest baseline across $23$ tasks. Finally, we demonstrate generalization with $80$% SR at $20\times$ longer time horizons on a real robot. Code and videos available at https://keyframe-mnemonics.github.io.

Most robotic handwriting rigidly mounts the pen and reduces writing to trajectory tracking. A freely held pen instead couples grasp stability, tip motion, and contact force. A two-branch actor separates pen retention from arm-wrist writing while preserving their closed-loop coupling. Mitigating Covariate Shift in Behavioral Cloning via Robust Stationary Distribution Correction 2026-11-06
📝 25 pages, 11 figures, NeurIPS 2024, Code: https://github.com/tzs930/drildice
Show abstract

We consider offline imitation learning (IL), which aims to train an agent to imitate from the dataset of expert demonstrations without online interaction with the environment. Behavioral Cloning (BC) has been a simple yet effective approach to offline IL, but it is also well-known to be vulnerable to the covariate shift resulting from the mismatch between the state distributions induced by the learned policy and the expert policy. Moreover, as often occurs in practice, when expert datasets are collected from an arbitrary state distribution instead of a stationary one, these shifts become more pronounced, potentially leading to substantial failures in existing IL methods. Specifically, we focus on covariate shift resulting from arbitrary state data distributions, such as biased data collection or incomplete trajectories, rather than shifts induced by changes in dynamics or noisy expert actions. In this paper, to mitigate the effect of the covariate shifts in BC, we propose DrilDICE, which utilizes a distributionally robust BC objective by employing a stationary distribution correction ratio estimation (DICE) to derive a feasible solution. We evaluate the effectiveness of our method through an extensive set of experiments covering diverse covariate shift scenarios. The results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed approach in improving the robustness against the shifts, outperforming existing offline IL methods in such scenarios.

CLAW: Continuous Latent Action World Models via Adversarial Regularization 2026-11-05
📝 9 pages, 27 pages of supplementary material
Show abstract

We introduce CLAW, a fully end-to-end self-supervised framework for learning a world model jointly with continuous latent action representations directly from action-free videos. Our approach leverages adversarial latent regularization to prevent the latent from encoding future observations, so that it captures the structure of the action. The diffusion-based world model then models environment dynamics as a distribution over future observations rather than as a single deterministic rollout. Both components are learned from observation sequences alone without any action annotations. By simultaneously training the Latent Action Model and world model, CLAW learns to reason about how inferred actions induce environment transitions from visual observations alone. We show that the resulting latent action world model supports both imitation learning from observation and goal-directed planning. In imitation learning, latent actions extracted from raw videos enable behavior cloning. For planning, CLAW generates sequences of latent actions and maps them to executable actions to reach desired goals. Extensive experiments across diverse tasks and embodiments demonstrate that CLAW produces semantically meaningful latent action representations, supports effective action transfer, and enables planning and imitation from observation, outperforming existing methods.

When Robots Outweigh Objects: How Inertia Mismatch Shapes Robot Policy Learning 2026-11-04
📝 9 pages, 17 figures. Project website: https://younghyopark.me/inertia-mismatch/
Show abstract

Conventional robot arms can be extremely heavy compared with the everyday objects they manipulate, creating a significant inertia mismatch at contact. Even a small accidental bump from a heavy arm can knock a light object out of place. Modern learning methods have nevertheless demonstrated a wide range of successful manipulation behaviors on these platforms, suggesting that even extreme inertia mismatch can be overcome with sufficient data. However, it remains unclear whether high inertia imposes a hidden cost on learning, potentially worsening scaling with data or lowering the performance ceiling of modern robot learning methods. We investigate this question through controlled simulation experiments that vary robot and object masses. We find that reinforcement learning succeeds across a broad range of mass combinations, while behavior cloning learns more reliable policies when a lighter robot manipulates a heavier object. Finally, because new hardware is rarely an option, we explore ways to lower an existing arm's inertia at contact with existing hardware, either by choosing a lower-inertia workspace before collecting data or by steering the demonstrator toward lower-inertia configurations during the task. Ambient sensors offer a privacy-preserving solution for activity recognition in smart homes, yet they face a critical challenge in multi-resident environments known as Data Association, which aims to identify the resident triggering a specific anonymous sensor event. Existing approaches fundamentally fail to maintain global consistency by often yielding conflicting associations for silent residents. Additionally, these methods frequently suffer from severe trajectory fragmentation caused by the ontological mismatch of applying continuous physical motion models to discrete logical behaviors. 📝 13 pages, 5 figures, 3 tables. Project page: https://juliusju572.github.io/x-fly/

Aerial vision-language navigation (VLN) agents built on large vision-language models are expensive to query. Executing a complete predicted action chunk before replanning amortizes this cost, reducing model inference from 195 to 42 ms per executed waypoint for our policy. It also moves the states at which the policy is queried away from expert demonstrations, which supervise neither recovery from these states nor termination at the learner's own arrival. GSBR: Geometric Support Behavior Regularization for Online RL 2026-11-03
Show abstract

Regularizing online reinforcement learning (RL) toward a pretrained base policy can improve sample efficiency and learning stability, but regularization that is too restrictive limits how much the policy can improve. Base policies -- typically diffusion or flow-matching models trained by imitation learning on large, diverse datasets -- capture many behaviors that are reasonable but suboptimal. Existing regularizers using such base policies are too restrictive in one of two ways: behavioral cloning regularizers pull the actor toward the full base-policy distribution, penalizing it for discarding suboptimal modes, while hard support constraints forbid improvements that lie just outside the base policy's support. We propose Geometric Support Behavior Regularization (GSBR), which penalizes actions by their distance from the base policy's support. Actions within the support incur no penalty, so the actor can concentrate on high-value modes; outside the support, where value estimates are less reliable, the penalty grows smoothly with distance while still permitting improvement when the value gained outweighs the penalty. GSBR estimates this distance from a finite set of base-policy samples, so it requires only sample access to the base policy. Across 19 tasks on four robotic benchmarks, GSBR raises the average success rate from 33% for the base policy to 95%, achieving the highest average success rate and throughput among all compared methods, with about 23% higher normalized throughput than the next-best RL method. Project website and open-source code at https://charlesxu0124.github.io/gsbr/ .

Coherent Off-Policy Improvement of Large Behavior Models with Learned Rewards 2026-11-03
📝 15 pages, 10 figures
Show abstract

Distilling expert demonstration data into large generative models using behavioral cloning is a scalable approach to learning capable policies for robotic control, particularly for dexterous manipulation. Reinforcement learning (RL) can be used as a means to finetune these policies further using additional experience. An open question is whether RL is more sample-efficient than collecting more expert demonstrations. Prior work has finetuned large pretrained policies in a scalable fashion by applying RL to a smaller residual policy that corrects the pretrained model. However, for typical sparse reward tasks, RL algorithms can struggle to optimize the behavior in a sample-efficient manner. We explore inverse reinforcement learning, where a dense reward function is learned from expert demonstrations, potentially reducing the challenge of RL finetuning. We specifically consider coherent imitation learning, an IRL method that facilitates improvement of the BC policy by using a specific learned reward formulation with theoretical guarantees for imitation learning. We show that our IRL method maintains or improves the performance of pi-0.5 on all six sparse manipulation tasks and achieves a $\geq 90\%$ success rate on five out of six complex manipulation tasks, outperforming RL-based baselines using sparse rewards. We also demonstrate real-world learning on a robot manipulation task, finetuning pi-0.5 to success with less than 30 minutes of environment interaction time. By ensuring our initial pretrained finetuning policy is optimal for our initial reward and critic, our method circumvents the initial drop commonly seen in RL finetuning and enables faster improvement.

Trusting Imagined Futures: Auditing Imagined Rewards for VLA Post-Training 2026-11-03
Show abstract

Video world models enable candidate actions to be evaluated through imagined outcomes, but high predicted rewards can favor trajectories with poor execution quality. We introduce Trusting Imagined Futures (TIF), an offline supervision selection method for vision-language-action (VLA) post-training that audits predicted rewards using execution feedback. A multidimensional reward model predicts a utility score for candidate ranking and five dimensions of execution quality. An auditor uses reward predictions and uncertainty to identify overestimation patterns learned from paired imagined videos and real executions. TIF accepts the candidate with the highest predicted utility when its advantage over the existing trajectory exceeds a margin and its audit risk passes a threshold; otherwise, it retains the existing supervision. The retained real trajectories then supervise behavior cloning. Across four LIBERO suites and three training seeds, TIF achieves a mean success rate of 76.25%, improving over training on the existing trajectories by 3.00 percentage points with the same trajectory count and training budget. Adding audit gating yields 1.47 percentage points with the reward model and advantage margin held fixed. The gains persist against advantage ranking and logistic regression risk selection at matched replacement counts. On recorded videos from three real robot tasks, TIF increases clean completion among selected trajectories from 58.33% to 70.00% across 60 candidate pools.
