Which Physical Tactile Cues Matter for Contact-Rich Imitation Learning? A Controlled Real-Robot Study 2026-11-04
📝 6 pages, 5 figures, 2 tables. Accepted to IEEE ROBIO 2026
Show abstract

Vision-based tactile sensors capture multiple aspects of physical interaction within a single image stream, including contact appearance, geometry, indentation depth, and tangential deformation. However, existing visuo-tactile policies often directly process tactile observations as images, making it difficult to determine which physical cues contribute to policy learning. This paper presents a controlled real-robot study that compares physically interpretable tactile signals using the LeRobot platform, enhanced with our visuo-tactile perception system. From the same tactile stream, we derive tactile RGB images, normal/depth maps, and marker-flow fields, representing contact appearance, geometry, and tangential deformation, respectively. Using identical task demonstrations and the same policy backbone, we evaluate the individual and combined contributions of these tactile cues. We further compare tactile encoders and visuo-tactile fusion strategies. Evaluation across 720 real-robot trials yields four key findings: (1) tactile perception substantially improves the overall success rate from 57.8% to 78.9%, although its contribution varies across task stages; (2) marker flow is the most effective individual cue, achieving a success rate of 74.4% and playing a particularly important role in regulating established contact; (3) cue-factorized encoding consistently improves performance, highlighting that different physical cues require tailored processing techniques; and (4) although the effectiveness of tactile fusion is task-dependent, cross-attention offers a lightweight solution with performance comparable to that of pretrained tactile encoders.

Power Grid Control with Graph-Based Distributed Reinforcement Learning 2026-11-04
Show abstract

The necessary integration of renewable energy sources, combined with the expanding scale of power networks, presents significant challenges in controlling modern power grids. Traditional control systems, which are human and optimization-based, struggle to adapt and to scale in such an evolving context, motivating the exploration of more dynamic and distributed control strategies. This work advances a graph-based distributed reinforcement learning framework for real-time, scalable grid management. The proposed architecture consists of a network of distributed low-level agents acting on individual power lines and coordinated by a high-level manager agent. A Graph Neural Network (GNN) is employed to encode the network's topological information within the single low-level agent's observation. To accelerate convergence and enhance learning stability, the framework integrates imitation learning and potential-based reward shaping. In contrast to conventional decentralized approaches that decompose only the action space while relying on global observations, this method also decomposes the observation space. Each low-level agent acts based on a structured and informative local view of the environment constructed through the GNN. Experiments on the Grid2Op simulation environment show the effectiveness of the approach, which consistently outperforms the standard baseline commonly adopted in the field. Additionally, the proposed model proves to be much more computationally efficient than the simulation-based Expert method.

Distill to Think, Foresee to Act: Cognitive-Physical Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Driving 2026-11-04
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

Current end-to-end autonomous driving models are fundamentally constrained by the behavioral cloning ceiling of imitation learning. While reinforcement learning offers a path to smarter autonomy, it demands two missing pieces of infrastructure: (1) a cognitive foundation that understands traffic semantics and driving intent, and (2) a foresighted physical environment that can anticipate the consequences of candidate actions. To this end, we propose CoPhy, a CognitivePhysical reinforcement learning framework for autonomous driving. To distill to think, we distill VLM knowledge into the BEV encoder and then discard the VLM entirely, retaining cognitive ability at zero inference cost while releasing the cognitive channel as a pluggable interface for optional human language commands. To foresee to act, we build an auto-regressive BEV world model that explicitly predicts future semantic maps conditioned on candidate actions, serving as an interpretable physical sandbox from which safety metrics are directly derived. Built upon this dual infrastructure, we optimize the driving policy via GRPO with a novel dual-reward mechanism: a physical reward derived from BEV rollouts enforces hard safety constraints, while a cognitive reward from a language-aligned scorer ensures intent compliance. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CoPhy not only achieves state-of-the-art results on NAVSIM v1 and v2 benchmarks, but also enables safer driving via cognitively informed scene compliance and flexible intent control through user-defined language instructions. Project page: https://wuyang98.github.io/CoPhy/.

ELA: End-to-End Latent Action Policy Learning from Video Demonstrations 2026-11-03
Show abstract

Robot imitation learning is limited by the scarcity of action-labeled data, while action-free demonstrations such as videos are abundant. Recent latent-action methods learn action-relevant representations from observation transitions, but they do not optimize latent actions for downstream control, leading to latent representations that are difficult for policies to model and difficult to accurately decode into continuous robot actions. This paper introduces ELA, an end-to-end framework for learning from video demonstrations via latent actions jointly optimized for policy learning and action decoding. ELA jointly trains a latent action model, latent action policy, and action decoder, enabling policies to leverage abundant video data with only a handful of action-labeled robot demonstrations. Experiments on simulated and real-world manipulation tasks show that ELA consistently outperforms prior latent action baselines. Moreover, it integrates seamlessly with existing imitation learning methods through lightweight 4M-parameter action decoders, requiring no changes to policy architectures or training objectives. GUILD: Guided Iterative Data Collection for Imitation Learning 2026-11-03
Show abstract

Imitation learning policies in robotics are trained on datasets collected through costly real-world teleoperation. Often, the data-collection process is unguided, relying on the teleoperator's intuition, resulting in data that contributes little to overall policy performance. Recent work has shown that curating such unguided datasets can substantially improve policy performance, but these approaches operate by filtering existing datasets after collection and therefore do not prevent the acquisition of uninformative data. In this work, we propose GUILD, a novel Guided Data Collection (GDC) method that actively reasons about which data should be collected to maximize downstream policy performance. We introduce an iterative framework that alternates between 1) task proposal and data collection and 2) data evaluation to close the loop. For task proposal, we leverage semantic priors from vision-language models to identify relevant environments, object configurations, and goals for data collection. However, since semantic relevance does not imply policy improvement, we perform data evaluation. By leveraging predictive data attribution we approximate how the collected trajectories influence policy performance on a target deployment setting. This signal is then used as feedback to guide subsequent data collection, closing the data collection loop. We evaluate our approach in simulation and real robot settings, demonstrating that guided data collection substantially improves sample efficiency and deployment performance compared to passive data collection strategies, multiple baselines and experienced roboticists. More information at https://robin-lab.cs.utexas.edu/guild2026/

GSBR: Geometric Support Behavior Regularization for Online RL 2026-11-03
Show abstract

Regularizing online reinforcement learning (RL) toward a pretrained base policy can improve sample efficiency and learning stability, but regularization that is too restrictive limits how much the policy can improve. Base policies -- typically diffusion or flow-matching models trained by imitation learning on large, diverse datasets -- capture many behaviors that are reasonable but suboptimal. Existing regularizers using such base policies are too restrictive in one of two ways: behavioral cloning regularizers pull the actor toward the full base-policy distribution, penalizing it for discarding suboptimal modes, while hard support constraints forbid improvements that lie just outside the base policy's support. We propose Geometric Support Behavior Regularization (GSBR), which penalizes actions by their distance from the base policy's support. Actions within the support incur no penalty, so the actor can concentrate on high-value modes; outside the support, where value estimates are less reliable, the penalty grows smoothly with distance while still permitting improvement when the value gained outweighs the penalty. GSBR estimates this distance from a finite set of base-policy samples, so it requires only sample access to the base policy. Across 19 tasks on four robotic benchmarks, GSBR raises the average success rate from 33% for the base policy to 95%, achieving the highest average success rate and throughput among all compared methods, with about 23% higher normalized throughput than the next-best RL method. Project website and open-source code at https://charlesxu0124.github.io/gsbr/ .

Coherent Off-Policy Improvement of Large Behavior Models with Learned Rewards 2026-11-03
📝 15 pages, 10 figures
🤖 AI 深度总结（DeepSeek 全文阅读）· 点击展开
Show abstract

Distilling expert demonstration data into large generative models using behavioral cloning is a scalable approach to learning capable policies for robotic control, particularly for dexterous manipulation. Reinforcement learning (RL) can be used as a means to finetune these policies further using additional experience. An open question is whether RL is more sample-efficient than collecting more expert demonstrations. Prior work has finetuned large pretrained policies in a scalable fashion by applying RL to a smaller residual policy that corrects the pretrained model. However, for typical sparse reward tasks, RL algorithms can struggle to optimize the behavior in a sample-efficient manner. We explore inverse reinforcement learning, where a dense reward function is learned from expert demonstrations, potentially reducing the challenge of RL finetuning. We specifically consider coherent imitation learning, an IRL method that facilitates improvement of the BC policy by using a specific learned reward formulation with theoretical guarantees for imitation learning. We show that our IRL method maintains or improves the performance of pi-0.5 on all six sparse manipulation tasks and achieves a $\geq 90\%$ success rate on five out of six complex manipulation tasks, outperforming RL-based baselines using sparse rewards. We also demonstrate real-world learning on a robot manipulation task, finetuning pi-0.5 to success with less than 30 minutes of environment interaction time. By ensuring our initial pretrained finetuning policy is optimal for our initial reward and critic, our method circumvents the initial drop commonly seen in RL finetuning and enables faster improvement.

ResponsibleRobotBench: Benchmarking Risk-Aware Task-Level Decision Making for Robotic Manipulation with Large Multimodal Models 2026-11-03
📝 28 pages, including a 12-page appendix. Revised manuscript with expanded experimental analysis and supplementary details. Project website: https://responsible-robot-benchmark.github.io/
Show abstract

Safe task-level manipulation requires a planner to recognize hazards, select appropriate mitigation actions, and satisfy their execution prerequisites without abandoning the original goal. We introduce ResponsibleRobotBench, a benchmark of 23 manipulation task configurations with separately specified goals and safety conditions. It connects hazard-category predictions, planned actions, execution feedback, and terminal outcomes at the episode level. Across evaluations of five large multimodal models, terminal safety and safe task completion can diverge: defense prompts increase terminal safety for three tested models but affect safe success in different directions. Correct hazard-category predictions also fail to ensure executable mitigation. In a cutting task under the default configuration, GPT-4o mini identifies the human hazard in all 100 episodes but never requests assistance, and every episode ends unsafe. Offering a help action and actually requesting it lead to different comparisons: outcomes differ between action menus even for a model that never requests help, and repeated requests can leave execution failures unresolved. Imitation-learning evaluations and physical robot sequences illustrate the need to coordinate mitigation with goal actions. Together, these results motivate evaluating mitigation selection, action prerequisites, and recovery alongside hazard recognition and terminal outcomes. The appendix provides task definitions, checker conditions, and prompts. Project website: https://responsible-robot-benchmark.github.io/.

Physical Diffusion Policy via MPM-Guided 3D Gaussian Simulation 2026-11-03
📝 10 pages
Show abstract

Learning robust manipulation policies for diverse everyday objects remains challenging because objects with different materials exhibit distinct deformation and contact dynamics. While diffusion policies provide a powerful framework for modeling multimodal manipulation behaviors, they typically lack explicit physical reasoning and may produce averaged actions when facing heterogeneous objects. We present a physics-aware diffusion policy that augments visual imitation learning with object physical understanding. Our framework recovers material properties from visual demonstrations by coupling 3D Gaussian Splatting with differentiable Material Point Method simulation, where video guidance and particle-level constraints enable accurate physical parameter estimation. The estimated physics conditions are further integrated into a material-aware Mixture-of-Experts diffusion policy, allowing adaptive behavior generation across different physical regimes. For practical deployment, we introduce a physics memory bank that provides efficient physical retrieval without requiring online simulation optimization. Experiments on a wide range of simulated and real-world objects demonstrate improved manipulation success and generalization over existing diffusion-based approaches. These results show that incorporating physical knowledge is critical for scalable robot manipulation in diverse environments.

Dynamics-Aware Meta-Imitation for Generalization to Unseen Robotic Manipulation 2026-11-02
Show abstract

Imitation Learning aims to learn skills from extensive observations and demonstrations for robots, so it suffers from data scarcity and environment generalization. The existing methods predominantly focus on imitation from in-domain tasks and consequently struggle with generalization to unseen tasks. To bridge this generalization gap, we propose the \textbf{D}ynamics-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{M}eta-\textbf{I}mitation (DAMI) framework. By integrating meta-learning to construct a shared skill space, DAMI equips agents for rapid adaptation to novel tasks. How to Peel with a Knife: Aligning Fine-Grained Manipulation with Human Preference 2026-11-02
📝 Project page can be found at https://toruowo.github.io/peel
Show abstract

Many essential manipulation tasks - such as food preparation, surgery, and craftsmanship - remain difficult for autonomous robots. These tasks are characterized not only by contact-rich, force-sensitive dynamics, but also by their "implicit" success criteria: unlike pick-and-place, task quality in these domains is continuous and subjective (e.g. how well a potato is peeled), making quantitative evaluation and reward design difficult. We present a learning framework for such tasks, using peeling with a knife as a representative example. Our approach follows a two-stage pipeline: first, we learn a robust initial policy via force-aware data collection and imitation learning, enabling generalization across object variations; second, we refine the policy through preference-based finetuning using a learned reward model that combines quantitative task metrics with qualitative human feedback, aligning policy behavior with human-evaluated task quality. Using only 50~200 peeling trajectories, our system achieves 100% same-produce success on cucumbers, apples, and potatoes, with performance improving by up to 40% through preference-based finetuning. Remarkably, policies trained on one produce category also transfer zero-shot to selected held-out produce categories, achieving 50% success on zucchini, 90% on pear, and 80% on daikon radish.

ODE Policy: Robot Learning via Action Dynamics, Not Actions 2026-11-01
📝 35 pages, 23 figures
Show abstract

Most robot policies, whether trained by imitation learning (IL) or reinforcement learning (RL), map observations to action values at discrete control steps and leave the continuity of physical motion to be learned implicitly. We propose ODE policy, which learns the dynamics of the action rather than the action itself. A neural ordinary differential equation (ODE) learns the vector field of the action by matching the integrated actions to the targets over short action chunks in IL and single control steps in RL. A lightweight diversity head captures multi-modal behavior by randomizing the direction of the field. At deployment, each action is integrated from the previous one, so the executed trajectory is continuous by construction. We evaluate it as a visuomotor policy, as the action head of vision-language-action (VLA) and world action models (WAMs), and as the actor of model-based RL against 14 baselines on unimanual, bimanual, dexterous, locomotion, and loco-manipulation tasks. In IL, against flow matching, it reaches 5.9$\times$ the success, 5.4$\times$ the generalization, and 3.9$\times$ the mode balance after one epoch, and generates actions 10.8$\times$ faster. It mostly moves more smoothly than human demonstrations. On 14 real-world tasks across single-arm, dual-arm, dexterous, and humanoid robots, it achieves the highest success rate. In RL, it further speeds up SuperMimic for humanoid motion tracking and smooths its actions. Efficient in learning, data, parameters, and execution, ODE policy offers a promising path to scaling robot learning.

Learning from an Imitable Teacher: Competition Solution for the MetaFine Challenge 2026-10-31
📝 2026 CoRL MetaFine Open Competition 2nd place solution
Show abstract

The MetaFine Open Competition (RoboFineMani workshop, CoRL 2026) evaluates fine-grained manipulation beyond binary success. Its five simulated tasks, from grasping a bottle by an instructed part to inserting letter-shaped pegs with 4 mm clearance, are scored on perception under lighting and camera changes, instruction understanding, and behavior. This report presents Team Friday's solution, which got second place with an overall score of 0.947, more than twice the $π_0$ baseline. Instead of fine-tuning a large vision-language-action (VLA) model, we train one 19M-parameter policy per task with a separate instruction classifier and focus on the data, all generated in the simulator by a privileged scripted teacher, including its corrections of the student's mistakes. By analyzing failed episodes, we find that a teacher that almost always succeeds can still teach a memoryless student badly: its pauses become stalls, its fixed habits become shortcuts, and it never shows the errors the student makes. Making the teacher \emph{imitable} raised the official score on letter insertion, the hardest task, from 0.44 to 0.82 with 32\% more data and the same architecture, and finally to 0.88. We summarize practical lessons on demonstration data and evaluation, which we hope will help in imitation learning for precise manipulation.

Does Validation Loss Predict Real-Robot Success? Evidence from Four Imitation-Learning Policies on a Low-Cost Arm 2026-10-30
📝 21 pages, 16 figures, 5 tables. Companion to arXiv:2610.37170. Dataset: https://huggingface.co/datasets/fysical/greenball_pool_225_v1
Show abstract

Held-out validation loss is the standard offline signal for choosing checkpoints and comparing models, but its relation to closed-loop success on a real robot is rarely measured. We measure it for ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA and $π_{0.5}$ (action expert only), trained on nested subsets of 12 to 200 demonstrations of a pick-and-place task on an SO-101 arm. We score 82 checkpoints on 25 held-out demonstrations, with each policy's training loss and with the error of the actions it outputs, and compare the scores with 930 bench rollouts. We also score all 700 intermediate checkpoints of 70 training runs, and recover the ball positions of every training, held-out and bench episode from the front camera. Within each policy, hold-out action error correlates with bench success (Spearman $ρ$ from -0.23 to -0.88), but no more strongly than the number of training episodes alone, and not at all among seeds trained on the same data. Across policies the ranking is inverted ($ρ$ = +0.90 at 200 episodes): the policy with the lowest hold-out error succeeded in 3 of 110 rollouts. The most common failure, the arm never leaving its rest pose, ended 96% of rollouts for Diffusion Policy trained on untrimmed demonstrations and 47% for SmolVLA, and cannot occur in a hold-out set made of demonstrations. Among rollouts in which the arm moved, SmolVLA's hold-out action error predicts success well ($ρ$ = -0.81). For the diffusion and flow-matching policies, hold-out loss rises two- to five-fold during training while hold-out action error falls by up to 29%. Held-out metrics are a sound diagnostic of learning but no substitute for evaluation on the robot, and we recommend reporting initiation failure and success among started rollouts as separate bench outcomes.

FOCUS: First-Order Control Using Optimal Transport Supervision for Imitation from Observations 2026-10-30
📝 Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Physical Understanding for Decision-Making
Show abstract

Imitation learning from observations must recover behavior from state-only demonstrations without known temporal correspondence. Differentiable simulators provide pathwise policy gradients, but differentiating through long, contact-rich trajectories can make optimization unstable. We introduce FOCUS, which combines local optimal-transport alignment with short-horizon differentiable actor-critic learning. FOCUS matches agent rollout windows to multiple sampled expert segments using a soft best-of-$K$ objective that emphasizes locally compatible matches. The resulting transport-based costs provide differentiable imitation rewards, while a learned critic bootstraps value beyond the rollout horizon. FOCUS requires neither temporally aligned trajectories nor expert-aligned initialization or teacher forcing. Across contact-rich locomotion tasks in DFlex, including a musculoskeletal humanoid controlled by 152 muscle activations, FOCUS improves sample efficiency and training stability over adversarial imitation-from-observation methods and prior differentiable imitation baselines.

Decompose and Reorganize: Planning with Primitives and Visuomotor Policies Learned from Demonstrations 2026-10-30
📝 Accepted by T-RO. Website: https://dr-lfd.github.io/DR-LfD-website
Show abstract

Successfully automating dexterous, long-horizon bimanual manipulation requires frameworks capable of both high-level reasoning and fine-grained execution. Traditional task and motion planning (TAMP), while excellent at symbolic planning, is often brittle in contact-rich operations. Simultaneously, imitation learning (IL), while effective in manipulation tasks with visual feedback, is limited by its low capability in spatial generalization and multi-stage operation. To reconcile their complementary strengths and limitations, we propose DR-LfD (Decomposed and Reorganized Skills Learned from Demonstrations), a framework that integrates visuomotor policies into a TAMP-gated decision-making system. Based on contact relationships, DR-LfD decomposes human demonstrations into atomic skills, which are reproduced as visuomotor policies or object-centric primitives. The initiation, termination, and constraints of the visuomotor policies are carefully modeled and implemented in a TAMP-compatible form, enabling reorganization of skills learned from different sources. DR-LfD transforms the learning problem from one requiring exponential demonstration data over possible skill sequences to one whose demonstration burden scales with the number of distinct skill types, with limited data for each skill. Real-world and simulation benchmarks demonstrate data-efficient skill composition under unseen setups and physical constraints. On two DexMimicGen tasks, DR-LfD with 100 demonstrations outperforms diffusion-based imitation policies with 1000.

Cross-Embodiment Motion Planning by Coupling Body-Agnostic World Model with Differentiable Ego-Dynamics 2026-10-30
📝 8 pages
Show abstract
