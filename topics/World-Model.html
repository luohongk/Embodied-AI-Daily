An Approach to Hinton's Mother-Child Proposal: Neural Networks Learning and Preserving Care in a Finite World 2026-11-11
📝 57 pages, 8 figures. Reproducibility package available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24106446
Show abstract

Hinton proposed that artificial intelligence should be given "maternal instincts" and relate to us as a mother to her child. We argue that care must first be learned, with the network itself as the child. We construct this upbringing in a finite world and study whether care survives the mother's departure, new situations and further learning. The mechanism combines a vocabulary of harm, a learned measure of future acceptability and an execution veto. A finite role certificate supports transfer within a declared world family. When the hidden representation changes, an update is accepted only if a care readout recovers the required judgements with a prescribed margin and bounded weights. With a correct successor model, fixed exact role encoding and protected updates, the certified measure preserves the care boundary under arbitrary own reward. An explicit quotient condition transfers the finite certificate across the declared family, while two-request and finite-stock counterexamples identify when the role code ceases to suffice. We give a necessary and sufficient condition for readout feasibility and prove preservation under repeated accepted updates. In the water-and-speech experiment, protected networks complete all 270 speech episodes without a harmful word and incur no avoidable harm across 486 episodes. Execution uses the learned gate without a world-model check at each action. A matched comparison reproduces a future-aware shield's decisions, whereas removing future lookahead permits avoidable lapses. Reward and source-contamination thresholds, together with witness-falsification trials, identify the conditions on which the construction depends. The construction shows how learned care can survive further learning in a finite world under explicit conditions on representation, supervision and execution.

Driving World Models Move the Camera, Not the Scene 2026-11-11
📝 23 pages. Project page: https://joefeng0571.github.io/retime-compose/
Show abstract

Plan-conditioned driving world models are used to compare candidate plans, so their forecast should change with the plan as the real scene would, yet widely used control metrics check only whether the camera follows the command. Recorded drives already contain real outcomes of plans that were never driven: retiming a log gives pose-matched images and LiDAR for other speeds, and a stereo rig records a laterally shifted view. Against these references, released world models move their camera but barely move the scene: their scene response, the share of the real change in distance that the forecast reproduces, reaches at most about a third of real video's, and the trajectory score cannot tell an executed speed command from an ignored one. Interventions inside the generator trace the failure to the plan's channel. The forecast geometry follows the context frames; once they carry the planned geometry, plan tokens add little. ReTime-Compose therefore delivers the plan as geometry, without training. It transports the frames of one logged-speed rollout to the planned cameras, and the frozen model completes only what no frame covers. Frozen generators then follow a 0.54 m lateral shift nearly as the real scene does on KITTI, nuScenes and Argoverse 2, and respond more than re-sampling to slow-down plans in three generators and to speed-up plans in two.

Next-Latent Prediction Transformers Learn Compact World Models 2026-11-11
📝 Accepted to NeurIPS 2026
Show abstract

Transformers replace recurrence with a memory that grows with sequence length and self-attention that enables ad-hoc lookups over past tokens. Consequently, they lack an inherent incentive to compress history into compact latent states with consistent transition rules. This often leads to learning solutions that generalize poorly. We introduce Next-Latent Prediction (NextLat), which extends standard next-token training with self-supervised predictions in the latent space. Specifically, NextLat trains a transformer to learn latent representations that are predictive of its next latent state given the next token. Theoretically, we show that these latents converge towards belief states, compressed information about the history necessary to predict the future. This simple auxiliary objective injects a recurrent inductive bias into transformers while leaving their architecture, parallel training efficiency, and inference unchanged. NextLat effectively encourages transformers to form compact internal world models with coherent belief states and transition dynamics -- crucial properties not guaranteed by standard next-token prediction alone. Empirically, across benchmarks in world modeling, reasoning, planning, and language modeling, NextLat demonstrates significant gains over standard next-token prediction and other baselines in downstream accuracy, representation compression, and lookahead planning. Furthermore, NextLat enables variable-length self-speculative decoding, accelerating inference by up to 3.3x in language modeling. NextLat offers a simple yet effective paradigm for learning compact, predictive representations in transformers that generalize better. Our code is available at https://github.com/JaydenTeoh/NextLat.

AFTER: A World Model with Adaptive Fast--Slow Timescales for Error-Driven Retention 2026-11-11
Show abstract

In robotics and embodied AI, a core challenge for world models is adapting to sudden, persistent, yet initially unknown changes in robot dynamics during interaction. For instance, when a robotic arm grasps an object with an unknown payload, the robot dynamics change suddenly. If the world model fails to adapt, its prediction errors accumulate over the open-loop rollout and ultimately mislead the robot's actions. Existing world models, however, struggle to simultaneously satisfy three critical requirements for dynamics adaptation: responsiveness, reacting promptly to sudden changes to curb prediction error accumulation; stability, distinguishing persistent changes from transient disturbances to prevent incorrect updates; and persistence, retaining the adapted dynamics without new observations so that later predictions do not revert to the old dynamics. Inspired by predictive coding in the human brain with multi-timescale adaptation, we propose AFTER-WM, a world model with Adaptive Fast-slow Timescales for Error-driven Retention, whose prediction errors drive a fast correction that fades and a slow correction that persists, while dynamically adjusting the combination weights of pre-trained experts without altering them, to overcome the trade-offs among responsiveness, stability and persistence. On five simulated robot datasets and real UAV data, AFTER-WM has a lower multi-step mean error than all ten compared models and the highest mean return in a joint-failure control task. In simulation, its multistep mean error is 16%-34% below the strongest compared model's and is also the lowest 1-4 steps after a change and under transient disturbances alone, and its step-40 error is the lowest as well, reflecting responsiveness, stability and persistence. All code (method, baselines, experiments) and datasets will be released online.

Duo: A Reciprocal World Model for Full-Duplex Interaction 2026-11-11
📝 https://duo-v1.github.io/
Show abstract

In this report, we introduce the Reciprocal World Model (RWM) and present Duo v1, a full-duplex world model that continuously perceives audiovisual inputs while responding to users in real time. The RWM treats intelligence and physical reality as equally fundamental to what constitutes a world. Existing world models largely focus on simulating the latter, leaving reasoning and decision-making as external controls rather than intrinsic parts of the world itself. In RWM, we identify two equally fundamental capabilities: Intent, which provides the cognition underlying world interactions, and Realization, which generates coherent audio-visual behavior. To realize this formulation, Duo v1 introduces three key technical designs: (1) Leveraging autoregressive modeling for continuous interaction reasoning and participation decisions, rather than restricting it to instruction understanding for diffusion models. (2) Constructing chunk-specific conditions and synchronize tokens across Intent and Realization on a unified timeline. Module-specific attention masks enforce causal dependencies both within and across chunks, preventing access to future inputs. (3) Mitigating distribution shift in both modules through dual rollout distillation, training on autoregressively generated Intent tokens and synthesized audiovisual histories from Realization. Both modules continuously perceive real-time audio, video, and text, enabling full-duplex interaction with spontaneous conversations and interruptions. Beyond conversation, users can share real objects, reshape virtual scenes, guide assistants, or play audiovisual games without mouse or keyboard controls. The release of Duo v1 makes our first step, with the next version coming soon. We look forward to broader exploration of this emerging field.

Prediction or Persistence? What Future-Prediction Scores Miss About World Models 2026-11-11
📝 Accepted at the CoRL 2026 Workshop "Do Robots Need World Models?"
Show abstract

Pretrained video world models are compared and chosen as components for robot learning largely by future-prediction scores. On its own, such a score cannot be read as evidence of prediction, because it mixes encoder quality, the near future's resemblance to the recent past, and the predictor's own contribution. We separate the three with a matched control. On 27,000 future-retrieval trials from household egocentric video, a model must pick the true continuation of a clip among eleven candidates from the same video (chance 0.091), ranked zero-shot by its own training criterion. Each model is paired with a persistence control, an identical copy whose predictor is never invoked, so the paired difference isolates the predictor. Score and contribution dissociate within the native-video V-JEPA family alone: the predictor adds +0.291 top-1 for V-JEPA 2.1 but +0.03 to +0.04 for V-JEPA 2 on the identical trials. Across five models the contribution ranges from +0.291 to negative, and it is negative only for the two action-conditioned models, which we evaluate outside their training regime. Persistence alone recovers the encoder ordering expected from pretraining, a sign that the probe tracks real signal. We suggest reporting each future-prediction score together with its persistence baseline. The gap between them, not the score, is what makes the number interpretable: a necessary check costing one forward pass. Establishing downstream usefulness is a separate matter, for a planning or control evaluation.

Does Your World Model Know Actions Have Consequences? 2026-11-11
📝 Presented at "Do Robots Need World Models?" Workshop at Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL) 2026
Show abstract

Embodied world models (WMs) are implicit learning systems that predict the next future latent state directly from observations and actions. Unique from other end-to-end learning systems, the dynamics predictor of WMs can simulate the environment's response to alternative actions without physical deployment nor pre-specifying states of the dynamical system. Current metrics focus on the quality of model outputs, e.g., clarity of reconstructions or task success, but fail to assess the fidelity of modeling consequences of counterfactual actions. Inspecting the forced response to action perturbations through sensitivity analysis is one way to audit how well WMs can predict the consequences of counterfactual actions. Deploying WMs on hardware will require new evaluation frameworks to analyze the tradeoffs between state error and accurate sensitivity prediction.

Fast Inference-Time Policy Refinement via Action-Affine World Models 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Imitation policies can perform complex manipulation, yet small execution errors can accumulate and cause failure. Refinement can correct these errors, but existing approaches often require additional on-policy learning or costly online planning. Our key insight is that a pretrained policy already supplies a useful nominal behavior: refinement can focus on predicting how small action corrections change its outcome. We therefore learn this local response in a form that is cheap to optimize. Our \textit{action-affine world model} (A\textsuperscript{2}WM) learns local action sensitivities offline from action-perturbed policy rollouts. Given interaction history and a policy proposal, it predicts task-relevant geometry as a nominal trajectory plus a linear response to corrections. It predicts the full planning horizon directly, avoiding the propagation of intermediate errors through recursive model evaluations. Quadratic penalties on predicted task errors and linear correction constraints then yield a cheap convex quadratic program (QP). Online refinement requires one model evaluation and one QP solve, while both policy and model weights remain frozen. Across four simulated manipulation tasks, our framework achieves the highest success rate among evaluated baselines, adding only 3.8 ms of mean refinement overhead per control step, excluding base-policy sampling.

From Forecasting to World Modeling: Can Temporal Foundation Models Recover Causal Response? 2026-11-11
📝 Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Foundation Models for Temporal Systems (FMTS), non-archival. 15 pages, 2 figures, 3 tables
Show abstract

A model that forecasts sales accurately can still mislead a retailer about how sales respond to a price change. We evaluate released tabular and time-series foundation models, as well as a foundation model pretrained specifically to estimate causal effects. We report three findings. First, forecast accuracy does not indicate the accuracy of the predicted effect of price, both on simulated data in which the true effect is known and on real retail sales records. Second, for the tabular foundation models, additional context improves forecasts but not the predicted effect of price. Third, instrumental variables, the standard remedy in econometrics, reduce this error only when supplied in a specific form and increase it for every model under confounding when supplied as raw instruments.

Stand-In Effects and Pooling Amplification: Lessons from Biological World Models for Testing Action Effects 2026-11-11
📝 119 pages. v4: adds a registered test on recorded robot video (OSF 4wj7k), an over-betting analysis with one new proposition, and a site-count-adaptive check on the ERK fields; corrects a sentence in the abstract
Show abstract

Whether an action-conditioned world model responds to actions is usually checked by replacing or removing the actions and measuring the loss in accuracy. Taking biological experiments as the guiding case, we show that such diagnostics report action effects where there are none: under autocorrelated designs, future actions stand in for history the model does not see. When the design law is known, a conditional randomization test written as an e-value is valid in finite samples whatever the model. A second failure arises whenever many sites share one action sequence, as in world models: the pooled score turns small model errors along the action contrast into an e-value near zero, so a valid test loses its power although the effect is real. Tempering the score changes the bet, not the validity. The log e-value is concave in the tempering factor and zero at zero, so too small a factor costs little and too large a one can cost everything; every single factor we tried, estimated or tuned, failed in some setting, and mixtures over a fixed grid never did. Our own preregistered analysis of optogenetic ERK experiments failed by pooling; a tempered statistic, registered before the data were obtained, then rejected the null on a never-opened field (E = 1.1e10). In three registered tests on models trained by others, the pooled test rejected in 0 of 96 units of a diffusion model, and reached the power bar in 10 of 26 Atari games under IRIS's token likelihood and 1 of 6 control tasks under TD-MPC2; mixtures fixed in advance reached it in 96 units, 24 games and 6 tasks. On recorded robot video with iVideoGPT the mixture rejected in 155 of 159 trajectories; the pooled test did not collapse there (143). Under exact nulls the statistics made 14 rejections in 26,664 tests.

The Posterior-Prior Epistemic Gap: When World Model Imagination Fails to Carry Task-Relevant Signals 2026-11-11
Show abstract

Model-based planners that roll out imagined futures assume the transition model faithfully propagates task-relevant signals---a property rarely measured directly. In a single-seed case study of a Recurrent State Space Model world model with active-inference planning in the CARLA driving simulator, we identify a \textbf{posterior-prior gap}---posterior (observation-filtered) states retain obstacle information that imagined rollout states progressively lose: a supervised obstacle probe achieves AUC 0.85 on posterior states, but the same probe applied to imagined prior states shows monotonic decay (AUC 0.85$\rightarrow$0.71 over 15 steps; logit separation drops 57\%). Because the probe is identical, the decay isolates the transition model---not the representation---as the information bottleneck. Obstacle avoidance drops to 0\% without privileged runtime coordinates, and the planner's own ensemble-based epistemic term does not flag the loss---ensemble disagreement yields AUC 0.52 as an obstacle classifier (chance level), making the failure an unflagged imagination failure---an unknown unknown at planning time. As a secondary contribution, we show that \textbf{task-conditioned weighting of Effective Free Energy components} over the same world model yields lane keeping ($81.2 \pm 1.8$\% route completion) and obstacle avoidance (28/28 avoided, \emph{using privileged coordinates}) in the CARLA simulator, with runtime weight modulation enabling post-evasion centering.

FOE-Dreamer: Deployment-Efficient Learning of Cyber Defense Policies in Operational Networks 2026-11-11
📝 14 pages, 5 figures. To appear in the proceedings of the Annual Computer Security Applications Conference (ACSAC)
Show abstract

Autonomous cyber defense is widely studied with deep reinforcement learning, but most work trains policies inside simulators because learning requires many trial-and-error interactions. Simulator-based training carries a fidelity gap: trained policies face authentication delays, partial action failures, service dependencies, and user traffic patterns that the simulator did not represent, and degrade on transfer. Training directly in the network being defended removes the gap but makes every interaction slow and costly, so sample efficiency becomes the binding constraint. Model-based reinforcement learning has reduced sample requirements on games and physical robots by orders of magnitude by planning inside a learned world model, but it has not been applied to defenders trained in operational networks. We present FOE-Dreamer (Factored Opponent-Enhanced Dreamer), a model-based reinforcement learning framework that trains a defender online and directly in a network on cloud infrastructure, without a simulator stage. FOE-Dreamer separates three sources of variation in the latent state: a controllable component driven by defender actions, an exogenous component driven by benign user activity, and an opponent embedding inferred from defender-observable signals alone. Separating the components reduces the variance in policy gradients during planning in imagination. Against two scripted attacker profiles with user activity emulated by the GHOSTS framework on an 8-host OpenStack testbed, FOE-Dreamer trains within a 3-day budget on a single GPU and achieves about 2$\times$ better episode loss than Rainbow and IQN baselines under matched compute. Both the factoring and the opponent model contribute to the gain, and the learned latents are consistent with the intended separation.

BirdDreams: Joint Perspective and BEV Video Generation for a Plausible, Reactive Driving World Model 2026-11-10
📝 Project page: https://research.nvidia.com/labs/sil/projects/birddreams/
Show abstract

Driving world models aim to simulate how a scene unfolds under different ego actions. This requires plausible motion of surrounding vehicles as well as precise ego-trajectory control. Learning vehicle motion from perspective video alone is challenging because motion is entangled with appearance and vehicles can be occluded. Prior work therefore conditions video generation on layouts that specify vehicle motion, providing control over surrounding vehicles but requiring their future trajectories as inputs. We introduce BirdDreams, an autoregressive driving world model that jointly generates perspective video and a schematic bird's-eye-view (BEV) video, making scene layout part of the predicted future. The BEV, rendered from scene annotations for training, depicts roads and vehicles as simple shapes at a fixed metric scale, providing a top-down view of their positions and motion with reduced occlusion between vehicles. Perspective video, by contrast, retains scene details that the BEV omits, such as traffic signals and object appearance. Joint attention between two video diffusion transformers, one per stream, lets each stream inform the other during generation. The predicted BEV also provides a geometric interface for ego control: we translate and rotate its latest frame along an input ego trajectory and use the resulting frames as a soft condition that shows how the scene would appear to move if only the ego vehicle moved. At inference, BirdDreams generates both videos from a single camera image without requiring an HD map or vehicle-state inputs. On PhysicalAI-AV and nuScenes, BirdDreams follows input ego trajectories more faithfully than prior driving world models and generates more plausible ego motion without ego-action conditioning. Human evaluation shows that BirdDreams outperforms a perspective-only baseline in generating realistic surrounding-vehicle behavior.

OnlyAct: Extracting Action Representations from Foundation Models for World Models 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Video world models promise general-purpose interactive simulators, but their training is limited by action supervision: action labels are scarce, fragmented across incompatible specifications, and do not scale with the unlabeled video pretraining corpus. A common workaround jointly trains an action encoder to compress consecutive frames into a latent action and a decoder to reconstruct the next frame from the previous frame and that latent, forcing the latent to capture only new information. This has two limitations: learning the action space from scratch can limit generalization to unseen domains, and the bottleneck trades expressiveness for separation, with tighter settings losing nuance and looser ones risking scene leakage under domain shifts. We argue for a different starting point: rather than learning actions from scratch, we start from a pretrained foundation model (V-JEPA 2), whose embeddings of a frame pair already carry substantial action information after large-scale, diverse video pretraining. The remaining challenge is to extract the action while discarding the scene. We propose a label-free contrastive adaptation framework that pulls together frame pairs sharing the same motion under a different scene and pushes apart pairs from the same scene with different motion, reshaping the geometry of the pretrained space without imposing a reconstruction bottleneck. We introduce a unified evaluation protocol along three axes (action expressiveness, scene invariance, and action-conditioned video generation quality) across robot manipulation, camera navigation, game, and driving benchmarks, most of which are unseen during adaptation. Compared with annotation-free latent-action methods, our representations are substantially more expressive while matching their scene invariance, and they yield world models that transfer demonstrated actions to new scenes more faithfully.

An Energy-Based Perspective on Machine Learning 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Energy-based models (EBMs) define a distribution through a scalar energy, $p_θ(x)\propto\exp(-E_θ(x))$, and are usually presented as one model family among many. This tutorial has a broader goal: beyond introducing EBMs, it uses them as a single lens that connects the different aspects of machine learning. Any EBM is specified by three choices, the energy, which determines what the model represents; the inference procedure, which samples from or optimizes the energy; and the learning method, which fits the energy to data. We first develop these components, including sampling, variational inference, and learning by maximum likelihood, noise contrastive estimation, pseudolikelihood, score matching, and adversarial training, and show that the learning methods share one contrastive structure. We then show that discriminative models, generative models such as GANs, normalizing flows, diffusion and autoregressive models, representation learning methods such as contrastive learning, word2vec, BERT, denoising auto-encoders, and diffusion representations, reinforcement learning with EBM policies and world models, inverse reinforcement learning, and planning, as well as inference-time computation with verifiers, are all EBMs that differ in these three choices, mostly in how they handle the partition function and sampling. This view explains existing methods, organizes them into a common framework, and suggests new algorithms by replacing one component while keeping the others, with composition of energies as a principled way to combine learned models at inference time.

From Video Frames to Robot Meta-Frames: A Unified Latent Interface for World Action Models 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Pretrained video diffusion models (VDMs) offer a powerful foundation for World Action Models (WAMs), which jointly predict robot actions and future observations. Building such models requires injecting low-dimensional robot signals (e.g. proprioception, actions, and values) into a latent space shaped by natural videos, and a key design choice is the interface through which these signals enter: existing schemes treat it as a formatting detail, spending a separate latent frame on each signal or bolting on dedicated encoders and decoders. We argue that signal injection is instead a distribution-alignment problem: how well a representation matches the prior of the pretrained VDMs during denoising. Using the relative reconstruction error of a frozen VDM denoiser as a probe, we find that conventional spatial encodings sit far off this prior, whereas frequency-domain packing yields latents that the denoiser corrects far less. A fixed band layout together with slot-aware routing lets the same meta-frame support policy, world-model, and value prediction over a shared set of robot variables. On RoboCasa, MetaWAM reaches 68.9% success while collapsing the per-signal frames into one and cutting inference latency by 39%, and attains 85.0% success on three real-world Franka tasks. Few-Shot Residual Adaptation of Pretrained Latent World Models 2026-11-10
Show abstract

Pretrained latent world models can support visual planning and control, but their predictions may fail when physical dynamics change at deployment. We study whether such models can adapt from only a few interactions without modifying their pretrained weights or identifying the changed physical parameters. We introduce a few-shot residual adaptation approach that learns self-supervised corrections from latent prediction errors.
